The previous lesson got media into durable storage and out to viewers efficiently. This lesson answers a different question: once a post exists somewhere, how does it end up in the right people's feeds, in the right order, and how does the Stories tray at the top of the screen work at all given that its content is supposed to vanish after a day?

These two features look similar — both are "things from people you follow, shown near the top of the app" — but they solve almost opposite engineering problems. The feed is a ranking problem over a permanent, ever-growing corpus. Stories are a freshness-and-expiry problem over a tiny, ephemeral one. An interviewer who asks "design Instagram" and lets you get away with treating them the same hasn't tested you yet — a strong answer explains why they diverge.

## Feed generation: the same fan-out trade-off, applied to posts

Building a home feed is fundamentally the same problem covered in this course's Twitter timeline lesson: the feed is a merge of posts from everyone you follow, and there are exactly two moments you can do that merge — when a post is created, or when the feed is read.

**Fan-out-on-write (push).** When a user posts, look up their followers and insert a reference to the new post into each follower's precomputed feed list (typically a capped list in Redis or a wide-column store, keyed by user ID). Reads are then just "fetch my list." This is cheap for the 99%+ of accounts with a normal follower count, and it falls over for accounts with millions of followers — one post from a huge creator account would otherwise mean millions of synchronous writes from a single API call.

**Fan-out-on-read (pull).** Do nothing at post time; when a user opens the app, query every followed account for recent posts and merge live. This makes posting always cheap, but shifts cost to reads — a user following 1,000 accounts triggers up to 1,000 lookups on every feed refresh, and reads vastly outnumber posts.

The standard fix is the same hybrid: push for accounts under a follower threshold, and skip fan-out for accounts above it, letting their posts be pulled in and merged at read time by the relatively small number of people who follow big accounts. If you haven't seen that trade-off worked through in detail with a worked queue-sizing example, it's covered thoroughly in the sibling Twitter module — no need to re-derive it here; just recognize it's the identical shape of problem with "tweet" replaced by "post."

## Ranking: candidate generation, then scoring

A pure reverse-chronological feed was Instagram's original design and it's a legitimate, simpler answer for an interview if you say so explicitly and explain the trade-off: chronological is trivial to compute (it's just the merged fan-out list, sorted by timestamp) but wastes attention on stale or low-quality posts when a user hasn't opened the app in a while. A ranked feed reorders that same candidate set by predicted relevance instead of recency.

Ranking a feed for hundreds of millions of users on every open is too expensive to do as one heavyweight step, so production systems split it into two stages:

- **Candidate generation**: a cheap, high-recall pass that narrows "everything from everyone you follow, plus some recommended accounts" down to a few hundred candidates — recent posts, filtered by basic rules (not already seen, not from a blocked account), pulled from the precomputed fan-out list plus a small pull-tier merge for big accounts.
- **Ranking**: a heavier model scores just those few hundred candidates using signals like predicted likelihood of like/comment/share, how long since you last saw a post from that author, and content type (video tends to get weighted differently than a photo). Only the ranking stage needs to be that expensive, because it runs over hundreds of items, not the full corpus.

This two-stage split is a general pattern worth naming explicitly in an interview — it's the same shape used by search engines (retrieve then re-rank) and recommendation systems generally, not something specific to feeds.

## Stories: the opposite architecture

Stories flip almost every assumption the feed relies on:

- **No ranking model needed for content order within a story.** A single user's story is just their posts in chronological order for the 24 hours they're live.
- **The tray ordering (whose stories appear first) is a much simpler heuristic** than feed ranking — typically "closest friends and most-interacted-with accounts first, unseen before seen," not a learned model scoring individual pieces of content.
- **Expiry is a first-class requirement, not an afterthought.** Every story needs to disappear exactly 24 hours after posting, for every viewer, with no manual cleanup job.
- **Per-viewer state matters far more than aggregate counts.** The feed cares about global like/comment counts; Stories care about *who specifically* has viewed *this* story, because the author-facing "seen by" list is a core feature.

### Handling expiry without a sweep job

The naive approach — a cron job that scans all stories and deletes ones older than 24 hours — doesn't scale and adds latency-shaped bugs (a story that's 23:59:59 old is still technically live and must still render). The standard fix is to let the storage layer expire entries natively: a TTL attribute in DynamoDB, or a Redis key with `EXPIRE` set to 24 hours from creation. The story simply stops existing (or stops being returned) at the right instant per-item, with no batch job, no lag, and no wasted reads scanning already-expired rows.

Underlying media (the actual video/image file) doesn't need to be hard-deleted at that same instant — it's fine to lazily garbage-collect the S3/blob object on a slower schedule, since the story is already invisible once the metadata/TTL record is gone.

### Read-state tracking

Each story needs a small set of "which user IDs have viewed this" — conceptually a row per `(story_id, viewer_id, timestamp)`, kept only as long as the story itself is live. This is a lightweight, high-write, short-lived table, not a ranking problem: writes happen every time someone views a story, and the only read pattern the author-facing UI needs is "give me the list of viewers for my story," which is a straightforward key-based lookup, not a computation.

## Worked example: sizing story writes

Say the platform has 500 million daily active users, and 30% of them post at least one story per day, averaging 4 story segments each. That's `500M * 0.3 * 4 = 600` million story-segment creates per day, or roughly 7,000/second average (higher at peak, so plan for 3x — about 21,000/second).

Now consider views: an average story gets viewed by 15% of the author's followers, and the average account has 300 followers. Per story-segment, that's `300 * 0.15 = 45` view-events. Across 600 million segments/day, that's 27 billion view-events/day — about 312,000/second average, and roughly 1 million/second at peak. That view-write volume is an order of magnitude larger than the story-creation volume itself, which is the number that should drive your storage choice: you want a store optimized for a huge volume of small, short-lived writes (a wide-column store or Redis) rather than one tuned for read-heavy relational queries.

<Callout type="warning">
A common mistake in this lesson's topic is treating Stories as "just a smaller version of the feed" and trying to reuse the feed's ranking pipeline for it, or conversely applying the feed's TTL-free permanent storage model to story content. Stories aren't a subset of the feed problem — they're a different problem (freshness/expiry-driven, per-viewer-state-heavy) that happens to render in a similar-looking UI surface. Design its data model and expiry mechanism independently rather than bolting story features onto the existing feed/post infrastructure; trying to force one schema to serve both usually means the story path inherits complexity (like counts, comment threads, permanent storage) it never needed, and the feed path inherits constraints (TTL semantics) it never wanted.
</Callout>
