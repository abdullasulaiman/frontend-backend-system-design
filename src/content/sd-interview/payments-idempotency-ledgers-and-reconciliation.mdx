Picture this: your service calls the payment processor to charge a
customer's card, the request is sent, and then... the connection drops
before you get a response. Did the charge go through? You genuinely don't
know. If your instinct is "just retry," you've now got a real chance of
charging the customer twice. If your instinct is "don't retry, ask the
user to try again," you've got a real chance of never charging them at
all and shipping the order anyway. Neither answer is acceptable at scale,
and this exact ambiguous state — "sent, no confirmed response" — is the
single hardest problem in payment systems. Everything in this lesson
exists to resolve it safely.

The previous lesson laid out the requirements and the request flow. Here
we cover the three mechanisms that make that flow trustworthy under
retries, crashes, and network partitions: idempotency keys, a ledger that
records money movement as immutable facts, and reconciliation that catches
whatever the first two miss.

## Idempotency: making retries safe

An idempotent operation produces the same result no matter how many times
you run it. `SET balance = 100` is idempotent; `balance += 10` is not.
Payment APIs are full of the second kind of operation by nature — "charge
this card," "transfer these funds" — so you have to bolt idempotency on
deliberately.

The standard mechanism is an **idempotency key**: the client generates a
unique token (a UUID is fine) for each logical payment attempt and sends
it with the request, typically as a header. The server's contract is:

- On first sight of a key, execute the operation and store the key
  together with the resulting response (success or failure) and the
  request's parameters.
- On any later request with the same key, skip execution and return the
  stored response verbatim.
- If a request arrives with a key that's mid-flight (another request with
  that key is still processing), reject or block rather than double-run —
  otherwise two near-simultaneous retries race past each other.

Two details trip people up in interviews. First, scope the key to the
caller: store it as `(merchant_id, idempotency_key)`, not just
`idempotency_key`, or two different merchants can accidentally collide.
Second, also store a hash of the request body alongside the key — if the
same key arrives with genuinely different parameters (a bug, or someone
reusing a key by mistake), that's an error condition, not a retry, and you
should reject it loudly rather than silently returning a stale response
for a different payment.

Idempotency keys solve retries from the *same* logical attempt. They do
not solve a user clicking "Pay" twice in two tabs and generating two
different keys for what is, to the user, one purchase — that needs a
separate check, like a short-lived lock on "customer X, checkout Y."

## The ledger: money movement as immutable facts

A payments system should never store a mutable "balance" column that gets
updated in place. Instead it keeps an **append-only, double-entry
ledger** — every event that moves money writes a new pair of rows, one
debit and one balancing credit, and existing rows are never changed. A
balance is a *query* (sum the entries for an account), not a stored field.

Why bother with this instead of just updating a balance column?

- **Auditability.** "What was the balance on March 3rd?" is trivial to
  answer — sum entries up to that date. With a mutable balance you'd need
  a separate audit log anyway, so you may as well make the audit log the
  source of truth.
- **Concurrency safety.** Two concurrent `balance += x` updates on the
  same row are a classic race condition without careful locking. Two
  concurrent *inserts* of new ledger rows don't conflict with each other
  at all.
- **Recoverability.** If a worker crashes mid-transaction, you can tell
  exactly what was and wasn't recorded by inspecting the ledger, and
  replay safely (especially combined with idempotency keys on the ledger
  write itself).

A minimal ledger entry needs: an account ID, an amount, a direction
(debit/credit), a reference to the payment/transaction it belongs to, a
timestamp, and the idempotency key that produced it. Double-entry means
every transaction writes at least two rows whose amounts sum to zero
across the accounts involved — e.g. a `$50` charge debits the customer's
"payable" account and credits the merchant's "receivable" account by `$50`
each, so the books always balance by construction.

Alongside the ledger, each payment has an explicit **state machine**:
`created -> authorized -> captured -> settled`, with `failed` and
`reversed` as branches. Persist the current state on the payment record
itself (not just as an implication of which ledger rows exist) so a
recovering worker can ask "what state was this payment in?" with a single
row read instead of reconstructing it from ledger history.

## Reconciliation: trusting, but verifying

Idempotency and the ledger handle correctness *inside* your system. But
your system and the payment processor are two separate systems connected
by an unreliable network, and they will occasionally disagree — a
timeout on your side might be a success on the processor's side, or vice
versa. **Reconciliation** is the scheduled process that catches this drift
before it becomes a customer complaint or an accounting problem.

The mechanics: the processor (Stripe, Adyen, a card network) publishes a
settlement report — typically a batch file or API feed — listing every
transaction it actually settled, usually once a day. A reconciliation job
pulls that report and diffs it against your own ledger:

- **In your ledger but not in the processor's report**: you think you
  charged someone but the processor doesn't agree. Likely a timed-out
  request that never actually completed on their end — mark the payment
  failed and, if goods/services were already granted, flag for
  collection or write-off.
- **In the processor's report but not in your ledger**: the processor
  charged the customer but your system never recorded it — likely a
  timeout where the request actually *did* succeed upstream. Record it
  after the fact and make sure downstream systems (order fulfillment)
  get the delayed confirmation.
- **Amount mismatches**: same transaction ID, different amount — almost
  always a bug, and worth paging someone rather than auto-resolving.

Run reconciliation on a schedule (hourly for high-value flows, daily is
common for typical e-commerce), and alert on any unmatched transaction
older than a defined threshold (say, 2 hours) rather than waiting for the
daily batch to quietly note it. Reconciliation is also your safety net
for bugs in the idempotency logic itself — if a race condition ever did
let a double-charge slip through, reconciliation against the processor's
authoritative record is what catches it.

## Worked example: the timeout case end to end

A customer buys a `$120` item. Your checkout service generates
idempotency key `pay_8f2c...` and calls the processor's charge API. The
TCP connection stalls for 15 seconds and your HTTP client times out —
you never see a response.

Without idempotency: your retry logic (or the user hitting refresh)
resends the charge with a *new* key, and if the original request actually
succeeded upstream, the customer is now charged `$240`.

With the mechanisms in this lesson: your retry resends the request with
the *same* key, `pay_8f2c...`. Two outcomes:

1. The processor never received the original request — it executes the
   charge for real now, returns success, and your service writes one
   ledger entry pair and moves the payment to `authorized`. Correct
   outcome, one charge.
2. The processor *did* receive and process the original request — it
   recognizes the retry (many processors support idempotency keys on
   their own API too) and returns the original success response without
   charging again. Your service, seeing a success response for a payment
   it had marked `created`, writes the ledger entry and moves to
   `authorized`. Correct outcome, one charge.

If both your retries are exhausted and you genuinely can't get a
confirmed answer, you leave the payment in `created` state and let the
next reconciliation cycle resolve it against the processor's settlement
report — a `$120` entry either appears there (you missed a success) or it
doesn't (the charge truly never happened), and you update your ledger to
match reality.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is implementing idempotency only on your own API's
  front door and assuming that's sufficient. If your service then calls
  the payment processor's API *without* forwarding an idempotency key on
  that outbound call, a retry inside your own service (e.g. after your
  worker crashes and resumes) can still double-charge upstream — your
  front-door idempotency only dedupes requests reaching *you*, it does
  nothing for calls *you* make onward. Idempotency has to be threaded
  through every hop that can retry, including outbound calls to the
  processor.
</Callout>
