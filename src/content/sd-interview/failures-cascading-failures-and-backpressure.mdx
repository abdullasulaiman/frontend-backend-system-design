The previous lesson in this module catalogued the failure modes you'll
meet in production: slow disks, GC pauses, network partitions, bad
deploys. Most of those start as a **local** problem in one service. What
turns a local problem into a full outage is almost never the original
fault — it's what every other service does in response to it. A payment
service that gets 30% slower doesn't cause a 30% dip in checkout success;
it can cause a 100% outage twenty minutes later, in services that never
had a bug of their own. That gap between "one thing got slow" and
"everything is down" is what this lesson is about.

This matters in interviews because "how does your design fail, and what
stops that failure from spreading" is one of the highest-signal follow-up
questions an interviewer can ask. Naming the mechanism (retry storms,
unbounded queues, no timeouts) and the specific countermeasure
(backpressure, circuit breakers, bulkheads) is what separates "I know this
system works" from "I know this system fails safely."

## How a cascade actually forms

A cascade needs three ingredients, and removing any one of them stops it:

1. **A trigger.** Some dependency gets slow or starts erroring — a
   database running a bad query plan, a downstream service overloaded by
   its own traffic spike, a network link dropping packets.
2. **A pileup mechanism.** Callers of the slow dependency don't fail fast;
   they wait. Threads block on a slow call, connections stay checked out
   of a pool, requests queue up in memory. Each waiting caller is now
   holding a resource (a thread, a socket, a chunk of heap) it would
   otherwise have released quickly.
3. **A propagation path.** The caller that's now resource-starved is
   itself a dependency for something else. Its own callers start timing
   out or queueing on *it*, and the same pileup mechanism repeats one
   level up. Repeat this a few hops and services with no relationship to
   the original trigger start failing.

The insidious part is that step 2 is often made *worse* by good intentions.
A service that retries failed calls "to be resilient" turns a slow
dependency into 2-3x more load on that dependency. A thread pool sized
generously "so we never reject work" just means more threads get stuck
waiting instead of failing fast. Naive resilience mechanisms, applied
without limits, are frequently the thing that turns a brief blip into a
sustained outage.

## Backpressure: the core defense

Backpressure is the general principle that when a consumer can't keep up,
it should signal that fact backward to whoever is producing work for it,
rather than absorbing unlimited work and hoping it catches up later. It
shows up at every layer:

- **At the network layer**: TCP's own flow control is backpressure — a
  receiver with a full buffer tells the sender to slow down.
- **At the queue layer**: a bounded queue that rejects or blocks producers
  once full is backpressure; an unbounded queue is the absence of it.
- **At the API layer**: a `429 Too Many Requests` response, ideally with
  a `Retry-After` header, is backpressure expressed as an HTTP contract.
- **At the load-balancing layer**: shedding the least important 10% of
  requests when the fleet is at capacity is backpressure applied with a
  priority policy.

The common thread: backpressure converts an overload condition into an
explicit, fast, cheap signal ("no, not right now") instead of an implicit,
slow, expensive one (a queue that grows until the process runs out of
memory, or a client that hangs until its own timeout fires). Fast failure
is recoverable. Slow, silent failure is what turns into a cascade,
because every hop upstream is still waiting, still holding resources,
still hoping the response is "just about to arrive."

### Retries: helpful in isolation, dangerous in aggregate

A single client retrying a single failed call is fine. The problem is
what happens when thousands of clients retry simultaneously against a
struggling service — the retries themselves become the load spike that
prevents recovery. Three mitigations matter:

- **Cap the retry count** (2-3 attempts, not unbounded).
- **Use exponential backoff with jitter** — each retry waits longer than
  the last, and the jitter (a random offset) prevents every client from
  retrying at exactly the same moment.
- **Retry budgets** — track the ratio of retries to original requests
  service-wide, and stop retrying entirely once that ratio crosses a
  threshold (e.g. retries are more than 10% of traffic), because at that
  point retries are clearly not helping.

### Circuit breakers: fail fast on purpose

A circuit breaker wraps a call to a dependency and tracks its recent
error/timeout rate. Once that rate crosses a threshold, the breaker
"opens": it stops calling the dependency at all for a cooldown window and
returns an immediate error (or a fallback) to the caller instead. After
the cooldown, it lets a small number of trial requests through
("half-open") to check whether the dependency has recovered, and only
then closes fully.

The value isn't avoiding failure — the call was going to fail either way.
The value is *how fast* it fails. A timeout-based failure might take 5-10
seconds per call to discover; a tripped circuit breaker fails in
microseconds. That difference is what keeps the caller's own thread pool
and connection pool from being consumed by calls that were never going to
succeed.

## Timeouts and bulkheads: containing the blast radius

Two more structural tools limit how far a slow dependency's damage
spreads, even before a circuit breaker trips:

- **Deliberate timeouts.** Every network call needs an explicit timeout
  set relative to the caller's own latency budget, not a framework
  default. If the API gateway must respond in 300ms, a downstream call
  with a 30-second default timeout can single-handedly blow that budget
  by two orders of magnitude while "technically not failing yet."
- **Bulkheads.** Named after ship compartments that keep one hull breach
  from sinking the whole vessel: give each dependency its own resource
  pool (its own thread pool, its own connection pool) instead of one
  shared pool for all downstream calls. If the pricing service's calls
  are all stuck waiting, they exhaust the pricing-specific pool — the
  pool serving calls to inventory or shipping is untouched, and those
  features keep working.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake teams make is treating retries, generous
  timeouts, and large thread pools as resilience on their own. Each one,
  applied without a cap, actually removes the fast-failure signal that
  would have contained the problem — a retry with no limit multiplies
  load on a struggling dependency, a 30-second timeout means a caller
  discovers trouble ten times slower than a 300ms budget requires, and an
  "unbounded so we never reject" thread pool just relocates the resource
  exhaustion one hop upstream instead of preventing it. Resilience
  patterns only work when they're bounded and fail fast.
</Callout>

## Worked example: a checkout service under a database hiccup

Say a checkout API depends on a pricing service, which depends on a
database. Normal latency: DB queries at 20ms, pricing responds in 40ms,
checkout responds in 80ms. The checkout service runs 200 worker threads
and normally uses about 30 of them at a time.

Now the database's query planner picks a bad plan after a schema
migration, and query latency jumps to 4 seconds. Walk the chain:

- **Without any protection**: pricing's calls to the DB now take 4s
  instead of 20ms. Pricing has no timeout configured, so its own worker
  threads block for the full 4s per call. Within a few seconds, all of
  pricing's threads are tied up waiting on the DB, and it stops accepting
  new connections. Checkout's calls to pricing start timing out at
  checkout's own default 30s timeout — but checkout's 200 threads are now
  each blocked for up to 30 seconds per request, and incoming checkout
  traffic (unrelated customers just trying to buy things) exhausts the
  pool within roughly `200 threads / (normal request rate)` seconds.
  Checkout goes down. So does everything that calls checkout.
- **With the countermeasures in this lesson**: pricing sets a 200ms
  timeout on its DB calls (tight, because pricing's own budget from
  checkout is 300ms) and fails those calls fast once the DB is slow.
  Checkout's circuit breaker on pricing trips within a few seconds of a
  bad error rate and starts returning a fallback (e.g. last-known cached
  price, or "pricing temporarily unavailable, try again") instead of
  waiting. Checkout's thread pool for pricing calls is a separate
  bulkhead from its thread pool for, say, the inventory check, so
  inventory keeps working normally. The database problem is real and
  still needs fixing, but it stays a pricing-degradation incident instead
  of a full checkout outage.

The difference between those two outcomes isn't the trigger — it's
identical in both. It's entirely in whether the system was built to fail
fast and contain damage, or to quietly absorb it until something breaks.
