Netflix streams to hundreds of millions of subscribers across nearly every
country with an internet connection, and at peak hours in many regions it
accounts for a meaningful double-digit share of all downstream internet
traffic. That number alone tells you this isn't a "scale the database"
problem. Serving video at that volume, reliably, while also personalizing
what every single subscriber sees the moment they open the app, forces a
specific architectural choice that shows up constantly in system design
interviews: **separate the part of the system that decides what to show
you from the part that actually delivers the bytes**, and scale each one
completely differently.

This case study walks through how that split works in practice, why
Netflix built its own CDN instead of only using a third-party one, and how
deliberately breaking your own production system became a core scaling
practice rather than a liability.

## The two-plane architecture

Think of a Netflix session as two almost entirely separate systems working
together:

- **The control plane** — everything that isn't video bytes: login,
  billing, search, the home page grid, recommendations, "continue
  watching," and the initial handshake that tells your device which server
  to actually stream from. This runs as hundreds of independent
  microservices, historically all on AWS.
- **The data plane** — the video segments themselves, delivered through
  **Open Connect**, a CDN Netflix built and operates itself rather than
  renting from a generic third party.

This split matters because the two halves have wildly different scaling
characteristics. The control plane handles metadata-sized requests
(kilobytes) but needs to be fast and highly personalized per user. The data
plane handles enormous payloads (gigabytes per stream) but the *same*
payload serves millions of different viewers — video isn't personalized
content, so it's the textbook case for aggressive caching.

## Open Connect: pre-positioning beats reacting

A generic CDN caches reactively: a viewer requests a segment, the nearest
edge node has a miss, it fetches from origin, and it's cached for the next
viewer nearby. That works, but Netflix pushed further: because a catalog
title is finite, predictable, and identical for everyone who watches it,
Open Connect **pre-positions** content before anyone asks for it, rather
than waiting for cache misses.

Open Connect appliances — Netflix-built servers loaded with storage and
network capacity — are physically placed in two kinds of locations:
directly inside ISP networks (so traffic never has to leave the ISP at
all) and at internet exchange points where many networks interconnect.
Each night, during a region's low-traffic hours, appliances "fill":
downloading new and popular titles from Netflix's own backbone. During
peak viewing hours, that fill traffic stops entirely and the appliances
serve purely from local storage — so peak-hour demand never competes with
the ingestion traffic that's populating the caches.

The predictability of viewing patterns is what makes this work: Netflix
knows in advance roughly which titles will be popular in which region (new
releases, regional trends, personalization data), so it can decide *what*
to pre-position and *where*, instead of discovering demand after the fact
the way a purely reactive CDN does.

### Why not just use a third-party CDN entirely

A generic CDN is a reasonable choice for many companies, and Netflix does
use multiple methods depending on region and ISP relationship. But at
Netflix's traffic volume, the economics and control matter enough to
justify owning the data plane:

- **Egress cost** at this volume is enormous; owning the appliances and
  placing them inside ISPs (often at the ISP's request, since it also
  saves the ISP's own transit costs) changes the cost structure entirely.
- **Traffic predictability** lets Netflix pre-position instead of relying
  purely on reactive cache-fill, which a generic CDN's shared,
  multi-tenant infrastructure can't tailor to one catalog's release
  calendar.
- **Direct ISP placement** removes an entire network hop and a shared,
  contended path for a huge fraction of requests.

The general lesson for an interview: "we'll put a CDN in front of it" is
the right instinct, but at large enough scale with predictable enough
content, pre-positioning content ahead of demand beats caching reactively
after a miss — and it's worth stating that trade-off explicitly rather than
treating "CDN" as a single undifferentiated box.

## The control plane: stateless services and shared caches

The control plane is built as many independently deployable
microservices — a request gateway routes and authenticates incoming
requests, then fans out to whichever backend services a given page needs
(one call to render the home page might touch a dozen services: profile,
recommendations, continue-watching, artwork selection, and more). Each
service is kept stateless so any instance can handle any request, which is
what makes horizontal scaling and fast redeploys possible. Shared state
lives in dedicated stores: a wide-column store (Cassandra) for durable,
highly available data that must survive node and even region failures, and
an in-memory caching layer in front of it to keep read latency low for the
data every request needs (like your profile or watch history).

Because a single page load fans out to many internal services, one slow
or unhealthy dependency can't be allowed to cascade and take down the
whole page. This is handled with the same techniques covered in this
module's failure-modes lesson: per-dependency timeouts, circuit breakers
that stop calling a failing service instead of piling up retries, and
bulkheads that isolate one service's failure from consuming resources
needed by others.

## Resilience as an engineering practice, not an afterthought

The idea Netflix is most associated with in interview prep circles is
**chaos engineering**: deliberately injecting failure into production to
verify the system actually survives it, instead of hoping the failure
paths work when a real outage hits. The practice started small — randomly
terminating individual service instances during business hours, on
purpose, to force every service to prove it tolerates losing an instance
without customer impact — and expanded to simulating the loss of an
*entire AWS region*, since a real region-level cloud outage is exactly the
kind of rare, high-blast-radius event you can't afford to discover you're
unprepared for.

This only works because of another structural decision: running **active
in at least three AWS regions simultaneously**, each capable of absorbing
the others' traffic. Losing one region becomes a traffic-redirection and
capacity problem the surviving regions must have headroom for, not a
subscriber-facing outage — but only if that failover path has actually
been exercised, which is exactly what the regional chaos drills verify.

## Worked example: what actually breaks in a regional AWS outage

Say Netflix's control-plane services in one AWS region go dark for twenty
minutes. Walk through the blast radius:

- **Subscribers mid-stream, already connected to an Open Connect
  appliance, keep watching.** The data plane doesn't depend on that
  region's control-plane services being healthy — it already told the
  device which appliance to use.
- **New "press play" requests and browsing** from users routed to the
  failed region need to fail over: DNS/traffic routing shifts them to a
  healthy region, and that region needs pre-provisioned spare capacity to
  absorb the extra load without falling over itself.
- **A service the interviewer might poke at: recommendations.** If the
  personalization service in the failing region is unreachable and
  failover hasn't happened yet, the right behavior is a graceful fallback
  (a generic or last-known-cached row of titles) rather than a broken home
  page — degrade a feature, don't fail the request.

The number worth internalizing: the failure of an entire region should
cost you spare capacity and a brief redirect, not an outage — if it costs
more than that, the chaos drills should have caught it before a real event
did.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common interview mistake is describing Netflix's resilience purely as
  "they have multiple servers" without naming the actual mechanism. The
  useful things to say out loud are specific: stateless services so any
  instance is disposable, circuit breakers so one slow dependency can't
  cascade, active-active multi-region so a whole region can be lost without
  an outage, and — critically — chaos testing that *proves* those failure
  paths work in production rather than assuming they do. An interviewer
  will push back on "it's resilient" the same way they'd push back on "it
  scales" with no supporting mechanism.
</Callout>

## What this case study is not about

Worth stating explicitly so you don't over-reach in an interview: this
lesson covers Netflix's delivery and resilience architecture, not the
recommendation algorithm itself (a machine-learning problem with its own
deep-dive) and not encoding/transcoding pipeline internals (conceptually
similar to the transcoding lesson elsewhere in this course, just at
Netflix's specific scale). If asked to "design Netflix" in an interview,
the two-plane split and the pre-positioned CDN are the structural ideas
worth leading with before going deeper into any one sub-system.
