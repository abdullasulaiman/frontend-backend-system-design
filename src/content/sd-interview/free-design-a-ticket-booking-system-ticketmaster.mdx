Most system design lessons are about scaling reads or making writes durable.
Ticket booking is different: it's a scaling problem wrapped around a
correctness problem. When tickets for a stadium tour go on sale, tens of
thousands of people can hit "buy" for the same 200 seats within the same
second. Get the architecture wrong and you either oversell seats (a real
customer shows up to a seat someone else is also holding — an outage of
trust, not just of servers) or you throttle so aggressively that the site
looks broken during the one moment it matters most. This lesson designs a
Ticketmaster-style system around that tension, then goes deep on the part
every interviewer actually cares about: how you guarantee a seat is sold
exactly once.

## Requirements

**Functional**

- Browse events, venues, and seat maps; see real-time seat availability.
- Select one or more seats and hold them temporarily during checkout.
- Complete payment and receive a confirmed booking; the hold either
  converts to a booking or expires back to available.
- Support cancellations/refunds that release seats back into inventory.

**Non-functional**

- **Correctness over availability for the booking step**: never sell the
  same seat twice, even under extreme concurrent load.
- **High read throughput** for browsing (millions of users looking at a
  handful of popular events simultaneously).
- **Fairness and load shedding** during on-sale spikes — a flash-sale style
  traffic pattern, not steady-state load.
- Reasonable latency: seat map loads in under a second; a seat hold
  confirms in low hundreds of milliseconds.

Scale anchor: a major tour on-sale can see 1-2 million concurrent users
hitting a site that's selling, say, 50,000 seats across 20 shows. That's a
2,000:1 ratio of demand to supply concentrated into the first few minutes —
the defining constraint for this design.

## High-level architecture

Split the system into a **browse path** (read-heavy, tolerant of slight
staleness) and a **booking path** (write-heavy on a tiny hot key set,
correctness-critical). Forcing both through the same components is the
most common design mistake here.

- **Search & catalog service** serves event/venue/seat-map browsing off a
  cache that's refreshed frequently (every few seconds) rather than read
  from the source of truth on every request. Slightly stale "this section
  looks available" is fine — the booking step re-checks truth.
- **Seat reservation service** owns the seat state machine and is the only
  writer to seat state. This is the correctness-critical component.
- **Waiting room / admission queue** sits in front of the booking path
  during on-sale events, admitting a controlled number of users per second
  so the reservation service never sees more concurrent writers than it
  can safely handle.
- **Payment service** is a separate, idempotent step that only runs after a
  seat is successfully held — you never charge a card before you know the
  seat is yours to sell.

## The core problem: exactly-once seat allocation

Model each seat with three states: `available`, `held`, `booked`. A hold
carries a TTL (5-10 minutes is typical) and an owner (the user's session or
booking-attempt ID).

The critical operation is: "flip this seat from `available` to `held` for
me, and fail if anyone else got there first." That must be atomic. Two
practical ways to build it:

1. **Conditional write / compare-and-swap** on a per-seat row: `UPDATE
   seats SET status = 'held', held_by = :user, expires_at = :ttl WHERE
   seat_id = :id AND status = 'available'`. If the affected-row count is
   zero, someone else already grabbed it — return "seat unavailable"
   immediately. This works well on a relational database with row-level
   locking, or on any store that supports conditional writes.
2. **Distributed lock per seat** (e.g. a Redis `SET seat:123 user_id NX PX
   300000`) for an even faster hot path, with the lock's owner later
   confirmed against a durable booking record before payment completes.
   This trades a small window of "lock says held, DB not yet updated" for
   much higher throughput on the hottest seats; you reconcile by writing
   the durable state as soon as the lock succeeds, in the same request.

Either way, the seat is a single logical resource with a single writer
path — there is no read-then-decide-then-write gap where two requests can
both believe they succeeded.

<Callout type="warning">
  The classic bug: reading seat status, checking it's "available" in
  application code, and then issuing a separate write to mark it "held."
  Under load, two requests both read "available" before either writes,
  and both proceed to book the same seat. Any check-then-act pattern that
  isn't a single atomic operation (a conditional write, a compare-and-swap,
  a database-enforced unique constraint) will oversell under enough
  concurrency, even if it looks correct in every manual test you ran with
  one browser tab.
</Callout>

### Hold expiry

Holds must expire automatically, or a user who abandons checkout locks a
seat forever. Two common approaches: a TTL directly on the lock/row
(Redis `PX`, or a database expiry column checked on every read), backed by
a periodic sweep job that reverts stale `held` rows to `available` for
stores without native TTLs. Always re-validate the hold's expiry at the
moment of payment confirmation too — don't trust that the client's
countdown timer matches server state.

## Handling the on-sale spike: queueing and fairness

Even with correct per-seat locking, you don't want a million requests all
hammering the reservation service in the same second — that's a thundering
herd regardless of how atomic your writes are. A **virtual waiting room**
in front of the booking path admits users at a controlled rate (say, 5,000
per minute) using a token or a randomly assigned queue position, so the
reservation service only ever sees a manageable number of concurrent
booking attempts. This is purely about load shedding and perceived
fairness — it does not replace per-seat locking, it protects the system
that does the locking.

Design the queue to be honest: show an estimated wait, don't let users
refresh to cut the line (bind position to a session token, not to
"first request wins on retry"), and make sure the queue itself is cheap to
serve (a lightweight token-dispensing service, not the same database doing
seat writes).

## Data model sketch

- **Events / Venues / Seats**: mostly static, cacheable, read-heavy —
  fine in a standard relational store or even a search index for browsing.
- **Seat state**: the hot, small, contention-heavy table — one row per
  seat per event, with `status`, `held_by`, `expires_at`, `version`. Keep
  this table narrow; every extra column is more lock contention.
- **Bookings**: append-only, one row per confirmed purchase, written once
  payment succeeds — this is your durable record and audit trail,
  decoupled from the mutable seat-state table.

Sharding seat state by `event_id` keeps the hot key concentrated to one
partition per event rather than spread thin — which is fine, because
contention is inherently per-event anyway; a popular Tuesday-night on-sale
for one arena doesn't need to coordinate with seat state for an unrelated
event across the country.

## Worked example: a 20,000-seat stadium on-sale

Say a stadium show has 20,000 seats and the on-sale opens at 10:00:00 AM,
with 800,000 users already waiting. The waiting room admits 4,000 users
per minute into the booking flow. Each admitted user's first action is a
seat-map read (served from cache, sub-100ms) followed by a hold attempt on
a specific seat via conditional write.

At peak, maybe 4,000 concurrent hold attempts land within the same
minute — but because each is a `WHERE status = 'available'` conditional
update on a specific seat row, only one succeeds per seat and the rest get
an immediate, correct "unavailable, pick another seat" response. No two
users ever both believe they hold seat `12A-14`. Within roughly 5 minutes,
all 20,000 seats are held or booked, and the remaining ~780,000 queued
users see "sold out" instead of piling further load onto a saturated
system — the queue did its job by capping concurrency, and the per-seat
lock did its job by making the actual allocation race-free.

## Trade-offs and what to say in an interview

- **Pessimistic locking (hold-then-book) vs. optimistic (try to book,
  retry on conflict)**: pessimistic holds give users a clear "you have 8
  minutes to check out" UX and simplify correctness; pure optimistic
  retries scale slightly better but produce a worse user experience under
  contention (repeated "someone beat you to it" failures). Most real
  ticketing systems use pessimistic holds for this reason.
- **Strong consistency for seat state vs. eventual consistency for
  browsing**: don't be afraid to say these need different guarantees out
  loud — it shows you're not defaulting to "one big consistent database
  for everything."
- **Waiting room adds latency and complexity for the 99% of days there's
  no spike.** A reasonable answer is to make it a feature flag that
  activates for high-demand on-sales, not a permanent gate on every
  purchase.
