"Design Quora" (or Stack Overflow, or Reddit) looks at first glance like a
simpler cousin of "Design YouTube" — it's text, not video, so how hard can
it be? The trap is treating it as a CRUD app for questions and answers.
The actual hard problem is that every user's home feed is a personalized,
constantly-reranked view over a graph of questions, topics, and people they
follow, and that ranking has to feel fresh and relevant at a scale where
computing it fresh, per user, per request, is not an option.

This lesson is about establishing the scope and the numbers before any of
that gets designed. Everything downstream — the data model, the feed
generation strategy, the scaling approach — is a direct response to the
read:write:vote ratio and the "who follows whom" fan-out shape we pin down
here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Agree on scope with the interviewer instead of assuming everything is in
play. A reasonable core:

- **Ask and answer**: a user posts a question (with topic tags); other
  users write answers to it.
- **Vote**: users upvote or downvote answers (and often questions), which
  feeds directly into ranking.
- **Follow**: users follow other users, topics, and individual questions,
  which determines what shows up in their feed.
- **Feed**: a personalized home feed surfaces new and re-ranked content
  from what a user follows.
- **Search**: find questions and topics by keyword.

Explicitly defer and say so out loud: real-time chat/DMs, ads and
monetization, detailed spam/abuse ML, and comment threads nested more than
one level deep are all real Quora features, but each is its own design
problem. Naming them and moving on signals seniority.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Read-heavy, and the reads are personalized.** Unlike a video's read
  path (same bytes served to everyone), a feed read here is a unique
  computation per user — that changes the caching strategy fundamentally.
- **Vote counts and rankings can be eventually consistent.** A vote count
  off by a handful for a few seconds is invisible to users; treating every
  vote as a strongly consistent write against a single row would tank
  throughput for no real benefit.
- **Authored content must be durable.** Losing a user's answer is a much
  worse failure than a stale vote count or a feed that's a few minutes
  behind — durability requirements are not uniform across the data model.
- **Feed freshness matters, but not real-time-chat levels of freshness.**
  A feed item appearing 30-60 seconds late is fine; a feed that never
  updates within a session is not.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Rough, stated assumptions beat no assumptions. A workable set of numbers
for a large (Quora-sized) Q&A platform:

- 300 million monthly active users, 30 million daily active users (DAU).
- Each DAU opens the feed about 4 times per session-day, loading roughly
  20 feed items per open.
- 1 in 3,000 DAU writes an answer on a given day; 1 in 15,000 DAU asks a
  new question.
- Each DAU casts an average of 8 votes per day (upvotes/downvotes on
  answers they read).
- Average answer: roughly 800 characters of text plus metadata, call it
  2 KB stored per answer.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say these numbers out loud before computing anything: "Let's assume 30
  million DAU and roughly 20 feed items loaded per feed open — does that
  match what you had in mind?" This turns the estimate into a shared
  checkpoint instead of a monologue.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive traffic and storage numbers

Work through content writes, votes, and feed reads separately — they
stress completely different parts of the system.

### Content write throughput

- Daily answers: 30,000,000 divided by 3,000 is 10,000 answers/day.
- Daily new questions: 30,000,000 divided by 15,000 is 2,000
  questions/day.
- Writes per second (average): (10,000 + 2,000) divided by 86,400 is
  about 0.14/sec — trivially small, even at a 5-10x peak multiplier.

### Vote throughput

- Daily votes: 30,000,000 DAU multiplied by 8 is 240,000,000 votes/day.
- Votes per second (average): 240,000,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  2,800/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 14,000/sec at peak.
- This is 4-5 orders of magnitude more writes than authored content — the
  vote stream, not the content stream, is the real write-scaling problem.

### Feed read throughput

- Daily feed opens: 30,000,000 DAU multiplied by 4 is 120,000,000 opens/day.
- Feed items served: 120,000,000 multiplied by 20 is 2.4 billion items/day.
- Feed opens per second (average): 120,000,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  1,400/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 7,000 feed-assembly
  requests/sec at peak — each one a personalized ranking computation, not
  a cache-identical byte stream.

### Storage

- Daily content storage: 10,000 answers multiplied by 2 KB is about 20 MB
  of new answer text per day — negligible even over years.
- Daily vote-log storage (if you keep a per-vote record for auditing/ML,
  not just an aggregate counter): 240,000,000 votes multiplied by roughly
  20 bytes per record is about 4.8 GB/day, or on the order of 1.7 TB/year.

The contrast is the key takeaway: **content storage is cheap; the vote
stream and the feed-assembly compute are the expensive parts.** A
relational or document store sized for questions and answers is trivial at
this scale — the real capacity planning problem is a counter-update
pipeline that absorbs ~14,000 writes/sec at peak, and a feed-ranking
service that assembles ~7,000 personalized feeds/sec.

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **Vote counts should be approximate, asynchronously-updated counters**,
   not synchronous strongly-consistent writes — at ~14,000 writes/sec
   peak, batching and eventual reconciliation is the only workable design.
2. **Feed assembly must be mostly precomputed, not built from scratch per
   request** — 7,000 personalized ranking computations/sec is too
   expensive to run live against raw data on every feed open; expect a
   fan-out or precomputation strategy, refined in the next lesson.
3. **Storage engineering is not the bottleneck** — plan capacity around
   compute (ranking, counter aggregation) and hot-key fan-out, not around
   text storage volume.
4. **A single, uniform "push every update to every follower" strategy
   will not survive a topic or user with millions of followers** — flag
   this "celebrity problem" now so the high-level design explicitly
   chooses a hybrid push/pull model instead of pure fan-out-on-write.

## Worked example: sanity-checking a hot topic

Suppose a topic like "Machine Learning" has 5 million followers, and a
single new answer gets posted to a question tagged with it. A pure
fan-out-on-write design would insert that one answer into 5 million
separate feed structures immediately — a 5-million-write burst from one
event. Re-running the estimation method with this case in mind: if even
0.1% of daily answers land in a topic this large, that's 10 such events/day
adding 500,000 fan-out writes each, or 5 million extra writes/day just from
"hot" content — on top of the 240 million vote writes already budgeted.
That's the concrete number that justifies pulling (computing on read) for
very large fan-out targets instead of pushing to every follower, which
we'll design explicitly in the next lesson.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is spending the estimation phase entirely on text
  storage — "how many bytes per answer, times number of answers" — because
  it feels like the YouTube-style exercise. For a Q&A platform the storage
  number is almost always a rounding error. The numbers that actually
  drive the design are vote throughput and feed-assembly QPS, plus the
  fan-out shape of a small number of very-high-follower topics or users.
  Skipping those in favor of a comfortable storage calculation leaves you
  with no justification for the ranking and feed-generation choices you'll
  need to defend later.
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The shape we derived — cheap content storage, a large approximate-counter
write stream, and an expensive personalized-feed read path with a
celebrity/hot-topic fan-out risk — is exactly what drives the high-level
architecture in the next lesson: a content service backed by a simple
store, a counting/aggregation pipeline decoupled from the request path,
and a feed service that mixes precomputed fan-out with on-demand
computation for outsized topics. Keep these numbers in mind as you move
forward — every choice in the rest of this module should trace back to one
of them.
