"Design a payment system" shows up in interviews for a reason that has
nothing to do with checkout pages: it's one of the few prompts where a
wrong design has a dollar sign attached to it. A newsfeed that shows a
stale post is a bug. A payment system that runs the same charge twice, or
that shows a customer's order as "paid" when the bank actually declined
it, is a lawsuit. That constraint changes almost every default instinct
you have from other system design problems — retries, caching, eventual
consistency, and "just fire and forget" all need to be rethought when
money is what's moving.

This lesson scopes the problem and builds the mental model you'll use for
the rest of the module: what the system actually has to guarantee, who
the external parties are, and the state machine a single payment moves
through from the moment a customer clicks "Pay" to the moment cash
actually lands in a merchant's bank account. The next lesson in this
module, "Idempotency, Ledgers, and Reconciliation," goes deep on the
mechanisms that make this flow safe under retries and failures — this
lesson is about getting the shape of the problem right first.

## What are we actually building?

Assume the prompt is: "Design a payment system that lets an e-commerce
platform charge a customer's credit card for an order." Concretely, that
system needs to:

- Accept a payment request from a merchant's checkout flow (amount,
  currency, order ID, and a reference to the customer's payment method).
- Talk to an external **payment gateway** (think Stripe, Adyen, or a
  direct connection to a card network) to actually move money — your
  system almost never touches raw card numbers or moves funds itself.
- Track the state of that payment reliably, even if your service crashes,
  the network times out, or the gateway is slow to respond.
- Support **refunds** and **partial captures** (e.g. an order ships in two
  boxes on two different days, and you only capture funds for each box
  when it ships).
- Reconcile what your system recorded against what the bank and gateway
  actually settled, so discrepancies get caught instead of silently
  costing someone money.

Say explicitly in the interview that you are **not** designing a bank, a
card network, or a PCI-compliant card vault — you're designing the
orchestration layer between a merchant's app and those external systems.
Naming that boundary early shows you understand what's in scope.

## Functional requirements

- A merchant can create a **payment** for an order: amount, currency,
  customer, and payment method.
- The system **authorizes** the payment (confirms funds are available and
  places a hold) before doing anything the merchant can see as "success."
- The system **captures** an authorized payment (actually moves the
  money), either immediately or later, and supports partial captures.
- The system supports **voids** (cancel an authorization before capture)
  and **refunds** (reverse a completed capture, fully or partially).
- Merchants and customers can query a payment's current status and
  history.
- The system **reconciles** its records against the gateway's/bank's
  settlement reports on a regular cadence.

## Non-functional requirements

- **Correctness over availability.** If you have to choose, a payment
  system should fail loudly and refuse to charge rather than risk a
  double-charge or a lost charge. This is the opposite bias from most
  consumer systems, where "show something, even if slightly stale" is
  fine.
- **Exactly-once effect, not exactly-once delivery.** Networks will retry
  requests, gateways will send duplicate webhooks, and clients will
  double-click "Pay." The system must guarantee that no matter how many
  times the same logical request arrives, the customer is charged once.
  (This is the idempotency problem — flagged here, solved in depth next
  lesson.)
- **Auditability.** Every state transition needs a durable, queryable
  trail — regulators, disputes, and your own future debugging sessions
  all need it.
- **Latency**: authorization should typically complete in under 2-3
  seconds so checkout doesn't feel broken, but it's fine for settlement
  and reconciliation to be asynchronous and take hours.
- **Security and compliance**: card data itself should never touch your
  servers in raw form — tokenize it at the client or via the gateway's
  hosted fields, and let PCI-DSS scope stay with the gateway.

<Callout type="interview">
  If the interviewer doesn't specify a domain, pick one (card payments for
  e-commerce is the easiest to reason about) and say so out loud, rather
  than trying to design a system generic enough for cards, bank transfers,
  and wallets simultaneously. You can mention those as extensions once the
  core flow is solid.
</Callout>

## The payment state machine

Every payment request should map onto an explicit state machine, not a
boolean "success/failure" flag. A typical shape:

- **Created** — request received and validated, nothing sent externally
  yet.
- **Authorized** — the gateway/bank confirmed funds exist and placed a
  hold; no money has moved yet.
- **Captured** — funds have actually been moved from the customer to the
  merchant (fully or partially).
- **Settled** — the captured funds have cleared through the banking
  network into the merchant's account (this step is usually asynchronous
  and batched, often happening hours or a day later).
- **Failed** — authorization or capture was declined or errored.
- **Voided** — an authorization was canceled before capture.
- **Refunded** — a captured payment was reversed, fully or partially,
  after the fact.

Draw this on the whiteboard rather than skip it: most hard edge cases in a
payments interview ("cancel after paying but before shipping?", "two boxes
ship on different days?") are just questions about which transitions are
legal and when. Splitting **authorize** from **capture** is the single
most important decision in this whole system — it's what makes delayed
fulfillment, partial shipments, and expiring holds possible at all.

## The end-to-end request flow

Walk through a single card payment from checkout to settlement:

1. The customer's browser or app collects card details and tokenizes them
   client-side via the gateway's SDK — your servers receive only a token,
   never the raw card number.
2. The merchant's backend calls your payment system's `create payment`
   API with the amount, currency, order ID, and the token, including a
   client-generated **idempotency key**.
3. Your system persists a `Created` payment record, then calls the
   gateway's authorize endpoint.
4. The gateway talks to the card network and issuing bank, gets an
   approve/decline decision, and returns it (usually within a second or
   two).
5. Your system records `Authorized` or `Failed` and returns the result to
   the merchant, who shows the customer an order confirmation (or a
   decline message).
6. Later — immediately, or when the order actually ships — the merchant
   triggers a **capture**. Your system calls the gateway's capture
   endpoint for the authorized amount (or less, for a partial capture)
   and records `Captured`.
7. The gateway batches captured transactions and settles them with the
   bank, typically overnight; your system receives a **webhook** or
   pulls a **settlement report** confirming funds actually cleared, and
   updates the record to `Settled`.
8. If a refund is issued, your system calls the gateway's refund endpoint
   and records `Refunded`, referencing the original capture.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake: treating the synchronous API response from "create
  payment" as the final word on whether the customer was charged. Gateway
  calls can time out *after* the charge has already gone through on the
  bank's side — your server never sees the success response, but the
  customer's card was charged. If you retry blindly on timeout, you risk a
  double charge; if you assume failure and don't retry, you risk telling
  the customer "payment failed" when it actually succeeded. The fix is an
  idempotency key on every write, plus treating "unknown" as its own
  state that gets resolved by querying the gateway or waiting for its
  webhook — never treat "no response" as equivalent to "failed."
</Callout>

## Worked example: sizing the write path

Say the platform processes 500,000 orders a day, each producing on
average 1.3 payment-system writes (one authorize, plus a capture on about
30% of orders needing a separate capture step for delayed shipping, plus a
smaller number of refunds and retries). That's roughly 650,000 writes/day,
about 7.5 writes/second average. Payments cluster around business hours
and sale events, so a realistic peak-to-average ratio of 5-8x puts peak
load around 40-60 writes/second — a volume any relational database with
proper indexing handles comfortably. The hard part isn't throughput, it's
correctness under retries and partial failures, which is exactly why
idempotency and reconciliation get a dedicated lesson next.

You now have: the requirements a payment system must satisfy, the
external boundary (gateway/bank do the actual money movement), the state
machine every payment moves through, and the end-to-end flow from
checkout to settlement. The next lesson builds on this exact state
machine to solve the two problems only hinted at here — making every
write safe to retry, and proving after the fact that your ledger matches
what actually happened at the bank.
