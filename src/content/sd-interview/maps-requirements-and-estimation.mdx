"Design Google Maps" sounds like one problem but is really three problems
wearing a trenchcoat: rendering a map you can pan and zoom, finding a place
by name or category, and computing a route with a live ETA between two
points. Candidates who treat it as one big undifferentiated "maps system"
end up with a design that's vague everywhere instead of solid anywhere. The
fix, as with any large system design prompt, is to nail requirements and
scale before drawing a single box — the numbers you produce here decide
whether you're building a tile-caching problem, a search-index problem, or
a routing-graph problem, and in which order to prioritize them.

This lesson is that scoping step. The high-level design, data model, and
routing lessons that follow all build directly on the constraints
established here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

In a 45-minute interview you cannot design "everything Google Maps does."
Propose a core scope and get the interviewer to confirm or redirect:

- **Map viewing**: a user can pan and zoom a map and see roads, buildings,
  and points of interest rendered smoothly.
- **Search**: a user can search for a place by name, address, or category
  ("coffee near me") and get ranked results with locations.
- **Routing and navigation**: given a start and end point, the system
  returns one or more routes with an estimated time of arrival (ETA), and
  can track a user's live position along that route.
- **Live traffic**: routes and ETAs should reflect current road conditions,
  not just static road speeds.

Explicitly defer, and say so out loud: street view imagery, public-transit
schedules, ride-hailing integration, and offline map downloads are all real
Google Maps features, but each deserves its own design discussion. Naming
what you're excluding is what tells the interviewer you understand the
problem's actual size.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Read-heavy, latency-sensitive map viewing.** Tiles must render in well
  under a second while panning, or the map feels broken — this pushes hard
  toward caching and CDN-style delivery over computing tiles on demand.
  Availability matters far more than strict consistency here: a tile that's
  a day stale is invisible to the user.
- **Routing must be fast and "good enough," not globally optimal.** Users
  expect a route back in under a second or two; spending 30 seconds to
  shave 40 seconds off a 25-minute drive is a bad trade. Correctness
  (a valid, drivable route) matters more than finding the mathematically
  shortest path every time.
- **Live location tracking must be low-latency but can tolerate loss.**
  Missing one GPS ping out of a stream every few seconds is fine; the
  client just interpolates. This is a classic case for favoring
  availability and throughput over strict delivery guarantees.
- **Road network and place data change slowly; traffic changes constantly.**
  These have very different staleness budgets and should not be treated as
  one data-freshness problem.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Concrete, stated assumptions beat a design built on "big numbers,
somehow." A reasonable working set for a global, Google Maps-scale service:

- 1 billion monthly active users, 150 million daily active users (DAU).
- Of those DAU, roughly 20% (30 million) start at least one active
  navigation session per day (turn-by-turn routing, not just glancing at
  the map).
- An average navigation session lasts 20 minutes and sends a location
  update every 4 seconds.
- The world's drivable road network is represented as roughly 500 million
  road segments (edges) once broken into short, routable pieces.
- The map is rendered as pre-generated tiles across 20 zoom levels (a
  standard range from whole-world view down to street-level detail).

<Callout type="tip">
  Say these numbers out loud before you compute anything, and invite a
  correction: "I'll assume 150 million DAU with about 20% of them actively
  navigating on a given day — does that match what you had in mind, or
  should I adjust?" It turns estimation into a checkpoint with the
  interviewer instead of a monologue they have to silently evaluate.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive the numbers for each sub-system

Work through map viewing, search, and routing/navigation separately —
lumping them into one QPS number hides which part of the system is
actually under the most load.

### Map tile storage

Each zoom level roughly quadruples the number of tiles needed to cover the
same area, since each tile at zoom `n` splits into four tiles at zoom
`n + 1`. At zoom level 0 the whole world is one tile; by zoom level 20
(building-level detail in dense areas) the tile count is enormous, though
in practice you don't pre-render every deep zoom level everywhere — dense
cities need it, open ocean doesn't.

- Assume an average of 256 KB per rendered tile (vector or raster,
  compressed) and that, across all 20 zoom levels combined, roughly 5
  billion tiles are actually pre-rendered and stored once you account for
  sparse rendering over low-interest areas (oceans, deep wilderness).
- Total tile storage: 5,000,000,000 tiles multiplied by 256 KB is about
  1.2 PB.

That number alone tells you tiles belong in blob/object storage behind a
CDN, not a database — this is nearly identical in shape to the "static,
cacheable assets at massive scale" problem you'd see designing a video
platform's read path, just applied to map imagery instead of video
renditions.

### Search QPS

- Assume 150 million DAU each issue an average of 3 search queries per day
  (place lookups, "near me" queries, address entry).
- Daily searches: 150,000,000 multiplied by 3 is 450 million searches/day.
- Average QPS: 450,000,000 divided by 86,400 seconds is about 5,200
  queries/sec. With a 5x peak multiplier for commute-hour spikes, peak
  search load is roughly 26,000 queries/sec.

### Routing and live navigation

- Route requests (the initial "give me a route" call): assume the 30
  million daily navigators each request 1.5 routes on average (some
  re-route mid-trip), giving 45 million route computations/day, or about
  520/sec average — small compared to search, but each one is more
  compute-heavy (a graph search over the road network) than a simple
  lookup.
- Live location pings: 30 million concurrent-ish sessions is the wrong way
  to think about it — instead, compute total pings across all sessions.
  Each 20-minute session at one ping per 4 seconds sends 300 pings per
  session. 30,000,000 sessions multiplied by 300 pings is 9 billion
  location updates/day, or roughly 104,000 pings/sec on average.

This is the counter-intuitive result worth calling out explicitly in an
interview: **live location ingestion, not search or route computation, is
the highest-QPS path in the whole system**, because it's a continuous
stream per active trip rather than a single request-response.

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

Turn the estimates into constraints you'll defend later in the interview:

1. **Tiles are a caching/CDN problem, not a compute-on-demand problem** —
   at 1.2 PB and near-static content, pre-render and cache aggressively;
   never render a tile per request.
2. **Location ingestion is the dominant QPS load** — at roughly 104,000
   pings/sec, this path needs a purpose-built, horizontally scalable
   ingestion pipeline (think a partitioned message queue keyed by user or
   trip), not a synchronous database write per ping.
3. **Routing computation is comparatively rare but expensive per call** —
   a graph search over hundreds of millions of edges is not something you
   re-run from scratch on every ping; only the initial route request and
   occasional re-routes need the heavy computation.
4. **Freshness requirements vary sharply by data type** — traffic and live
   position data need near-real-time handling; road geometry and place
   metadata can lag by minutes to hours with no user-visible impact.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is computing one blended "QPS for the maps system" and
  sizing everything to it. That hides the fact that search, routing, and
  location ingestion have wildly different volumes and latency budgets — a
  system sized only for the ~26K search QPS would be crushed by the ~104K
  location-ping QPS it never accounted for. Always break estimates out per
  sub-system before combining anything into an infrastructure plan.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sanity-checking a follow-up

An interviewer might push: "What if we tightened the location-ping
interval from 4 seconds to 1 second for smoother live tracking?" Re-derive
rather than guessing: pings per session go from 300 (20 minutes / 4
seconds) to 1,200 (20 minutes / 1 second), a 4x increase, so location
ingestion QPS jumps from roughly 104,000/sec to about 416,000/sec — while
search and routing QPS are completely unaffected, since neither depends on
ping frequency. That's the right instinct to demonstrate: identify exactly
which estimate a changed assumption touches, rather than re-computing
everything from scratch or assuming a global slowdown.

## Where this leads

The three-way split from this lesson — read-heavy cacheable tiles, a
place-search index, and a routing/tracking path with real-time ingestion —
is exactly the shape of the high-level architecture in the next lesson: a
tile-serving layer behind a CDN, a search service over indexed place data,
and a routing service backed by a road-network graph, with a separate
ingestion pipeline for live location updates. Keep the QPS gap between
search (~26K/sec) and location pings (~104K/sec) in mind — it's the reason
those two paths end up on very different infrastructure.
