You've now spent four lessons going deep on requirements, the high-level
architecture, matching and dispatch, real-time location, and pricing and
ETA. Each of those lessons deliberately isolated one concern so it could be
taught cleanly. A real interview never asks you to present five separate
concerns — it gives you 45 minutes and expects one coherent design, and the
questions that actually separate a strong answer from a mediocre one live
in the seams *between* those pieces: what happens when location data is a
few seconds stale and matching uses it anyway, what happens when a match
fails and a driver needs to become available again, what happens when
surge pricing and dispatch success start influencing each other. This
lesson is about assembling the pieces and being ready to defend the seams,
not about re-teaching any of them from scratch.

## Budgeting a 45-minute answer

You cannot re-derive five lessons' worth of depth in one session, and
trying to is the most common way candidates run out of time before
reaching trade-offs. A workable budget:

- **5 minutes — clarify scope.** Rider and driver apps, or just one side?
  One ride type or multiple (pool, premium)? Real-time tracking precision
  required? These answers change what you estimate and design next.
- **5 minutes — capacity estimate.** Trip volume, concurrent drivers,
  location-ping rate, peak-to-average multiplier. Keep this tight — it's
  there to justify design choices later, not to be a standalone exercise.
- **10-12 minutes — high-level architecture.** Name the services (location,
  matching, trip, pricing, notification, payment) and the two-plane split
  between churn-heavy location data and durable trip state.
- **12-15 minutes — one deep dive.** Interviewers usually steer this; the
  most common ask is matching (the driver-claim race) or location (update
  frequency and staleness). Go deep on whichever they pick rather than
  trying to touch all three lightly.
- **remaining time — trade-offs and failure modes.** This is where the
  seams below come in, and it's often skipped when time runs short — don't
  let it be the part you cut.

## Where the seams actually connect

Each earlier lesson treated its service as a clean box with a clean
interface. In reality, three connections between services carry real
design weight, and an interviewer who's paying attention will probe
exactly these.

### Location freshness is matching's problem too

The location service answers "who's nearby" from whatever it last heard.
If a driver's last ping is 12 seconds old and they're moving at city
traffic speed, "nearby" might already be wrong by 100-150 meters. Matching
has to apply a freshness cutoff — discard or down-rank any candidate whose
last ping exceeds some threshold (say 15-20 seconds) — rather than trusting
the location index blindly. Push this threshold too low and you shrink
your candidate pool during exactly the load spikes when drivers' pings are
most likely to queue up; push it too high and you dispatch drivers who've
already turned a corner.

### Pricing and matching feed each other

Pricing reads live supply/demand signals — often derived from the same
data matching uses (how many available drivers near a hot area, how many
open ride requests). But it runs the other way too: when match success
rate for a region drops (too many riders, too few available drivers),
that's itself a demand signal that should push price up, which changes who
actually requests a ride, which changes the match rate again. This is a
closed feedback loop, not two independent read paths, and naming it
explicitly is one of the highest-value things you can say in this part of
the interview.

### A failed or cancelled match must release its claim

Matching's driver-claim step (covered in the matching lesson) takes a
driver out of the "available" pool the instant a candidate is selected. If
the rider cancels during `driver_en_route`, or the driver's app times out
mid-claim, that driver has to go back into the available pool immediately
— not after some idle-timeout garbage-collection sweep. Miss this and
you silently bleed driver supply during the busiest, most contested
minutes of a surge, which is the worst possible time to lose capacity you
already had.

## Worked example: a seam breaking under Friday-night surge

Say a downtown zone has 400 available drivers and a normal request rate of
80 requests/minute at 8pm on a Friday, but a concert lets out and requests
spike to 600/minute for ten minutes. Matching's claim success rate drops
from its usual ~95% to maybe 60%, because many candidate drivers returned
by the location query get claimed by a competing request microseconds
earlier. Two things need to happen at this exact seam: pricing needs to see
the falling match-success signal and raise the surge multiplier within
seconds (not the next batch cycle) so demand cools and some drivers who
were idle nearby get pulled in by the higher fare; and every failed claim
needs to release its driver back to the pool in well under a second, or
the 400-driver pool effectively shrinks to something smaller purely from
claim contention, making the surge worse than the raw supply/demand numbers
would suggest. If you can narrate that dynamic instead of just saying "we
raise prices during surge," you've demonstrated you understand the system
as a system, not five stapled-together diagrams.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common failure in a synthesis-style answer is presenting each
  service correctly in isolation and never connecting them — describing
  matching, then pricing, then location as three unrelated slides. An
  interviewer will notice the silence at the seams and ask directly: "how
  does pricing know demand is high?" or "what happens to the driver if the
  match attempt fails?" Have the feedback loops and failure-state answers
  ready before you're asked, and mention at least one of them proactively
  during your high-level walkthrough.
</Callout>

## Closing the design

Two states deserve explicit handling because they're easy to gloss over
when you're moving fast: **no drivers found** (trip service needs a
distinct terminal-ish state here — retry with a wider radius, surface a
wait estimate, or suggest a different ride type, rather than silently
failing) and **payment failure at trip completion** (the trip should sit in
a `completed_pending_payment` state with retries, not be marked fully
closed until payment actually confirms). Both are one sentence to mention
and both are exactly the kind of edge case interviewers ask about once the
happy path is drawn. With the architecture, the two feedback loops, and
these two failure states in hand, you have a design that holds together
end to end rather than five good answers to five separate questions.
