"Design Twitter" shows up in almost every system design interview loop
because it looks deceptively simple — post a short message, show it to
followers — and then quietly turns into one of the hardest fan-out
problems in the field. The trap is treating it like a chat app (deliver
one message to one recipient) or a blog (write once, read rarely). Twitter
is neither: it's a system where a single write from one account can need
to appear, within seconds, in the timelines of tens of millions of
followers, while the overwhelming majority of accounts have so few
followers that the same operation is nearly free.

This lesson exists to pin down the requirements and the numbers before any
of that gets designed. The timeline-generation strategy, the data model,
and the scaling decisions in the rest of this module are all direct
responses to the read:write ratio and the follower-count skew we establish
here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Agree on scope out loud rather than assuming everything is in play. A
reasonable core for an interview-sized version of Twitter:

- **Post a tweet**: short text (up to some character limit), optionally
  with an image or video attached.
- **Follow**: a user follows other users; following is one-directional
  (unlike a mutual "friend" relationship).
- **Home timeline**: a reverse-chronological (or lightly ranked) feed of
  tweets from accounts a user follows.
- **Like and retweet**: lightweight engagement actions that also need to
  surface in timelines and counts.
- **User timeline / profile**: the list of tweets a single account has
  posted.

Explicitly defer and say so: direct messages, search, trending topics,
detailed abuse/spam ML, ads, and threaded replies as a first-class nested
structure are all real features of the product, but each is its own
design problem. Naming them and moving on is a strong signal in an
interview.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Extremely read-heavy.** Users check timelines far more often than
  they post — the read path has to be fast and cheap even though the
  underlying data changes constantly.
- **Low write-to-read latency for the home timeline.** A tweet from a
  followed account should show up within a few seconds, not minutes — this
  is closer to a "live feed" latency budget than a batch-report one.
- **Tweets must be durable once acknowledged.** Losing a posted tweet is a
  hard failure; a like count or retweet count lagging by a few seconds is
  invisible to users.
- **Available over strictly consistent.** It's fine if two users briefly
  see slightly different versions of a timeline or counter; it's not fine
  if the whole write path goes down because one replica is unreachable.
- **Highly skewed load.** A small number of accounts (celebrities, news
  outlets) generate a disproportionate share of both fan-out reads and
  writes — the system has to survive that skew, not just the average case.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Rough, explicitly-stated assumptions beat no assumptions. A workable set
of numbers for a large (Twitter-sized) platform:

- 250 million daily active users (DAU).
- Each DAU opens their home timeline about 6 times per day, loading
  roughly 40 tweets per open.
- 1 in 500 DAU posts at least one tweet on a given day.
- Each DAU performs about 15 engagement actions per day (likes + retweets
  combined).
- Average follower count across all accounts: about 200. But the
  distribution is heavily skewed — the top accounts have tens of millions
  of followers.
- Average tweet: roughly 100 characters of text (call it 300 bytes with
  metadata); about 1 in 5 tweets includes a photo (~200 KB average).

<Callout type="tip">
  Say the numbers out loud before computing anything: "Let's assume 250
  million DAU, roughly 40 tweets per timeline load, and 6 loads a day per
  user — does that match the scale you had in mind?" Turning the estimate
  into a checkpoint with the interviewer beats silently crunching numbers.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive traffic and storage numbers

Work through tweet writes, timeline reads, and engagement writes
separately — each stresses a different part of the system.

### Tweet write throughput

- Daily tweets: 250,000,000 divided by 500 is 500,000 tweets/day.
- Writes per second (average): 500,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  6/sec — tiny, even with a generous 10x peak multiplier (roughly 60/sec
  at peak).

### Timeline read throughput

- Daily timeline opens: 250,000,000 multiplied by 6 is 1.5 billion
  opens/day.
- Tweets served: 1.5 billion multiplied by 40 is 60 billion tweet-views/day.
- Opens per second (average): 1.5 billion divided by 86,400 is about
  17,000/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 85,000 timeline-assembly
  requests/sec at peak.
- That's a read:write ratio on the order of **2,800:1** on requests alone,
  and far higher on the number of individual tweet-records touched, since
  each read pulls back 40 tweets.

### Engagement (like/retweet) throughput

- Daily engagement actions: 250,000,000 multiplied by 15 is 3.75 billion
  actions/day.
- Actions per second (average): 3.75 billion divided by 86,400 is about
  43,000/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 215,000/sec at peak.
- Like this figure looks big, but each action is a small counter increment
  and a cheap join-table row, not a timeline rewrite — it's a different
  kind of load than tweet writes or timeline reads.

### Storage

- Daily text storage: 500,000 tweets multiplied by 300 bytes is about
  150 MB/day — trivial even over years.
- Daily media storage: 1 in 5 tweets (100,000/day) multiplied by 200 KB is
  about 20 GB/day, or roughly 7 TB/year — and this is the dominant
  storage line item by a wide margin over text.

The contrast is the key takeaway: **tweet writes are cheap, storage for
text is cheap, and even engagement writes are individually cheap — the
expensive part is assembling and serving personalized timelines at
~85,000 requests/sec, especially for the follower-count outliers.**

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **Home timelines should be mostly precomputed, not assembled from
   scratch per request** — at ~85,000 read requests/sec, running a live
   query across every followed account's recent tweets on each timeline
   open is far too expensive to do synchronously for every user.
2. **Tweet writes are cheap enough to afford some extra work per write** —
   with only ~60 writes/sec at peak, a design that pushes each new tweet
   out to precomputed follower timelines (fan-out-on-write) is affordable
   for typical accounts.
3. **The follower-count skew breaks a uniform fan-out-on-write strategy** —
   pushing one tweet into tens of millions of follower timelines the
   instant it's posted is a completely different cost profile than pushing
   it to 200 followers. This "celebrity problem" needs a hybrid push/pull
   design, which the next lesson builds explicitly.
4. **Storage engineering should be planned around media, not text** —
   photo/video bytes dwarf tweet text by a couple of orders of magnitude,
   so object storage and CDN delivery for media matter more than the
   schema for the tweet row itself.

## Worked example: sanity-checking a celebrity tweet

Suppose an account with 50 million followers posts a single tweet. A pure
fan-out-on-write design would immediately insert that one tweet into 50
million separate precomputed timeline structures — a 50-million-write
burst triggered by one event, dwarfing the entire system's average tweet
write rate (6/sec) by seven orders of magnitude for that one moment.

Even if this happens only 20 times a day across all such large accounts,
that's `20 x 50,000,000` = 1 billion extra fan-out writes/day purely from
outlier accounts — more than double the total tweet-write volume the rest
of the platform generates. That's the concrete number that justifies
**not** fanning out to every follower for very large accounts, and instead
computing their tweets into a follower's timeline at read time (pull) —
the hybrid model the next lesson designs in detail.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is spending the whole estimation phase on tweet
  character counts and storage bytes, because it feels like a clean,
  countable exercise. For a microblogging platform, text storage is
  almost always a rounding error. The numbers that actually drive the
  design are the timeline-read:tweet-write ratio and the shape of the
  follower graph — specifically, how far the largest accounts sit from
  the average. Skip that and you have no numeric justification for the
  fan-out strategy you'll need to defend in the next lesson.
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The shape derived here — cheap tweet writes, a read path two to three
orders of magnitude larger than the write path, media as the real storage
cost, and a follower graph with a long, heavy tail — is exactly what the
next lesson's high-level design responds to: a tweet-ingestion service, a
fan-out worker that treats "normal" and "celebrity" accounts differently,
and a timeline service built to serve precomputed data fast. Keep these
numbers in mind going forward; every architectural choice in this module
should trace back to one of them.
