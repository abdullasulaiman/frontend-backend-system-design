By this point you've worked through requirements gathering, capacity
estimation, high-level architecture, and deep dives across a dozen case
studies — feeds, maps, payments, chat, search, and more. You know the
building blocks: load balancers, caches, queues, sharding, consensus,
consistency trade-offs. None of that guarantees a good interview.

The uncomfortable truth about system design interviews is that two
candidates with identical technical knowledge can get very different
scores based purely on how they used the 45 minutes. One rambles through
requirements for 15 minutes and never gets to a deep dive. Another jumps
straight to drawing Kafka and Cassandra boxes without ever asking what the
system needs to do. This lesson is about the meta-skill that sits above
the technical content: running the interview itself as a structured
conversation you're driving, not a lecture you're delivering or an
interrogation you're enduring.

## The interview is a conversation, not an exam

The single biggest mindset shift that separates strong performances from
weak ones: your interviewer is not a proctor silently scoring a written
test. They are (usually) a senior engineer who has designed something
like this before, sitting there wanting to see how you think, and often
willing to nudge you if you ask the right questions or look stuck. Treat
the 45 minutes as a design review with a colleague, not a solo
performance.

That means:

- **Talk while you think.** Silence for 90 seconds while you stare at the
  whiteboard tells the interviewer nothing. Narrate your reasoning even
  when you're unsure — "I'm weighing a queue versus direct write here
  because..." gives them something to grade and something to redirect.
- **Ask, don't assume.** Every case study in this course opened with
  requirements gathering for a reason: assumed requirements that turn out
  wrong can invalidate 20 minutes of design work. A clarifying question
  costs 10 seconds; a wrong assumption costs the rest of the interview.
- **Watch for steering, and follow it.** If your interviewer keeps
  pulling the conversation toward, say, how you'd handle duplicate
  message delivery, that's not a random tangent — that's almost certainly
  the area they're scoring most heavily. Following that thread beats
  finishing your own planned outline.

## Budgeting the clock

A 45-minute system design interview has a shape, and losing track of it
is the single most common way candidates run out of time before reaching
the parts that actually differentiate strong answers. A rough allocation
that works across most case studies:

| Phase | Time | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements & scope | ~5 min | Functional scope, scale, and the 1-2 features to prioritize |
| Capacity estimation | ~5 min | Rough QPS, storage, bandwidth numbers to justify design choices |
| High-level design | ~10 min | Boxes and arrows for the main request path, end to end |
| Deep dives | ~15-20 min | The 1-3 components the interviewer cares most about |
| Wrap-up | ~5 min | Bottlenecks, trade-offs, what you'd do with more time |

Say this plan out loud in the first minute: "I'd like to spend a few
minutes on requirements, then sketch a high-level design, then go deep on
whatever you'd like to focus on — does that work?" This does two things:
it shows structure without being asked, and it gives the interviewer an
explicit moment to redirect ("actually, let's spend most of the time on
the ranking algorithm") before you've committed to the wrong emphasis.

Notice the estimation step is short on purpose — it exists to produce two
or three numbers that justify later decisions ("at 50K writes/sec we need
to shard, a single Postgres primary won't take this"), not to be a math
exam. Spending 15 minutes computing exact bandwidth to three significant
figures is a classic way to burn the clock on the least-graded part of
the interview.

## Recovering when a deep dive goes sideways

Every candidate eventually hits a question they can't cleanly answer.
What separates a recoverable moment from an interview-ending one is how
you handle it:

- **Say what you don't know, then reason anyway.** "I haven't
  implemented consistent hashing myself, but here's how I'd think about
  it: we need to add/remove shards without reshuffling everything, so..."
  This is worth more than guessing confidently and worth far more than
  going silent.
- **Fall back to the trade-off vocabulary you already know.** If you
  blank on the specific mechanism, you can usually still reason about
  consistency vs. availability, latency vs. throughput, or push vs. pull
  — general trade-offs from earlier lessons apply even when the specific
  technology name escapes you.
- **Ask for a hint, cleanly.** "Would it help if I focused on the
  read path or the write path first?" is a legitimate move, not a
  concession of failure. Interviewers expect to give hints; candidates
  who never ask often just spend more time stuck.

## A worked example: recognizing a steer mid-interview

Say you're 20 minutes into designing a URL shortener. You've covered
requirements, estimated ~1,000 writes/sec and ~10,000 reads/sec, and
sketched a high-level design: an API layer, a key-generation service, a
key-value store, and a cache in front of reads. You're about to walk
through your base-62 encoding scheme for short codes when the interviewer
asks: "What happens if two app servers try to generate the same short
code at the same time?"

That question is a steer. It's telling you the interviewer cares about
concurrency correctness in key generation, not the encoding scheme
itself. The wrong response is to finish your planned explanation of
base-62 encoding and then circle back. The right response is to drop the
plan immediately: "Good question — let's say we're handing out ranges of
IDs to each server so they never overlap, backed by a counter service, so
two servers can never independently pick the same code." You've now
spent 90 seconds directly on what's being graded instead of 5 minutes on
what you'd rehearsed.

This is the pattern across every case study in this course: the
technical content (sharding, caching, ledgers, ranking) is necessary but
not sufficient. The candidates who score best are the ones who treat
interviewer questions as the scoring rubric made visible, and adjust in
real time.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common and costly mistake: treating requirements gathering as a
  box-checking ritual you rush through to "get to the real design." Doing
  this produces designs built on invented assumptions — sometimes wrong
  ones — and often means running out of time before reaching a deep dive,
  since a design built on the wrong requirements has to be partially
  redone once the mismatch surfaces. Spend the first five minutes
  genuinely narrowing scope and confirming scale; it's the cheapest time
  in the interview to spend well.
</Callout>

## Bringing it together

None of this replaces knowing the material — a perfectly paced interview
built on a broken architecture still fails. But an excellent architecture
delivered with no narration, poor time budgeting, and no response to
steering will consistently score lower than a good-enough architecture
delivered as a clear, adaptive conversation. Strategy and content are both
necessary; this course's case studies gave you the content, and this
lesson is the strategy layer that makes it land on interview day. The
next and final lesson turns this into a concrete pre-interview checklist.
