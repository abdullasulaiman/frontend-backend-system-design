Almost every backend system eventually needs to tell a human that something
happened: an order shipped, a payment failed, someone commented on your
post, a security alert needs attention. It looks like a small feature —
"just send an email" — until it's plugged into fifty different services,
each of which wants to reach users through push, email, SMS, and in-app
alerts, with different urgency, different rate limits, and different
provider APIs. Interviewers like this problem because a naive design (each
service calls the email API directly) looks fine in a demo and falls over
in production the first time an email provider has a bad day and takes
half your product down with it.

This lesson designs a general-purpose notification system: a service that
sits between "something happened" and "the user was told," and does it
reliably, at scale, without becoming a single point of failure for the rest
of the company's backend.

## Requirements

**Functional**

- Accept notification requests from many internal services (order service,
  social feed, security team, marketing) with a common API.
- Deliver through multiple channels: push (mobile), email, SMS, and in-app
  (a bell icon with unread items).
- Respect per-user preferences — which channels, which categories, quiet
  hours.
- Support both single, immediate notifications ("your payment failed") and
  templated bulk sends ("flash sale starts in 1 hour," to 10 million
  users).

**Non-functional**

- Reliability over raw speed for most notification types — a notification
  is allowed to arrive a few seconds late, but it must not silently
  disappear.
- Scale to tens of millions of notifications a day, with bursty spikes
  (a marketing campaign or an outage alert can 100x normal volume in
  minutes).
- No single slow or failing downstream provider should degrade unrelated
  traffic (an SMS provider outage shouldn't delay push notifications).
- At-least-once delivery per channel, with dedup so retries don't spam
  users.

What's explicitly out of scope for this lesson: rendering rich in-app
notification feeds (that overlaps with a general feed/timeline design) and
building your own SMS/push carrier infrastructure — we treat those as
third-party providers behind an API, which is how virtually every real
system does it.

## High-level design

The core idea is a **producer/consumer split with a durable queue in the
middle**. Internal services are producers: they don't know or care how
delivery happens, they just publish "notify user X that Y happened." A
fan-out layer reads those events, expands them into per-channel jobs, and
hands each job to a channel-specific worker pool that talks to the actual
provider (APNs/FCM for push, an email API, an SMS gateway).

Walking the request path:

1. A producing service calls the **notification API** with a structured
   event: user ID (or a list/segment), event type, and the data needed to
   render it (order ID, commenter name, etc.) — never a pre-rendered
   message, so templates can be updated without touching every producer.
2. The API writes the event to a **durable queue** and returns immediately.
   This is the single most important decision in the whole design: the
   caller's request finishes the instant the event is durably queued, not
   when the notification is actually delivered.
3. A **fan-out worker** picks up the event, looks up the user's channel
   preferences and quiet-hours settings, decides which channels apply, and
   renders the per-channel message from a template.
4. It enqueues one job per channel onto per-channel queues.
5. **Channel workers** each own their provider integration, retry policy,
   and rate limiting, and write delivery status back to a status store.

### Why split fan-out from delivery

You could try to do steps 3-5 in one worker, but splitting them buys two
things. First, isolation: if the SMS gateway is down, SMS jobs pile up in
their own queue while push and email keep flowing — nothing shares a
thread pool or a retry budget with anything else. Second, independent
scaling: email volume and push volume don't grow at the same rate, and
providers have wildly different rate limits (an SMS gateway might cap you
at a few hundred messages per second; a push provider might handle tens of
thousands). Separate queues and worker pools let you scale each
independently instead of over-provisioning everything to match your
noisiest channel.

## Deep dive: the pieces that make this actually reliable

### Idempotency and exactly-once-feeling delivery

Queues in this kind of system are almost always **at-least-once**: a
worker can crash after sending to the provider but before acknowledging
the queue message, causing a redelivery. If you re-run the send blindly,
the user gets the same push notification twice. The fix is a dedup key —
typically `hash(event_id, channel, user_id)` — checked against a store
(Redis with a TTL works well) before sending. If the key already exists,
skip the send and just re-acknowledge the queue message. This turns
"at-least-once delivery to the queue" into "effectively-once delivery to
the user," which is what actually matters.

### Preferences, quiet hours, and digesting

Preferences aren't just an on/off toggle — treat them as a matrix of
(category, channel): a user might want push for "someone messaged you" but
only email (not SMS) for "your subscription renewed." Check this matrix in
the fan-out worker, before rendering, so you never waste a render or a
provider call on a suppressed notification.

Quiet hours and digesting live in the same layer. Instead of firing five
separate "X liked your post" pushes within a minute, hold low-priority
events in a short window (say, 2-5 minutes) and collapse them into one
"5 people liked your post" notification. High-priority events — a failed
payment, a security alert — skip this window and go out immediately, so
priority needs to be a first-class field on every event, not an
afterthought.

### Delivery status and read receipts

"Sent" (you handed it to the provider), "delivered" (the provider confirms
the device received it), and "read" (the user opened it) are three
different states, and conflating them causes real problems — product
teams will ask "how many people saw this alert" and the honest answer
requires all three. Store a status row per (notification, channel) and
update it as provider webhooks and read events come in. This table is
also your operational dashboard: a spike in "failed" for one channel is
your earliest signal that a provider is degraded.

### Rate limiting and backpressure

Bulk sends (a marketing blast to 10 million users) are a different traffic
shape than transactional sends (one payment failure) and need different
handling. Give bulk campaigns a lower-priority queue and a token-bucket
rate limiter tuned to what each provider allows, so a campaign never
starves a transactional "your password was reset" notification sitting
right behind it in an unpartitioned queue. Priority queues — or simply
separate queues per priority tier consumed with weighted preference — keep
latency-sensitive notifications fast even while a campaign of millions is
draining in the background.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is letting the producing service call the email/SMS/push
  provider directly "just for this one urgent case," bypassing the queue
  "to save time." This reintroduces exactly the coupling the whole design
  exists to avoid: now the order service's checkout latency depends on a
  third-party email API's response time, and an email provider outage can
  take down checkout. Every notification, urgent or not, should go through
  the same durable queue — use priority tiers within the system to make
  urgent notifications fast, not a bypass around it.
</Callout>

## Worked example: estimating the queue and worker fleet

Say the product sends an average of 50 million notifications a day across
all channels, with a peak-to-average ratio of 5x during a flash sale or
incident (common for bursty notification traffic).

- Average rate: 50,000,000 / 86,400 seconds is about 580 events/sec.
- Peak rate: 580 x 5 is about 2,900 events/sec.
- If the channel mix is roughly 60% push, 30% email, 10% SMS, peak SMS
  traffic alone is about 290/sec — comfortably under most SMS gateways'
  per-account rate limits, confirming SMS needs its own queue and worker
  pool rather than sharing capacity with push.
- Each fan-out worker doing a preference lookup, template render, and
  three queue writes might sustain roughly 200-300 events/sec per
  instance, so peak load needs on the order of 10-15 fan-out worker
  instances, auto-scaled off queue depth rather than a fixed count.

The number that actually matters in an interview isn't the precise
instance count — it's showing you can reason from daily volume to peak
QPS to per-channel load, and that you'd size and isolate the SMS path
differently from push because its provider limits are an order of
magnitude tighter.

## Trade-offs to call out

- **Push vs. pull for in-app notifications**: pushing over a WebSocket
  gives instant in-app updates but adds persistent-connection
  infrastructure; polling is simpler but adds latency and load. Most
  systems start with polling plus a lightweight WebSocket/SSE push once
  in-app "live" feel is a real requirement.
- **Single queue vs. per-channel queues**: a single queue is simpler to
  operate but couples channel failure domains together — per-channel
  queues cost more operational surface area but are worth it once you have
  more than one provider with meaningfully different reliability.
- **Synchronous preference checks vs. precomputed preference cache**: at
  very high fan-out volume (a segment of millions), a per-event database
  lookup for preferences doesn't scale — cache preferences in a fast store
  keyed by user ID and invalidate on preference change.
