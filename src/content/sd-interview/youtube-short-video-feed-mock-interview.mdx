Every lesson so far in this module designed the "watch a specific video"
path: a user searches or clicks a link, and you serve one piece of content
well. A short-video feed — think TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels —
is a different consumption model built on the same storage and delivery
foundation. Nobody searches for the next clip; the app decides for them,
and it has to decide fast, over and over, sometimes dozens of times a
minute. That shift changes what an interviewer will actually probe, and
it's common enough as a variant prompt that it's worth a dedicated,
timed run before you sit a real interview.

This lesson is structured as a mock interview: a prompt, a suggested time
budget, and a scoring rubric, rather than a fresh conceptual deep dive. Treat
it like the real thing — set a timer, talk out loud (or write your reasoning
down) as if an interviewer were listening, and only check the rubric after
you've made your attempt.

## What's actually different from long-form YouTube

Don't restart the whole design from scratch. Upload, transcoding, adaptive
bitrate streaming, and CDN edge delivery work the same way they do for a
ten-minute video — reuse everything from the earlier lessons in this
module. Three things genuinely change for a short-video feed, and they're
where this mock interview should spend its time:

- **Discovery is push, not pull.** There's no search box in the primary
  loop. A feed/ranking service decides what to show next, using engagement
  signals (watch-through rate, replays, likes, skips) rather than a search
  query.
- **Prefetching becomes a first-class design concern.** Long-form video
  buffers ahead within *one* video. A feed has to buffer ahead *across*
  videos — fetching manifests and priming CDN connections for content the
  user hasn't chosen yet, so the swipe-to-next transition feels instant.
- **The feedback loop is tight and continuous.** Every few seconds of watch
  time or a quick skip is a signal that should influence what the same
  user sees a few videos later in the same session, not just in tomorrow's
  batch job.

## Suggested time budget

A realistic 45-minute slot for this variant:

- **5-7 min** — clarify scope (feed only, or also upload/search?) and state
  the two or three non-functional priorities you'll optimize for.
- **5 min** — capacity estimate focused on feed QPS and view-event volume,
  not storage (storage math is the same as long-form YouTube; don't redo
  it here).
- **15 min** — high-level architecture: client, feed service, candidate
  generation, ranking, CDN delivery, event pipeline.
- **12 min** — deep dive on prefetching and the ranking-feedback loop —
  this is the part that differentiates a strong answer from a generic one.
- **remaining time** — trade-offs and failure modes (cold-start users, feed
  staleness, thundering-herd on a viral clip).

## The feed and prefetch pipeline

The core request/response loop looks different from a single-video watch
page: the client asks for a *page* of candidate videos, not one video, and
it starts working through a local buffer well before that buffer runs out.

<Mermaid client:visible chart={`
flowchart TD
    A[Client requests next feed page] --> B[Feed Service]
    B --> C[Candidate Generation - embeddings / ANN lookup]
    B --> D[Ranking Model - rerank by predicted engagement]
    C --> D
    D --> E[Return metadata + CDN manifest URLs, ~10 videos]
    E --> F[Client feed buffer]
    F --> G[Player: play current, prefetch next 2-3 manifests]
    G --> H[Watch-time / skip events]
    H --> I[Event pipeline]
    I --> D
`} />

A few points worth calling out explicitly in the interview:

- **Candidate generation vs. ranking are separate stages.** Generation
  narrows millions of videos down to a few hundred plausible candidates
  cheaply (embedding similarity, creator-follow graph, trending pool);
  ranking is a heavier model that scores that smaller set. Doing heavy
  ranking over the entire catalog per request doesn't scale.
- **The client buffer, not just the server, absorbs latency.** If the
  feed service call takes 150ms, that's fine as long as it happens while
  the user is still watching video 3 of a 10-video buffer — the client
  never blocks visibly as long as the buffer stays ahead of playback.
- **The event pipeline closes the loop asynchronously.** Watch-time and
  skip events stream into the ranking model's feature store so the *next*
  feed request for that user reflects what just happened, without making
  playback itself wait on that write.

## Worked example: sizing the feed service

Say the product has 50 million daily active users, each watching an
average of 40 short clips (15-30 seconds each) in a 20-minute session, and
sessions are spread over a 6-hour peak window with a 4x peak-to-average
multiplier.

Average feed requests per day: 50 million users times roughly 4 feed-page
fetches per session (assuming the client fetches a batch of 10 videos and
refills after every 10 watched) gives about 200 million feed-service
calls a day. Average QPS is 200 million divided by 86,400 seconds, or
roughly 2,300 requests per second; applying the 4x peak multiplier puts
peak feed-service load around 9,000 requests per second.

Compare that to view/watch-time events: 50 million times 40 equals 2
billion watch events a day, versus maybe 50-100 million total video plays
a day on a long-form platform at similar DAU. That 10-20x gap in
event volume is the real capacity difference a short-video feed
introduces — it's not the video bytes stored or transcoded, it's the
sheer rate of small events and feed-ranking calls the backend has to
absorb.

<Callout type="interview">
  A common mistake in this exercise is spending all the deep-dive time on
  the video pipeline (transcoding ladders, CDN cache-control headers) because
  it's the part candidates rehearsed for the long-form version of this
  question. An interviewer asking specifically about a short-video feed
  wants to hear you reason about candidate generation, ranking latency
  budgets, prefetch depth, and the watch-event feedback loop — say early
  on that you're reusing the upload/CDN design from the base case so you
  can spend your limited time on what's new.
</Callout>

plan your approach, then talk through it end to end — clarify, estimate,
design, deep-dive, discuss trade-offs — before checking the rubric.
