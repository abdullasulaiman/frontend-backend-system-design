  - "Model each chat room (guild/channel) as a single owning process or shard that serializes membership, permission, and ordering decisions for that room — this avoids split-brain on 'who is currently in this room' without needing a distributed lock per message."
  - "Fan-out-on-write (push to every connected member's socket) works until a room has hundreds of thousands of members; past that, batch/rate-limit the push and let clients pull the backlog instead of trying to deliver every event to everyone instantly."

Most chat-system designs in an interview assume small groups: a 1:1 DM, or a
group chat with a few dozen members. Discord's actual production problem is
different in kind, not just degree — a single community ("guild," in
Discord's internal vocabulary) can have hundreds of thousands to over a
million members, with tens of thousands of them online and typing in the
same channel during a live event. Layer on top of that a completely
different real-time workload — voice and video calls, which cannot tolerate
the latency of "store the message, then fan it out" — and you get a system
that has to solve two very different real-time problems under one product,
at a scale where a naive fan-out design falls over immediately. That
combination — huge rooms, mixed durability requirements, and a
persistent-connection tier serving millions of simultaneous sockets — is
what makes this a distinct case study from a generic "design a chat app"
exercise.

## The two workloads: text and voice are not the same problem

It's tempting to design one real-time pipe and push both chat messages and
voice audio through it. Discord's architecture treats them as separate
systems on purpose, because their correctness requirements are opposite:

- **Text messages** must be durable and ordered. A message you send has to
  survive a server restart, show up in history for someone who joins the
  channel a week later, and appear in a consistent order for everyone
  reading it. Latency matters, but a few hundred milliseconds of delivery
  lag is invisible to users.
- **Voice/video** must be low-latency and can tolerate loss. A dropped audio
  frame from half a second ago is worthless — retransmitting it just adds
  more delay. There's no "history" to replay. What matters is getting the
  *next* frame there in tens of milliseconds, not guaranteeing every frame
  arrives.

Designing both as "send it through the same durable message queue" either
makes voice unusably laggy (waiting on a write-ahead log per audio frame) or
makes text unreliable (dropping chat messages the way you'd tolerate
dropping audio). The fix is architectural separation: a gateway/session tier
plus a durable message store for text, and an entirely separate real-time
media relay for voice that never touches the message-storage path.

## Text path: gateway, guild owner, and message store

### The persistent-connection gateway

Every connected client holds one long-lived WebSocket to a gateway server.
The gateway's job is narrow: authenticate the connection, multiplex many
users' traffic over few TCP connections to backend services, and push
events (new messages, member joins, presence changes) down to the client as
they happen. Gateways are stateless enough to scale horizontally and are
sharded by user, not by guild — a single client only ever needs one socket,
regardless of how many guilds it belongs to.

### One logical owner per guild

Membership, roles/permissions, and channel state for a given guild need a
single consistent view — two servers independently deciding "who's currently
allowed to see this channel" is how you get a permission change that only
half the system respects. Discord's approach is to make each guild owned by
one process (running on the Erlang/Elixir BEAM runtime, which is built for
exactly this: millions of cheap, isolated, message-passing processes). That
owner process holds the guild's live state in memory, applies changes
serially, and pushes deltas out to every gateway connection currently
subscribed to that guild. Sharding by guild ID means a single very large
guild always lands on one physical node's worth of that guild's ownership
logic, which is precisely the scaling limit the next section deals with.

### Durable storage for messages

The guild owner doesn't hold message history — that goes to a separate,
horizontally-scalable store (a wide-column, log-friendly database is a
natural fit, since the access pattern is almost entirely "append a message"
and "read the last N messages in a channel," rarely arbitrary updates).
Writing a message is a two-step: persist it durably first, then hand it to
the guild owner to fan out to connected members. If the fan-out step fails
or is slow, the message still exists and a client can always fetch history
on reconnect — durability doesn't depend on delivery succeeding.

## The million-member guild problem

Fan-out-on-write — push every message to every connected member instantly —
is fine for a 50-person guild. It breaks down when a guild has 500,000
members and 60,000 of them are online during a launch event, all watching
one announcements channel. A few specific techniques handle this:

- **Lazy member/presence loading.** Clients only get the full member list
  and live presence for the small slice of the guild they're actually
  looking at (the visible member sidebar), not all 500,000 members'
  statuses at once. The rest loads in small chunks on demand.
- **Coalescing presence updates.** Online/offline/status-change events are
  extremely high-churn and low-value individually. Rather than pushing each
  one immediately, they get batched over a short window (tens to low
  hundreds of milliseconds) and sent as one update, cutting event volume by
  orders of magnitude with no perceptible UX cost.
- **Read-heavy, not write-heavy scaling for the guild owner.** Since one
  guild's ownership logic lives on one node, an enormous guild's peak load
  is bounded by that node's throughput. The mitigation is keeping the
  per-event work on that node as cheap as possible (routing and small
  deltas only, never re-serializing large member lists per message) so the
  ceiling is as high as it can be before a guild needs further splitting of
  responsibilities.

## Worked example: estimating fan-out for one hot channel

Say a 200,000-member guild has 40,000 members concurrently connected during
a live event, and the announcements channel gets one message per second
during the peak. Naive fan-out-on-write means each message triggers 40,000
individual pushes — 40,000 events/second sustained just from this one
channel, before counting any other guild activity on the same gateway
fleet.

At that rate, a gateway shard handling, say, 5,000 of those connections
still has to emit 5,000 pushes per incoming message — trivial for a single
event, but it compounds fast if ten channels across different large guilds
are all this active simultaneously, or if the message rate spikes to ten
per second during a hype moment. The mitigations that keep this tractable
are the same ones described above: presence and typing-indicator traffic
(much higher churn than actual messages) get coalesced hard, and the guild
owner batches outgoing deltas per gateway shard rather than making one
round trip per member. The message-send path itself stays simple — write
once, fan out once per connected shard, not once per member — because the
gateway shard is the one that expands one event into N socket writes, not
the guild owner.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common design mistake is routing voice/video media through the same
  stateful room-owner process that handles chat message ordering. It seems
  convenient — one component "owns" the room, why not have it relay audio
  too — but it means a spike in chat activity (or a slow message-store
  write) can add latency to live voice audio, and it means the owner
  process, sized for message-ordering throughput, now also has to handle
  raw media bandwidth. Keep media on its own regional relay tier that only
  the participants in that specific call touch, with the room owner
  involved solely in call signaling (who's joining/leaving), not the media
  itself.
</Callout>

## What this case study is really testing

An interviewer using Discord as a prompt is rarely testing whether you know
Erlang. They're testing whether you instinctively separate durability
requirements (chat must persist and order correctly) from latency
requirements (voice must be fast and can lose data), whether you reach for
"shard by room, one owner per room" instead of a single global lock for
membership/ordering, and whether you notice that presence — the thing every
naive design treats like a normal event — is actually the highest-volume,
lowest-value traffic in the whole system and needs to be treated
differently from messages. If you find yourself proposing one pipeline for
everything real-time, that's the signal to stop and split the problem.
