"Design a web crawler" looks deceptively contained: fetch a page, find the
links on it, fetch those too. Most candidates can sketch that loop in thirty
seconds. What separates a strong answer from a weak one is everything that
loop doesn't show — the web has no known size, a huge share of it is
duplicate or near-duplicate content, some pages regenerate infinite new URLs
on purpose or by accident, and a naive crawler will either get stuck forever
on one site or get its IP banned by every site it touches on day one.

This lesson scopes the problem the way you should in the first ten minutes
of the interview: what the crawler actually needs to do, how big "big" is,
and the shape of a design that can hold that scale. The next lesson,
Politeness, Deduplication, and Scaling, goes deep on the three hardest
sub-problems this design surfaces. Here, the goal is a defensible skeleton.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Get these on the table before designing anything:

- **Seed and discover.** Start from a set of seed URLs, fetch each page,
  extract outbound links, and add newly discovered URLs back into the work
  queue. This is the core loop.
- **Store crawled content.** Downstream systems (a search index, a data
  pipeline, an archive) need the fetched HTML and extracted metadata, not
  just a list of visited URLs.
- **Avoid re-fetching the same page repeatedly.** A URL, and ideally a
  piece of content, should not be fetched over and over with no benefit.
- **Re-crawl over time.** Pages change. A one-shot crawl is rarely the
  actual requirement — most real systems need pages refreshed on some
  cadence, faster for pages that change often (news homepages) and slower
  for pages that rarely do (a company's "About" page).

Just as important is naming what's out of scope, out loud: rendering
JavaScript-heavy single-page apps, crawling behind logins, and full
duplicate-content clustering across the entire corpus are all real problems
a production crawler eventually faces, but each is its own design
discussion. For this module, assume a broad crawl of public, mostly-static
HTML pages, refreshed periodically.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Scalability.** The design must handle billions of pages and keep
  growing without a redesign — this pushes toward a distributed,
  horizontally scalable architecture from the start, not a single machine
  with a queue.
- **Politeness.** The crawler must not overwhelm any single web server. This
  is a hard constraint, not a nice-to-have — violate it and your crawler
  gets IP-blocked, which ends the crawl for that host entirely. (Full
  treatment in the next lesson.)
- **Robustness.** The open web is adversarial by default: malformed HTML,
  redirect loops, multi-gigabyte files, servers that hang instead of
  responding, and deliberately generated infinite URL spaces (crawler
  traps). The design must bound resource use per host and per URL rather
  than trusting the web to behave.
- **Freshness over completeness.** It's usually better to have slightly
  stale coverage of the whole web than perfectly fresh coverage of a
  fraction of it — freshness budget should be spent where content actually
  changes.
- **Extensibility.** New content types, new extraction rules, or new
  priority signals should be pluggable without rewriting the fetch loop.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say the scope out loud early: "I'll assume a broad crawl of public HTML
  pages at web scale, with periodic re-crawling for freshness — not
  JS-rendered pages or authenticated content. Does that match what you had
  in mind?" This single sentence usually saves you from designing the wrong
  system for fifteen minutes.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Concrete numbers turn "a big crawler" into a design you can defend:

- Target: crawl 1 billion pages per month.
- Average fetched page size (HTML only): 500 KB.
- Average number of outbound links extracted per page: 20.
- Re-crawl policy: most pages re-crawled roughly every 30 days, with a
  smaller high-priority set (news, frequently updated pages) re-crawled
  daily.

From the monthly target, derive throughput:

- 1,000,000,000 pages per month divided by roughly 2,600,000 seconds in a
  30-day month gives about 385 pages/sec average sustained fetch rate.
- Peak provisioning at a 2-3x multiplier for headroom and re-crawl bursts:
  roughly 1,000 pages/sec at peak.
- Content storage: 1,000,000,000 pages multiplied by 500 KB is about
  500 TB per crawl cycle. Retaining a few months of history for
  freshness comparison pushes this into low-petabyte territory over time.
- URL/metadata volume: 1 billion crawled pages multiplied by 20 links each
  is 20 billion discovered URL references per month — most pointing at
  URLs already known, which is exactly why a fast "have we seen this
  before" check matters more than raw fetch speed.

<Callout type="tip">
  State the re-fetch assumption explicitly — it's the number an
  interviewer is most likely to push on. "Most pages re-crawled every 30
  days, hot pages daily" is a specific, defensible policy; "we crawl
  everything as often as possible" isn't a policy at all.
</Callout>

## Step 3: The high-level shape of the design

Break the single "fetch, extract, repeat" loop into components that scale
and fail independently:

1. **URL frontier** — the queue of URLs waiting to be fetched. Not a plain
   FIFO: it needs to decide ordering (priority, freshness) and pacing per
   host, which is the subject of the next lesson.
2. **Fetcher pool** — a horizontally scaled set of workers that pull a URL
   from the frontier, resolve DNS, open a connection, and download the
   page. Since each fetch is dominated by network round-trip time, not CPU,
   a single machine can run many fetches concurrently.
3. **URL filter** — before a URL ever reaches the frontier, check it against
   robots.txt rules, URL-scheme/extension allowlists (skip `.zip`, `.mp4`,
   etc. if out of scope), and a "seen before" store, so obviously
   unwanted or duplicate work never enters the queue.
4. **Parser and link extractor** — takes downloaded HTML, extracts the
   text/metadata for storage and the outbound links for re-queueing.
5. **Content store** — durable storage (object storage, not a relational
   database) for the fetched page bodies, keyed by URL or content hash.
6. **URL/metadata store** — a separate, smaller-record store tracking each
   URL's crawl state (queued, fetched, last-crawled time, content hash) —
   this is what the "seen before" check in the filter queries.

Notice the loop: extracted links flow back through the filter before
re-entering the frontier, so a page that's already fully crawled, or a URL
that robots.txt disallows, never gets re-queued. That filter step is doing
most of the work that keeps this from turning into an infinite, wasteful
crawl.

## Worked example: sizing the fetcher pool

Take the 1,000 pages/sec peak target from Step 2. If a single fetch (DNS
lookup, connection, download, TLS where needed) takes an average of 2
seconds end-to-end, and each fetcher worker can hold, say, 200 connections
open concurrently using asynchronous I/O, one worker sustains roughly
200 connections divided by 2 seconds per fetch, or 100 pages/sec.

To hit 1,000 pages/sec peak, you need roughly 1,000 divided by 100, or
about 10 fetcher workers running concurrently — a small, horizontally
scalable pool, not hundreds of machines. The bottleneck this reveals isn't
raw fetch capacity; it's making sure the frontier can hand out 1,000
politeness-compliant URLs per second without starving any single host or
handing out URLs faster than the filter and metadata store can validate
them. That hand-off — frontier throughput under politeness constraints — is
exactly where the next lesson picks up.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is designing the frontier as a single shared FIFO queue.
  Under real crawl conditions, a handful of large, highly-linked sites (a
  major news outlet, a popular forum) dominate the queue with thousands of
  their own URLs, so fetchers end up hammering those few hosts back-to-back
  while the rest of the crawl starves. Any workable frontier design needs
  per-host queuing or throttling built in from the start, not bolted on
  after the first IP ban.
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The skeleton here — frontier, filter, fetcher pool, parser, and two
separate stores — is what makes the harder problems tractable. The next
lesson takes each weak point in this design and hardens it: how the
frontier enforces per-host politeness without starving the rest of the
crawl, how to detect duplicate and near-duplicate content cheaply at
billions-of-pages scale, and how to shard and scale each component as the
crawl grows past a single data center's worth of fetchers.
