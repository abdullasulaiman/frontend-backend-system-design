"Design Instagram" (or any photo/video sharing app) is a favorite interview
prompt because it looks like a simpler cousin of "Design Twitter" — post
something, show it to followers — but it hides a second axis of scale that
text-only feeds don't have: every post carries real weight in bytes. A
tweet is a few hundred bytes; a single Instagram photo, once you account
for the thumbnail, feed-resolution, and full-resolution renditions you
generate for it, is several megabytes, and a short video is tens of
megabytes across its transcoded copies. Get the requirements phase wrong
here and you'll spend the rest of the interview reasoning about query
counts while ignoring the fact that media storage and bandwidth are what
actually strain the system.

This lesson exists to pin down scope and numbers before any of that gets
designed. The storage architecture, CDN strategy, and feed design in the
rest of this module are direct responses to the upload:read ratio, the
photo:video mix, and the byte volumes established here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

State scope out loud rather than assuming the whole product is in play. A
reasonable core for an interview-sized version:

- **Upload a post**: one or more photos, or a short video, with an
  optional caption, tagged users, and location.
- **Follow**: one user follows another; following is asymmetric, like
  Twitter, not a mutual "friend" relationship.
- **Home feed**: a feed of posts from accounts a user follows, blending
  recency with some ranking signal.
- **Like and comment**: lightweight engagement actions attached to a post.
- **Profile grid**: the list of posts a single account has published.
- **Stories**: short-lived posts that disappear after 24 hours, shown in
  a separate strip above the feed.

Explicitly defer, and say so out loud: direct messages, an explore/search
ranking system, shopping tags, live video, and augmented-reality filters
are each their own design problem. Naming them and moving on is a strong
signal in an interview — this module itself defers stories' full design to
its own lesson later on, rather than trying to design everything on lesson
one.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Read-heavy, but not purely a request-count problem.** Feeds are
  loaded far more often than posts are created, but unlike a text feed,
  each read also pulls back real media bytes — the system has to be fast
  on both requests per second *and* bandwidth.
- **Uploaded media must be durable once acknowledged.** Losing a user's
  photo or video after confirming the upload succeeded is a hard failure
  users notice immediately and never forgive.
- **Engagement counters and feed ranking can be eventually consistent.** A
  like count that's off by a handful for a few seconds, or a feed that
  reorders slightly between two loads, is invisible to almost everyone.
- **Feed freshness matters but isn't chat-speed.** A new post from a
  followed account should reach a feed within roughly a minute, not
  instantly — this is a looser latency budget than a messaging app, but
  tighter than a batch report.
- **Highly skewed creator graph.** A small number of accounts (creators,
  brands, celebrities) hold a hugely disproportionate share of followers —
  the same "celebrity problem" from the Twitter case study shows up here
  and has to be named now, even though the feed-generation lesson is where
  it gets solved.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Rough, stated assumptions beat silence. A workable set of numbers for a
large photo/video sharing platform:

- 500 million daily active users (DAU).
- Each DAU opens their feed about 10 times per day, loading roughly 20
  posts per open.
- 1 in 200 DAU uploads at least one post on a given day; of those uploads,
  9 in 10 are photos and 1 in 10 is a short video.
- Each DAU performs about 10 likes and 1 comment per day.
- An uploaded photo, once you store the thumbnail, feed-resolution, and
  full-resolution copies, averages about 3 MB total. An uploaded video,
  once transcoded into two or three renditions for different network
  conditions, averages about 50 MB total.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say the numbers out loud before computing anything: "Let's assume 500
  million DAU, about 20 posts per feed load, 10 loads a day per user, and
  roughly a 90/10 photo-to-video upload mix — does that match the scale
  you had in mind?" That turns the estimate into a checkpoint with the
  interviewer instead of a silent calculation.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive traffic, bandwidth, and storage numbers

Work through uploads, feed reads, engagement, and storage separately —
each stresses a different part of the system, and media size changes the
answer in a way pure request counting would miss.

### Upload throughput

- Daily uploads: 500,000,000 divided by 200 is 2,500,000 uploads/day —
  about 2,250,000 photos and 250,000 videos.
- Writes per second (average): 2,500,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  29/sec — small even with a generous 5x peak multiplier (roughly
  145/sec at peak).

### Feed read throughput

- Daily feed opens: 500,000,000 multiplied by 10 is 5,000,000,000
  opens/day.
- Posts served: 5 billion multiplied by 20 is 100 billion post-views/day.
- Opens per second (average): 5,000,000,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  58,000/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 290,000/sec at peak.
- That's a read:write ratio of roughly **2,000:1** on requests alone, and
  the gap widens further once you count individual post-views instead of
  requests.

### Engagement throughput

- Daily engagement actions: 500,000,000 multiplied by 11 (10 likes plus 1
  comment) is 5.5 billion actions/day.
- Actions per second (average): 5.5 billion divided by 86,400 is about
  64,000/sec; at a 5x peak multiplier, roughly 320,000/sec at peak.
- Each action is a cheap counter increment or small row insert, not a
  media read — a different, lighter kind of load than serving feeds.

### Storage

- Daily photo storage: 2,250,000 uploads multiplied by 3 MB is about
  6.75 TB/day.
- Daily video storage: 250,000 uploads multiplied by 50 MB is about
  12.5 TB/day.
- Combined media storage is roughly 19.25 TB/day, or about 7 PB/year —
  and video, despite being only 1 in 10 uploads, contributes nearly
  twice the daily bytes that photos do.

The contrast is the key takeaway: **request-count math alone (uploads,
feed opens, likes) tells you QPS, but the byte volume of the media itself
is what actually dictates the storage and CDN architecture.**

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **Media belongs in object storage, not a database, and behind a CDN,
   not served from origin.** At 290,000 feed-assembly requests/sec, each
   pulling back roughly 20 posts' worth of media references, origin
   servers cannot be in the hot path for every image byte — the next
   lesson builds around blob storage plus edge caching.
2. **Uploads justify asynchronous processing.** At only ~145 writes/sec
   peak, there's room to accept an upload, acknowledge it immediately, and
   do thumbnailing/transcoding as background work rather than blocking the
   user on it.
3. **Feed assembly should favor precomputation for typical accounts**,
   the same conclusion as the Twitter case study, for the same reason: a
   live query across every followed account on every one of 290,000
   feed-opens/sec is too expensive to run synchronously.
4. **The creator/follower skew breaks a uniform fan-out approach** — an
   account with tens of millions of followers can't be pushed into every
   follower's precomputed feed the instant it posts. This constraint is
   named here and solved in the feed-generation lesson.
5. **Storage and bandwidth capacity planning should be driven by video
   megabytes, not post counts** — a 10% share of uploads contributing
   nearly double the daily storage bytes of the other 90% is the number to
   defend the media pipeline's design with.

## Worked example: sanity-checking a viral video's bandwidth

Suppose one video post goes viral and is viewed 10,000,000 times in a
single day. Assume an average of 2 MB actually gets served per view once
you account for adaptive bitrate matching each viewer's connection and
device.

Bytes served for that one video: 10,000,000 multiplied by 2 MB is
20,000,000 MB, or about 20 TB in a single day — more than the entire
platform's total video *upload* storage added system-wide that same day
(about 12.5 TB, from Step 3). One popular post can, in serving bandwidth
alone, outweigh everything newly uploaded that day.

That gap is the concrete argument for serving reads from a CDN cache
close to the viewer instead of re-fetching from origin storage on every
view: origin storage durably holds the bytes once; it was never meant to
answer 10 million individual read requests for the same file.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is estimating a photo/video sharing app the same way
  you'd estimate a text feed: count requests per second, size a database,
  and stop. That ignores the axis that actually defines this system —
  media byte volume. A design that nails request-per-second capacity but
  serves every photo view from a single origin store, or stores every
  video rendition in a row-oriented database, will fall over on bandwidth
  and storage engineering long before it hits a QPS ceiling. Always
  estimate bytes-per-upload and bytes-served-per-view alongside requests.
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The shape derived here — a two-orders-of-magnitude read:write ratio on
requests, a larger gap once you weigh in bytes, video outweighing photos
in storage despite being the minority of uploads, and a skewed creator
graph — is exactly what the next lesson's high-level design responds to:
an ingestion path that hands off to asynchronous processing, dedicated
media storage fronted by a CDN, and a feed service built to serve
precomputed results fast.
