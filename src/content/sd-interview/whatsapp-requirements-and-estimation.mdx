Most system design prompts ask you to serve reads fast or rank content well.
"Design WhatsApp" asks something different: don't lose anyone's message,
ever, even though the recipient's phone might be off, out of signal, or
sitting in airplane mode for a day. A dropped social media post is a bug
users shrug off. A dropped or duplicated chat message is a bug users notice
immediately and stop trusting the product over. That single constraint —
guaranteed, ordered, at-least-once delivery over an unreliable network,
at global scale — is what should drive every decision in this module, and
it starts with scoping the problem honestly before drawing any boxes.

This lesson does that scoping. The high-level design, the delivery and
ordering guarantees, presence/groups, and media/encryption lessons that
follow all build on the numbers and constraints you establish here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Say the scope out loud and let the interviewer redirect rather than
silently guessing:

- **Send and receive 1:1 messages.** Text messages between two users,
  delivered as close to real time as the network allows.
- **Group messaging.** A message sent to a group reaches every member,
  typically tens of members, occasionally into the hundreds.
- **Delivery and read receipts.** Sender sees when a message reached the
  recipient's device (delivered) and when it was opened (read).
- **Presence.** Online/offline status and a "last seen" timestamp.
- **Media sharing.** Photos, short videos, and voice notes attached to a
  message, not just text.
- **Offline delivery via push.** If a recipient's app isn't running, a
  push notification wakes it and the message is waiting once it opens.

Explicitly defer: voice/video calling, status/stories, a business
messaging API, and multi-device backup/restore mechanics. Each is a real
feature of a real chat app, but pulling all of them into one session
leaves no time to go deep on any single one. (Multi-device *sync* — the
same account logged in on a phone and a laptop at once — does come up
later in this module, but it's a delivery-fan-out concern, not something
to design from scratch in this lesson.)

### Non-functional requirements

- **Low latency when both parties are online.** A message should land on
  the recipient's screen within a second or two, not tens of seconds.
- **Durability over immediacy.** If the recipient is offline, the message
  must be held and delivered later — it must never simply vanish because
  a device wasn't reachable at send time.
- **Per-conversation ordering.** Messages within a single 1:1 or group
  chat must appear in the order they were sent, even if they take
  different network paths or hit different servers.
- **At-least-once delivery with client-side dedup.** Over a lossy mobile
  network, retries are unavoidable — the honest guarantee is "delivered
  one or more times," with a client-generated message ID used to drop
  duplicates on arrival, not a false promise of exactly-once delivery.
- **End-to-end encryption constrains the architecture, not just the
  wire.** If message content is encrypted so only the endpoints can read
  it, the server can route and store ciphertext but cannot search
  content, scan for spam, or generate a preview server-side. That
  assumption has to be baked in from this lesson forward, not patched in
  later.
- **Massive connection concurrency, not just request throughput.** Every
  online user holds a persistent connection waiting for messages. The
  gateway tier has to hold hundreds of millions of simultaneous
  connections, which is a fundamentally different scaling problem than a
  stateless web tier handling short-lived HTTP requests.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

State assumptions and invite correction:

- 1 billion monthly active users (MAU); 600 million daily active users
  (DAU) — chat apps have unusually high daily stickiness compared to
  browsing apps.
- Each DAU sends an average of 40 messages a day (a mix of 1:1 and group
  chats, text and media combined).
- 25% of sent messages go to a group; the remaining 75% are 1:1. Average
  group size is 8 members (so a group message fans out to 7 other
  recipients).
- 15% of sent messages carry media (photo, short video, or voice note)
  averaging 150 KB; the rest are plain text averaging 100 bytes.
- At global peak, roughly 25% of DAU are connected simultaneously (peak
  overlap across time zones), rather than everyone online at once.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say it plainly: "I'll assume 600 million daily active users sending 40
  messages a day each, a quarter of those to groups averaging 8 members,
  and roughly 150 million simultaneous connections at peak — does that
  match the scale you have in mind?" Anchoring on DAU and messages-per-user,
  not MAU, keeps every later number defensible.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive the numbers

### Messages sent vs. messages delivered

- Total messages sent per day: 600,000,000 users times 40 messages is
  24,000,000,000 (24 billion) sent per day.
- Average sent QPS: 24,000,000,000 divided by 86,400 seconds is about
  278,000 sends/sec. Apply a 3x peak multiplier (chat traffic clusters
  around evenings and regional peaks, though less spiky than, say,
  live-event traffic): roughly 833,000 sends/sec at peak.
- Deliveries are a different, larger number because of group fan-out:
  75% of the 24 billion (18 billion) are 1:1, each producing exactly one
  delivery. The remaining 25% (6 billion) are group messages, each
  producing 7 deliveries (one per other member): 6 billion times 7 is
  42 billion. Total deliveries: 18 billion plus 42 billion is 60 billion
  deliveries/day — 2.5x the send count, purely from group fan-out.
- Average delivery QPS: 60,000,000,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  694,000 deliveries/sec, roughly 2.08 million/sec at the same 3x peak.

### Connection concurrency

- 25% of 600 million DAU online at once is about 150 million concurrent
  persistent connections at peak. This is the number that sizes the
  gateway/connection tier — it has nothing to do with message QPS and
  everything to do with how many idle sockets a fleet must hold open and
  route to simultaneously.

### Storage: text is trivial, media is not

- Text: 24 billion messages/day at roughly 100 bytes is about 2.4 TB/day
  of raw text — under a petabyte a year even before compression, easily
  handled by an ordinary distributed store.
- Media: 15% of 24 billion is 3.6 billion media messages/day, averaging
  150 KB each, which is 3,600,000,000 times 150,000 bytes, or about
  540 TB/day. That's over 200x the text volume from a fifth as many
  messages — media capacity, not text, is the real storage number to
  size for, and it's why media is typically stored once in object storage
  and referenced by a link rather than duplicated per recipient.

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **The connection tier is its own scaling problem.** 150 million
   concurrent persistent connections at peak requires a horizontally
   sharded gateway fleet with per-user sticky routing, distinct from the
   stateless services handling message processing.
2. **Fan-out multiplies group traffic, and the design must account for
   it explicitly.** 2.5x delivery amplification from a modest 25%
   group-message share means group chats deserve deliberate fan-out
   handling (the next lesson covers this), not an assumption that a
   group message is "one write."
3. **Media needs a separate storage and delivery path from text.** At
   roughly 200x the byte volume of text despite being a fifth of message
   count, media belongs in object storage with a CDN in front of it, not
   inline in the same store as message records.
4. **Encryption is a day-one architectural constraint.** Because message
   bodies are unreadable to the server, every server-side feature —
   ordering, storage, delivery confirmation, even push notification
   payloads — has to work off metadata and ciphertext, never plaintext
   content.

## Worked example: sanity-checking a follow-up

Suppose the interviewer asks: "What if average group size grows from 8 to
20?" Re-derive rather than guess. Group fan-out per message goes from 7
recipients to 19. Group messages are still 25% of the 24 billion sent
(6 billion), so group deliveries go from 6 billion times 7 (42 billion) to
6 billion times 19 (114 billion) — a jump of 72 billion deliveries/day.
Total deliveries rise from 60 billion to 18 billion (unchanged 1:1) plus
114 billion (new group figure), or 132 billion/day — more than double.
Meanwhile sent QPS, connection concurrency, and text/media storage per
message are completely untouched by this change, since none of them
depend on group size. Naming exactly which number moves — and which don't
— is what separates a candidate reasoning about the system from one just
re-running a formula.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is quoting the *sent* message rate as if it were the
  system's real load. It isn't — group fan-out means the delivery rate
  (what the fan-out and push paths actually have to handle) can be several
  times the send rate, and that gap grows further if average group size is
  larger than assumed. A related mistake: sizing storage off text alone
  and forgetting media, which in this estimate outweighs text by two
  orders of magnitude despite being a small fraction of message count.
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The three constraints established here — a connection tier sized for
concurrency rather than throughput, a delivery pipeline that has to
tolerate a multi-times fan-out multiplier from groups, and an encrypted
message body that the server can route but never read — are exactly the
shape of the architecture in the next lesson. They're also why ordering
and delivery guarantees, presence and group fan-out, and media/encryption
each get their own dedicated lesson: each is a distinct hard problem
hiding behind the same chat bubble UI.
