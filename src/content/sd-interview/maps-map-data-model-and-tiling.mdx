Every interviewer designing a mapping product eventually asks some version
of: "OK, but what does the data actually look like, and how does the client
get a smooth, zoomable map without downloading the whole planet?" This is
the question this lesson answers. The high-level design gives you boxes
labeled "tile service" and "map storage" — this lesson is what's actually
inside those boxes: how the physical world gets turned into a graph for
routing and a pyramid of pre-rendered tiles for rendering, and why those are
two genuinely different data models serving two genuinely different jobs.

Get this wrong in an interview and it shows immediately: candidates who
treat "map data" as one blob (say, "we store the map in a database and
query it") can't explain why panning a map at street level doesn't hit a
database at all, or why routing doesn't fetch tiles. Separating the two
models up front makes both halves of the design fall out cleanly.

## Two data models, not one

Google Maps solves two different problems with the same underlying
geography, and each needs its own representation:

1. **"Show me what's here"** — rendering roads, buildings, water, and
   points of interest as you pan and zoom. This is a **rendering** problem,
   solved with **tiles**.
2. **"Get me from A to B"** — computing a path and an ETA. This is a
   **graph traversal** problem, solved with a **road network graph**.

They share the same source data (surveyed roads, building footprints) but
diverge completely in how that data is stored and accessed. Conflating them
is the single most common design mistake for this problem — for example,
trying to compute a route by querying rendered tiles, or trying to render
a map by walking a routing graph. Keep them as two pipelines from the same
raw data.

## The road network graph

For routing, represent the world as a weighted directed graph:

- **Nodes**: intersections, dead ends, or synthetic waypoints inserted
  along a long stretch of road so no single edge is unreasonably long.
- **Edges**: a directed road segment between two nodes, carrying attributes
  like length, speed limit, road class (highway vs. residential), one-way
  flag, and turn restrictions at its endpoints.

This graph is what the routing service (covered in the next lesson)
searches over — Dijkstra-style or A*-style algorithms walk edges from a
source node to a destination node. It's a relatively compact,
mostly-static structure: from the requirements lesson's estimate of roughly
500 million road segments worldwide, storing an edge as source node,
destination node, and a handful of numeric attributes fits in well under a
kilobyte per edge — a few hundred gigabytes total, small enough to keep
partitioned in memory across a fleet of routing servers rather than hitting
disk per lookup.

Crucially, this graph is edited rarely (a new road opens; a street is
closed) and read constantly, so it's built and refreshed as a batch
pipeline, then pushed out to routing servers as an immutable snapshot —
not updated row-by-row in a live transactional database.

## Tiles: pre-rendering the world into a quadtree

Rendering a world map on demand — running a rasterizer over raw geometry
every time someone pans — would be far too slow and far too repetitive,
since a huge number of users are looking at overlapping areas (downtown San
Francisco gets viewed far more than mid-ocean). The fix: **pre-render the
map once, offline, into small fixed-size images called tiles, and serve
those tiles from cache.**

A tile is addressed by three integers: `(zoom, x, y)`.

- **Zoom** controls level of detail, typically ranging from 0 (the whole
  world in one tile) to 20+ (individual buildings and street labels).
- **x, y** locate the tile within that zoom level's grid, using a
  projection (commonly Web Mercator) that maps latitude/longitude onto a
  flat square grid.

At zoom 0 there's exactly one tile covering the whole world. At zoom 1
that tile splits into a 2x2 grid — 4 tiles. At zoom `n`, there are `4^n`
tiles total, because each tile at zoom `n` splits into exactly four
children at zoom `n+1`. This nesting structure is a **quadtree**: tile
`(z, x, y)` is the parent of `(z+1, 2x, 2y)`, `(z+1, 2x+1, 2y)`,
`(z+1, 2x, 2y+1)`, and `(z+1, 2x+1, 2y+1)`.

The client doesn't know or care about this hierarchy explicitly — it just
computes, from the current viewport's center and zoom level, which
`(zoom, x, y)` tiles are visible, and requests exactly those, typically
prefetching one ring of neighboring tiles so panning feels instant.

### Raster vs. vector tiles

- **Raster tiles** are pre-drawn images (PNG/JPEG/WebP) — simple to serve
  and cache, but every style change (dark mode, a different label
  language, highlighting a route) needs a whole new set of tiles rendered
  server-side.
- **Vector tiles** ship compact encoded geometry (roads as line strings,
  buildings as polygons, labeled with metadata) plus a client-side style
  sheet. The client renders the tile with GPU-accelerated code (this is how
  modern Google Maps and Mapbox work). Vector tiles are usually smaller on
  the wire, support smooth rotation and zoom animation, and let you restyle
  the whole map (dark mode, traffic overlay, walking vs. driving theme)
  without re-fetching a single byte of geometry — only the style sheet
  changes.

For an interview answer, vector tiles are the stronger default to propose:
they cost more client-side rendering work but save enormous server-side
rendering and storage cost, and they're what the leading real products
actually ship.

### Sparse rendering by density

You don't pre-render every zoom level everywhere. Mid-ocean has no reason
to exist past zoom 6 or so; downtown Tokyo needs tiles all the way to
zoom 20. Rendering is driven by data density and, in practice, actual
traffic — tiles for popular areas get generated (and refreshed on data
changes) eagerly, while tiles for sparse regions can be generated lazily on
first request and then cached, since nobody will ask for that ocean tile at
zoom 19 more than once in a blue moon.

## Keeping tiles static: where does traffic go?

Tiles are ideal cache candidates specifically because road geometry and
buildings barely change. Live traffic color-coding, incidents, and a
user's own route overlay must **not** be baked into the tile itself, or
you'd have to re-render and invalidate cache entries constantly for data
that changes every few minutes. Instead:

- The base tile (roads, buildings, labels) is served from cache, keyed only
  by `(zoom, x, y)` and a data version.
- Traffic, incidents, and the active route are separate data layers,
  fetched independently and drawn on top by the client — usually as a thin
  vector overlay keyed by the same tile coordinates, refreshed on a much
  shorter interval (say, every 60-120 seconds) than the base tile.

This separation is what lets the tile layer stay a near-pure CDN caching
problem while the traffic layer stays a fast-changing, short-TTL problem —
mixing them would force the whole tile to inherit traffic's freshness
requirements.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is proposing "store the map in a relational database and
  query rows for the current viewport" as the rendering path. At Google
  Maps scale that means a database query, a server-side render, and a
  network round trip on every pan — far too slow, and it throws away the
  fact that millions of users are requesting the same handful of popular
  tiles. Pre-render (or lazily render-and-cache) fixed tiles, keyed by
  `(zoom, x, y)`, and let a CDN absorb the read traffic. A database (or
  object store) sits behind the cache purely as the source of truth for
  tiles that haven't been rendered yet or need a refresh.
</Callout>

## Worked example: how many tiles does one pan touch?

Say a phone screen shows roughly 400x800 logical pixels, and tiles are
256x256 pixels. A single viewport at any given zoom level covers about
`(400/256) * (800/256)`, roughly 5 tiles, though in practice you round up
and fetch a slightly larger grid — say a 3x4 grid, 12 tiles — so a small
pan doesn't cause a visible gap while the newly-revealed edge tile loads.

Now walk the deeper question an interviewer likes to ask: "How much does
zooming in one level change what you fetch?" Zooming from level `n` to
level `n+1` doesn't just add tiles — the whole visible area is now covered
by four times as many tiles at the new zoom level, because each old tile's
area is exactly covered by its four quadtree children. The client discards
the old zoom level's tiles (or keeps them briefly as a low-res placeholder
while the new ones load — the "blurry then sharp" effect you've seen in
every map app) and requests roughly 4x as many tiles at the new zoom to
cover the same physical area. This is exactly why tile storage is dominated
by the deepest zoom levels: from the requirements lesson's estimate of
around 5 billion tiles pre-rendered at 256 KB each (about 1.2 PB total),
the overwhelming majority of that count sits in the last few zoom levels,
since each one has roughly 4x the tiles of the level above it.
