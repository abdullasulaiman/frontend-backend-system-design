The previous lesson in this module walked through designing a newsfeed
system piece by piece: requirements, data model, fan-out strategy, and
ranking. That's how you *learn* the material. It is not how an interview
actually goes — in a real 45-minute session nobody hands you the pieces in
order, nobody tells you when to move from requirements to architecture, and
nobody stops you from burning 20 minutes on a diagram that doesn't matter.
This lesson is that pressure test: one prompt, one clock, one rubric, no
scaffolding.

Treat it like the real thing. Set a timer, talk out loud or write your
reasoning as you go, and only look at the rubric after you've made an
honest attempt. The value of a mock interview evaporates if you peek early.

## Why a dedicated mock-interview pass matters

Knowing the concepts and performing them under time pressure are different
skills. A newsfeed prompt is popular specifically because it rewards
structure: it has enough moving parts (post storage, follow graph, fan-out,
ranking, delivery) that a candidate who dives straight into whiteboarding
microservices without first agreeing scope with the interviewer will run
out of time before reaching the parts that actually differentiate a strong
answer — the fan-out trade-off and the ranking pipeline.

The goal of this run-through is to practice the *shape* of a good answer,
not just recall the facts: clarify, size, design broad-to-narrow, deep-dive
on the one or two hardest problems, then discuss trade-offs and failure
modes. That shape transfers to almost every system design prompt you'll
get, not just this one.

## Suggested time budget

A realistic 45-minute slot:

- **5 min** — clarify requirements: ranked or chronological feed, real-time
  push or pull-on-refresh, whether "celebrity" accounts with huge follower
  counts are in scope, read-heavy vs. write-heavy assumption.
- **5 min** — back-of-envelope estimate: daily active users, posts per day,
  average follower count, read:write ratio.
- **15 min** — high-level architecture: post service, follow-graph service,
  fan-out/feed-assembly path, ranking service, delivery to the client.
- **12 min** — deep dive on the fan-out strategy for high-follower accounts
  and how ranking fits into feed assembly — this is where most candidates
  either shine or stall.
- **remaining time** — trade-offs, consistency model, and at least one
  failure mode (a fan-out worker falling behind, a celebrity post going
  viral, a follow-graph write racing a feed read).

## The core architectural decision: where does fan-out happen?

Almost every newsfeed design question converges on the same fork in the
road, so it's worth having a crisp mental model of it before you sit the
real interview.

**Fan-out-on-write** (push model): when a user posts, a worker immediately
writes that post's ID into the precomputed feed list of every follower.
Reading a feed is then a cheap lookup of an already-assembled list. This is
fast for readers but the write cost scales with follower count — a post
from an account with 10 million followers means 10 million writes, and
those writes have to land before the post is "in" everyone's feed.

**Fan-out-on-read** (pull model): nothing happens at post time beyond
saving the post itself. When a user opens their feed, the service fetches
recent posts from everyone they follow and merges them on the fly. Writes
stay cheap and constant-cost regardless of follower count, but every feed
read now does real work proportional to how many people that user follows.

Production systems almost always use a **hybrid**: fan-out-on-write for
the common case (accounts with a normal follower count), and
fan-out-on-read for a small set of very-high-follower accounts, merging
the pulled results into the recipient's read path at request time. Naming
this hybrid unprompted, and explaining *why* the split exists, is one of
the highest-signal things you can do in this exercise.

A few points worth stating explicitly if the interviewer probes this
diagram:

- **Ranking happens after fan-out, not instead of it.** Fan-out decides
  *which posts are candidates*; ranking decides *what order they appear
  in*. Conflating the two into one step is a common design smell.
- **The fan-out worker is asynchronous and can lag.** A design that
  requires fan-out to complete synchronously before the post "exists"
  couples post latency to follower count, which defeats the point of the
  push model.
- **Feed staleness of a few seconds is an acceptable trade**, but losing
  the underlying post or a follow-graph edge is not. Say this distinction
  out loud — it shows you understand which parts of the system tolerate
  eventual consistency and which don't.

## Worked example: sizing the fan-out problem

Say the product has 100 million daily active users, posting an average of
2 posts per user per day, with an average follower count of 300. That's
200 million posts a day generating, at 300 followers each, roughly 60
billion feed-list writes a day from fan-out-on-write alone — about 700,000
writes per second sustained, well before accounting for peak multipliers.

Now isolate the tail: if 0.01% of those 100 million users are
"celebrity"-tier accounts averaging 5 million followers each, that's
10,000 accounts. Even one post per day from each of those, fanned out
naively, adds 10,000 times 5 million, or 50 billion additional writes a
day — comparable to the entire rest of the platform's fan-out volume from
a tiny sliver of accounts. That's the concrete number that justifies
routing celebrity accounts to a fan-out-on-read path instead: without the
split, a handful of accounts would dominate total write load.

<Callout type="interview">
  A common mistake in this exercise is treating "how do we scale the
  database" as the hard problem and racing to talk about sharding and
  replication. The database layer is largely a solved, standard problem
  once you've chosen a data model — the interviewer already expects you
  know how to shard a table. The actual hard problem, and where this
  prompt wants your attention, is the fan-out strategy and how ranking
  interacts with it. Spend your deep-dive minutes there, not on generic
  database scaling advice you could give for any system.
</Callout>

## Run the mock interview

Set a timer for about 40-45 minutes. Read the prompt once, jot a quick
plan, then talk through it end to end: clarify, estimate, design,
deep-dive, discuss trade-offs. Only open the rubric once you've finished
or the clock runs out.
