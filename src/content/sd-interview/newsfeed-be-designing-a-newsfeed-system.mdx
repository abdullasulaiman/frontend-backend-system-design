"Design a newsfeed" is one of the most common system design prompts precisely because it's shallow to describe and deep to actually build. Everyone has used one — Instagram's home feed, LinkedIn's feed, a Facebook timeline — so the interviewer doesn't need to explain the product. What they're actually testing is whether you can turn "show me posts from people I follow, newest and most relevant first" into a system that survives a user with ten million followers, a viewer who follows five thousand accounts, and a read volume that dwarfs writes by orders of magnitude.

This lesson builds a complete newsfeed backend from scratch: requirements and scale, data model, the feed-generation strategy that dominates the design conversation, a basic ranking layer, and where the system actually breaks under load. The companion mock-interview lesson in this module gives you a timed prompt to practice the same ground live.

## Clarifying requirements

Before drawing boxes, pin down what "newsfeed" means for this system, out loud, in the interview:

**Functional requirements**
- Users can create posts (text, optionally an image or video attachment).
- Users can follow other users (one-directional follow, not mutual friendship — this changes the fan-out math, so ask which model the interviewer wants).
- A user can request their feed: a paginated, ranked list of posts from people they follow.
- Users can like and comment on posts, and those signals should influence ranking.

**Non-functional requirements**
- Read-heavy: feed reads happen far more often than posts are created — assume a read-to-write ratio of 100:1 or higher.
- Low read latency: a feed load should return in well under a second.
- High availability over strict consistency: a slightly stale feed is fine; a feed endpoint that's down is not.
- Eventual consistency is acceptable for how quickly a new post propagates to followers' feeds.

Nailing these down early matters because "read-heavy with tolerable staleness" is the single assumption that justifies almost every design decision that follows — precomputation, caching, and async fan-out all trade a little freshness for a lot of read performance.

## Back-of-envelope estimation

Say the platform has 200 million daily active users, each posting an average of 0.5 times/day and loading their feed 10 times/day.

- **Writes**: 200M x 0.5 = 100M posts/day ≈ 1,150 posts/second average.
- **Reads**: 200M x 10 = 2 billion feed requests/day ≈ 23,000 requests/second average (assume 3x that at peak, so ~70,000/second).
- **Read:write ratio**: roughly 20:1 on request volume alone, and the gap widens further once you count that a single feed read implicitly touches dozens or hundreds of followed authors' posts.

That ratio is the whole design argument in one number: it's worth spending write-side effort (precomputing, fanning out, caching) to make each of those 70,000 peak reads/second cheap, because the write side only has to absorb ~1,150 posts/second.

## High-level architecture

A newsfeed backend splits cleanly into a handful of services around two data stores: a durable post store and a fast feed store.

- **Post service**: accepts a new post, writes it durably to the post store, and enqueues a fan-out job. Returns to the client as soon as the post is durably stored — fan-out is not on this critical path.
- **Social graph store**: who follows whom. Needs fast "get all followers of user X" and "get all accounts user Y follows" lookups; typically a graph-friendly store or a well-indexed relational/key-value table, not something you query live against millions of rows per request.
- **Fan-out workers**: pull jobs off the queue and write post references into each follower's feed-store entry.
- **Feed store**: per-user, a capped-length, roughly time-ordered list of post references — not full post content.
- **Feed service**: on a read request, pulls the requesting user's precomputed list, runs it (plus any real-time merges) through ranking, resolves full content, and returns a page.

## The feed-generation trade-off

This is the crux of the interview. There are exactly two moments you can assemble a feed: when a post is *created*, or when a feed is *read*.

**Precompute at write time.** When a user posts, immediately look up their followers and push a reference to the new post into each follower's feed-store entry. Reads become a single lookup — fast and cheap. The cost shows up on the write side, and it's proportional to the poster's follower count: a post from an account with 50 million followers means 50 million feed-store writes from one API call.

**Assemble at read time.** Do nothing at post time beyond storing the post. When a feed is requested, query every followed account's recent posts and merge them live. Posting is always cheap — O(1) regardless of follower count — but every read now does the work that precompute did once, over and over, for every viewer.

Neither extreme survives a real follower-count distribution, which is heavily skewed: most accounts have a modest number of followers, and a small minority have counts in the millions. The standard answer is a hybrid, split by a follower-count threshold: precompute (fan out) for accounts under the threshold, and merge live for the handful of high-follower accounts any given viewer follows. Because a typical viewer only follows a few such accounts even if they follow hundreds of ordinary ones, the live-merge cost per read stays small and bounded, while the vast majority of accounts still get cheap, precomputed reads for their followers.

Whichever side you land on, fan-out must be asynchronous — a background job on a queue, processed by an independently scalable worker pool — never something the post-creation request waits on synchronously.

## A minimal data model

- **`posts`**: `post_id`, `author_id`, `body`, `media_url`, `created_at`, `like_count`, `comment_count`.
- **`follows`**: `follower_id`, `followee_id`, `created_at` — indexed both directions (by follower, to find who a user follows; by followee, to find their followers).
- **`feed_entries`** (the feed store, keyed by viewer): a capped list of `(post_id, author_id, score_or_timestamp)` tuples, typically held in an in-memory store like Redis for low-latency access, with the post store as the durable source of truth.

Keeping `feed_entries` as references rather than copies means an edited post or an updated like count is visible everywhere immediately, since every viewer's feed points at the same underlying post row instead of a stale, duplicated copy.

## Ranking beyond "newest first"

A pure reverse-chronological feed is the simplest baseline and a reasonable place to start the conversation, but real feeds score each candidate post with something closer to:

`score = w1 * recency_decay + w2 * affinity(viewer, author) + w3 * content_weight(post)`

- **Recency decay**: newer posts score higher, but decay smoothly rather than using a hard cutoff, so a great post from six hours ago can still outrank a mediocre one from six minutes ago.
- **Affinity**: how much this specific viewer has historically engaged with this specific author — likes, comments, profile visits. This is what makes two users' feeds from the same follow-set look different.
- **Content weight**: signals like early engagement velocity, post type (a video may be weighted differently than plain text), or whether the viewer's close connections have interacted with it.

You don't need to design a machine-learning ranker to satisfy this part of the interview — naming these three signal categories and explaining how they combine into a single sortable score is usually enough. Where this actually gets computed matters too: for a small candidate set (a viewer's precomputed feed entries, typically capped at a few hundred), scoring at read time is cheap and lets you use the freshest engagement counts.

<Callout type="warning">
A common mistake is treating the fan-out threshold as a one-time classification made when an account is created. Follower counts change — a normal post goes viral, or a creator crosses into celebrity-tier territory — and a design that only checks the threshold once will keep precompute-fanning-out an account that has since become a write-storm risk, or worse, leave a newly-huge account's followers with stale feeds because nothing was flipped over to the read-time path. You need to periodically re-evaluate an account's tier and define what happens to already-generated feed entries during the transition, so followers neither lose posts nor get duplicates.
</Callout>

## Where this design breaks first

At the estimation numbers above, the first thing to fall over is usually the feed-store hot path for very active viewers — someone who follows thousands of accounts still needs their feed assembled fast, so cap the precomputed list length (a few hundred entries) and paginate beyond that from a colder store. The second common bottleneck is the social graph store under fan-out load, since every post triggers a "get all followers" query — this store needs to be optimized for exactly that access pattern, separately from any query pattern used for the follow/unfollow write path.
