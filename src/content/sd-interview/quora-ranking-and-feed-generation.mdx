The previous lesson gave you a schema for questions, answers, and votes, and an API to write and read them. None of that decides what order things appear in — and on a Q&A platform, order *is* the product. Open a popular question and the first answer you see should be the one that actually answers it best, not just the one posted first. Open your home feed and it should surface questions and answers you'll actually care about, out of a corpus that could be billions of items, without your servers falling over the moment someone with ten million followers posts.

Those are genuinely two different problems with two different constraints, and interviewers will push on both. Ranking answers under a single question is a bounded, on-demand sort — you can afford to recompute it fairly often. Generating a personalized feed is an unbounded, per-user problem that has to work at write time and read time simultaneously. This lesson covers the ranking logic for each, and the fan-out architecture that makes feed generation feasible at scale.

## Ranking answers on a question page

### Why raw net votes fail

The obvious first idea: sort answers by `upvotes - downvotes`, highest first. It's simple, but it has a structural bias toward *whichever answer showed up first*. The first answer to a fast-moving question gets seen by the most people (everyone who visits early), accumulates votes fastest, and locks in a lead that's very hard for a later, better answer to overcome — even if the later answer is more accurate or more complete. You end up ranking by "arrival time plus traffic," not by "quality."

Net votes also treat an answer with 3 upvotes and 0 downvotes the same as one with 300 upvotes and 0 downvotes when both are compared against an answer with, say, 250 upvotes and 10 downvotes — a net score of `3` vs `240` correctly ranks the second higher, but a naive *ratio*-based score (upvotes / total votes) would rank the 3-vote answer's 100% approval above the 250-vote answer's 96% approval, which is clearly the wrong call. You need a scoring function that accounts for *how much evidence* you have, not just the ratio.

### A confidence-based score

The standard fix is to rank by the lower bound of a confidence interval on the true upvote ratio, given the votes observed so far — commonly called a Wilson score interval. The intuition, without the statistics: with only 3 votes, you genuinely don't know if the "true" quality is 100% or something much lower — you don't have enough samples to be confident. With 250 votes at 96%, you have enough evidence to be fairly confident the true quality really is around 96%. The lower bound of the confidence interval is low for the 3-vote answer (wide uncertainty) and only slightly below 96% for the 250-vote answer (narrow uncertainty), so the 250-vote answer correctly ranks first.

You don't need to derive the formula from scratch in an interview — stating the idea ("rank by a confidence lower bound on the vote ratio, not the raw ratio or raw net count, so vote *volume* matters as much as vote *direction*") is the signal an interviewer is looking for.

### Adding recency and quality signals

Vote-based scoring alone still has a blind spot: it can't distinguish "this answer is now outdated" (a great answer about a product's pricing from three years ago) from "this answer is still correct." Layer in additional signals on top of the vote score:

- **Time decay** — apply a decay factor to the vote-confidence score based on answer age, so an answer that stopped accumulating votes years ago gradually loses ground to a newer, actively-upvoted one.
- **Edit recency** — an answer edited last month should get partial credit over one untouched since 2019, even with a similar vote history.
- **Quality/trust classifiers** — length heuristics, spam/abuse classifiers, and author reputation (has this author been marked as a subject-matter expert on this topic?) all combine into a final composite score used for the sort.

The composite is typically a weighted sum: `score = w1 * confidence_vote_score + w2 * recency_decay + w3 * author_reputation`, tuned by offline evaluation against what humans judge to be the better answer.

## Generating the personalized home feed

Ranking answers under one question is bounded — there might be a few hundred candidates. The home feed is a different scale problem entirely: the candidate pool is every question or answer posted by everyone you follow, every topic you're subscribed to, plus algorithmically recommended content — easily billions of items platform-wide. You cannot run an expensive ranking model over all of it for every feed load. The standard architecture splits the problem into stages.

**Candidate generation** narrows billions of possible items down to a few hundred plausible ones per user — items from followed topics, followed people, and a recommendation index — cheaply, using simple filters, not a heavy model. **Feature scoring** attaches signals to each candidate (topic match, author reputation, how similar it is to things you've engaged with before). The **ranking model** turns those features into a single relevance score per candidate. The **diversity re-rank** pass then adjusts the pure-score order so the top of your feed isn't ten answers about the same trending topic from the same three authors — without this step, a pure engagement-optimizing model over-indexes on whatever is currently viral. Only after all of that do you paginate and serve.

### Push vs. pull: where the fan-out happens

The remaining question is *when* candidate generation happens: at write time (someone posts) or at read time (you open your feed)?

**Push (fan-out-on-write)**: when an author posts, immediately insert a reference to that item into the precomputed feed of every follower. Reading your feed is then just "read my precomputed list" — fast and cheap. This breaks down badly for high-follower authors: one post from someone with ten million followers means ten million feed-store writes fired from a single API call.

**Pull (fan-out-on-read)**: don't precompute anything; when you open your feed, query every source you follow live and merge the results. This avoids the write storm, but it means every feed load for a normal user — who might follow hundreds of topics and people — issues hundreds of queries synchronously, which is too slow for a page that needs to render in a few hundred milliseconds.

The practical answer almost every large feed system converges on is **hybrid fan-out**: push for authors under some follower-count threshold (the vast majority of users), and pull-at-read for authors above it, merging the pulled results into the same precomputed feed before ranking. This keeps write amplification bounded for the small set of very-high-follower accounts while keeping read latency low for everyone.

## Worked example

Say two answers on a question both currently show `net votes = +240`. Answer A has 250 upvotes and 10 downvotes; answer B has 245 upvotes and 5 downvotes. Net-vote sorting can't even distinguish them cleanly if the totals were closer, and it certainly can't distinguish either from a brand-new answer with 12 upvotes and 0 downvotes, which has a *perfect* ratio but far less evidence. A confidence-lower-bound score correctly ranks B slightly above A (higher ratio at similar volume) and ranks both well above the 12-vote answer, because 12 votes isn't enough evidence to be confident the true quality is actually 100%. As that new answer accumulates more upvotes with a sustained high ratio, its lower bound rises and it can legitimately overtake A and B — which is the behavior you want: consistent evidence over time beats an early lead.

For the feed side: imagine an author with 8 million followers posts a new answer. Under pure push, that single write fans out to 8 million per-user feed-store entries — at even a modest cost per insert, that's a multi-second (or minute-scale) background job contending with every other fan-out job in the system. Under the hybrid model, that author is flagged "pull-tier," so the post triggers zero fan-out writes; each of their followers instead picks it up the next time their feed is generated, merged in alongside their normal push-tier candidates.

<Callout type="warning">
A common mistake in this lesson is designing pure push fan-out and only noticing the problem when asked "what if this author has 10 million followers?" If your design writes to every follower's feed synchronously (or even asynchronously but unconditionally) on every post, you've built a system where one popular author's write volume scales with their follower count, not with how often they post — that's the wrong axis to scale on. Call out the hybrid push/pull split before the interviewer has to ask for it; it's the detail that separates a design that merely works from one that survives a celebrity account.
</Callout>
