You've spent six lessons building a ride-hailing system from the ground up:
estimating load, splitting services along the location/trip planes, matching
riders to drivers, streaming location updates, and pricing a trip with a
live ETA. The real test of whether you actually learned the *transferable*
skill — not just memorized "Uber's architecture" — is whether you can point
that same toolkit at a system that looks similar on the surface but has a
different core constraint. Food delivery is that test. It reuses almost
every building block from this module, but it adds a third party (the
restaurant) whose behavior you don't control and can't instrument the way
you can a driver's GPS.

This lesson is a full mock interview: a prompt, a rubric, and a
self-graded reflection — no walkthrough. Treat it like the real thing: 35–40
minutes, out loud, on a whiteboard or blank doc, before you look at the
rubric.

## Why this isn't just "Uber with food"

It's tempting to answer this prompt by copy-pasting the ride-hailing design
and renaming "driver" to "courier." That gets you most of the way but misses
the part interviewers are actually probing for: can you spot where the new
domain's constraints change the design, not just its vocabulary?

Three differences matter:

- **A third actor with its own latency.** A driver in the Uber design is
  either available or busy — a binary you can query instantly. A restaurant
  has a *prep time* that varies by dish, kitchen load, and time of day, and
  you usually only get a soft estimate from the restaurant's system (or none
  at all). Your matching and ETA logic both depend on a number you can't
  measure directly and have to predict.
- **Matching has a timing dimension, not just a spatial one.** Ride-hailing
  matching is "find the nearest available driver, now." Food delivery
  matching is "find a courier who will arrive at the restaurant close to
  when the food is actually ready" — match too early and the courier idles
  in a parking lot; match too late and the food sits under a heat lamp
  losing quality and losing you the customer.
- **Batching is a first-class optimization, not a nice-to-have.** A courier
  can reasonably carry two or three orders picked up from restaurants near
  each other and dropped off along a similar route. Ride-hailing has an
  analogous idea (pooled rides) but it's optional; in food delivery,
  batching is often the difference between a courier network that's
  profitable and one that isn't, because delivery fees rarely cover a
  single dedicated trip per order.

Everything else — the geospatial index for "who's nearby," the durable
order/trip ledger, the separation of high-churn location pings from
low-churn state transitions, the ETA service composing multiple estimates —
carries over directly from the earlier lessons in this module.

## The order lifecycle, as a state machine

Just like the Uber trip, the food delivery order is the entity with a
lifecycle worth modeling explicitly as one row with a status column, not as
scattered booleans across three services.

<Mermaid client:visible chart={`
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Placed
    Placed --> Accepted: restaurant confirms
    Placed --> Cancelled: restaurant rejects / times out
    Accepted --> Preparing
    Preparing --> ReadyForPickup: kitchen marks ready
    ReadyForPickup --> PickedUp: courier confirms pickup
    Accepted --> CourierAssigned: dispatch matches courier
    CourierAssigned --> PickedUp
    PickedUp --> Delivered: courier confirms drop-off
    Placed --> Cancelled: customer cancels
    Delivered --> [*]
    Cancelled --> [*]
`} />

Notice `CourierAssigned` can happen concurrently with `Preparing` — that's
the "match against predicted ready-time, not actual ready-time" idea from
above, represented directly in the state machine as a branch that doesn't
wait for `ReadyForPickup`.

## A worked scenario to reason through out loud

Say a customer places an order at a restaurant with a quoted 18-minute prep
time. If dispatch waits until the food is physically ready before searching
for a courier, you add the courier's full travel-to-restaurant time on top
of the 18 minutes — a real delivery might then take 35–40 minutes end to
end. Instead, dispatch should start searching for a courier roughly
`prep_time - avg_courier_travel_time_to_restaurant` minutes after the order
is accepted, so the courier's arrival and the food's readiness land close
together. Get this offset wrong in either direction and you either pay for
idle courier time or add pure waiting time to every order — worth walking
through with concrete numbers if the interviewer asks "how do you decide
when to dispatch."

<Callout type="interview">
  A strong signal in this interview is naming the failure mode of getting
  the dispatch timing wrong, in both directions — not just describing the
  happy path. Interviewers are listening for "what happens when the
  restaurant's prep-time estimate is wrong," since that's the crux of why
  this problem differs from ride-hailing matching.
</Callout>
