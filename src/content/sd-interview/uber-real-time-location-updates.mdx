The high-level design named a "location service" as one box among many. This
lesson is about what that box actually has to do, because it's carrying more
load than every other service in the system combined. With a few million
drivers online and each one pinging every few seconds, you're looking at
roughly a million writes a second at steady state, before a single ride
request has even been made. Get this pipeline wrong and two very different
things break: the matching service starts dispatching against stale
positions, and the rider's app shows a driver marker that jumps and stutters
instead of gliding down the street. Neither failure crashes the system, which
is exactly why it's easy to under-design — the bug is "it feels laggy and
janky," not "it's down."

## Two consumers, two different needs

Before touching the pipeline, separate who actually reads this data:

- **The matching service** (covered in the previous lesson) needs a
  reasonably fresh index to answer "which drivers are near this pickup
  point right now" — a one-shot read, tolerant of a position being a few
  seconds stale, but it needs the *index* itself to be queryable fast.
- **The rider's app** needs a stream of updates it can render as a smoothly
  moving marker on a map, over the whole duration a driver is en route.
  This consumer cares about visual smoothness far more than about the exact
  freshness of any single point.

These two consumers want the same underlying data but shaped differently:
one wants a queryable snapshot, the other wants a display-ready stream. Build
one pipeline that serves both without conflating "how often does this update
internally" with "how often should a rider's phone redraw a marker" and the
design holds up; conflate them and you either starve the index or flood the
rider's phone.

## The ingestion path

A driver's app doesn't call the trip service or the matching service
directly with its GPS coordinates — it streams to the location service alone,
on its own path:

1. The driver app sends a location update (lat, lng, timestamp, driver ID)
   over a lightweight, persistent connection (a long-lived WebSocket or
   gRPC stream is typical) rather than opening a fresh HTTP request per
   ping — at this volume, connection setup overhead alone would be a real
   cost.
2. Updates land on an ingestion tier, which does the minimum necessary
   validation (is this driver online, is the timestamp newer than the last
   one accepted for them) and pushes the update onto a queue.
3. A consumer reads off the queue and writes the position into a fast,
   ephemeral geo-indexed store — a geohash-keyed in-memory store or a
   purpose-built geospatial database, not the durable trip ledger. Only the
   *latest* position per driver needs to be kept; there's no product reason
   to retain a full location history in this store.

The queue in step 2 matters for one specific reason: it decouples "a burst
of pings just arrived" from "the geo-index can absorb them at whatever rate
it can sustain," so a momentary spike (everyone's app reconnecting after a
network blip) doesn't take down the index itself.

### Adaptive ping frequency

A fixed ping interval is wrong in both directions. Consider tuning it to
trip state and device signals instead:

- **Idle, online, no trip**: ping every 10-15 seconds — riders aren't
  watching this driver yet, so freshness matters less than battery life.
- **En route to pickup or in-trip**: ping every 2-4 seconds — a rider is
  actively watching this marker, and the matching service may still need
  fresh positions for nearby re-dispatch decisions.
- **Backgrounded or low battery**: fall back to OS-level low-power location
  updates rather than the app's own high-frequency timer.

## Fanning updates back out to riders

Once a position lands in the index, getting it onto a rider's screen is a
separate design decision from ingestion. Two broad options:

- **Push** (WebSocket/SSE to the rider's app): the location service (or a
  thin fan-out layer subscribed to it) sends each driver's position to any
  rider currently tracking that driver's trip.
- **Poll**: the rider's app requests the driver's current position every
  `n` seconds.

Push feels more "real-time," but the fan-out rate to the rider does not need
to match the ingestion rate from the driver. If a driver pings every 2
seconds, sending every single one of those straight to the rider's phone is
wasted bandwidth and — worse — makes the marker visually jerk from point to
point. Down-sample on the way out (send every 2nd or 3rd update, or batch on
a fixed 2-3 second broadcast tick) and have the rider's app **interpolate**:
animate smoothly between the last two known points over the interval between
updates, rather than snapping the marker to each new coordinate the instant
it arrives. This single client-side trick is responsible for most of the
"the map feels smooth" experience riders associate with these apps — it has
nothing to do with server-side update frequency.

## Scaling the hot path

Sharding the geo index by geohash prefix (the same technique covered in
proximity-service designs) lets you distribute drivers across shards by
location. The catch specific to ride-hailing: driver density is wildly
uneven and shifts by time of day. A downtown core at rush hour can have
10-20x the driver density of the same area at 3am, and far more than a
suburban shard ever sees. Plan for hot shards explicitly — either by using
finer-grained geohash prefixes in historically dense areas, or by
autoscaling shard capacity based on observed load rather than a static
partition scheme decided once at launch.

## Handling flaky mobile networks

Mobile connections drop, queue, and reorder. Two defensive rules keep this
from corrupting the index:

- **Reject stale updates by timestamp.** If a driver's app briefly loses
  connectivity and then flushes three queued pings at once, only the
  newest one (by client timestamp, not arrival order) should update the
  index — discard the rest rather than trying to replay history into a
  store that only holds current position.
- **Expire drivers who go silent.** If no update arrives for a driver
  within a short window (say, 30-60 seconds), mark them offline/unavailable
  in the index rather than letting the matching service dispatch against a
  position that's minutes old.

## Worked example: sizing the pipeline

Say the platform has 5 million drivers online at peak, each pinging every 4
seconds on average while idle and every 3 seconds while in-trip, with roughly
20% in-trip at any moment. That's about `4,000,000 / 4 + 1,000,000 / 3`,
call it 1.3 million pings per second at peak. The queue and ingestion tier
need to sustain that comfortably; the geo-index only needs to hold one
current position per driver — 5 million small records, easily fitting
in-memory across a sharded fleet. On fan-out, if 500,000 riders are actively
tracking a driver right now and each gets an update every 2.5 seconds, that's
200,000 outbound updates per second — an order of magnitude below the
inbound rate, which is exactly the point of down-sampling on the way out.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is treating the location pipeline like the trip ledger:
  writing every ping straight into a durable, transactional, replicated
  store "to be safe." At a million-plus writes per second, that store either
  falls over or becomes the most expensive part of the whole system, for
  data that's obsolete within seconds anyway. Keep this path ephemeral and
  in-memory, and reserve durable, transactional storage for the trip plane
  (request created, matched, completed) described in the high-level design
  lesson — the two planes have opposite durability requirements for a
  reason.
</Callout>

## Where this leaves you

The location service now has real shape: an ingestion path built for
sustained high write volume with a queue absorbing bursts, an ephemeral
geo-index sized for "one current position per active driver," and a fan-out
path deliberately decoupled from ingestion rate so rider apps animate
smoothly instead of replaying every raw ping. The next lesson turns to
pricing and ETA, which lean on this same position data (and the matching
service's dispatch decisions) to quote a fare and an arrival time before the
rider even confirms a trip.
