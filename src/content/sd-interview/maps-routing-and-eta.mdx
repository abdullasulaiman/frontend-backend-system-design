Ask "how do you find the shortest path in a graph" in an algorithms
interview and everyone reaches for Dijkstra. Ask "how does Google Maps
route you across a continent in under a second, with an ETA that's usually
right within a couple of minutes" and Dijkstra is where the real interview
starts, not where it ends. The road network for even one country is
hundreds of millions of edges — running textbook Dijkstra from scratch on
every route request would take seconds to minutes, not milliseconds, and
it still wouldn't give you an accurate ETA, because the fastest route
depends on traffic conditions that change minute to minute.

This lesson covers the two problems candidates conflate: finding a good
*route* (a graph search problem) and predicting an accurate *ETA* for it (a
data-fusion and modeling problem). It builds on the road-segment graph
introduced in the map data model lesson and the ~520 route
computations/sec estimate from the requirements lesson.

## The road network as a routable graph

A road network maps naturally onto a directed, weighted graph:

- **Nodes** are intersections, on-ramps, forks — anywhere a driver can
  change direction.
- **Edges** are the road segments between two nodes, directed (a one-way
  street is a single edge; a two-way street is two edges, one per
  direction).
- **Edge weight** should be travel *time*, not distance. A 2-mile stretch
  of highway and a 2-mile stretch of school-zone side street are the same
  distance and wildly different travel times — routing on distance alone
  produces routes that are "shortest" but not "fastest," which is what
  users actually want.

Each edge weight is itself a function of a base speed (the road's speed
limit or typical free-flow speed) adjusted by time-of-day patterns and
current traffic — more on that below. For now, treat routing as: given
this weighted graph, find a low-cost path from source to destination.

## Why plain Dijkstra doesn't scale

Dijkstra's algorithm explores the graph in expanding rings outward from
the source, visiting nodes in order of increasing distance until it reaches
the destination. On a graph with hundreds of millions of edges, that means
touching a large fraction of the entire road network for a cross-country
query — it has no notion of "which direction the destination is in," so it
wastes enormous effort exploring nodes that are nowhere near the eventual
path.

Two techniques fix this, and interviewers want to hear you can layer them.

### Bidirectional search

Run Dijkstra simultaneously from the source (forward) and from the
destination (backward along reversed edges), alternating expansion between
the two searches. Stop once the two search frontiers meet. Because both
searches only need to expand roughly half the distance, the explored area
shrinks dramatically — intuitively, you've replaced "search a circle of
radius `d`" with "search two circles of radius `d/2`," which covers far
less area in total. This is a straightforward addition to a standard
Dijkstra implementation and a reasonable first improvement to propose.

### Contraction hierarchies: precompute the shortcuts

Bidirectional search alone still isn't fast enough for real-time queries
over a continental graph. The bigger win is doing expensive work
*offline*, once, rather than per query — this is the same "precompute at
write time, serve cheaply at read time" instinct that shows up everywhere
else in this course.

A **contraction hierarchy** ranks nodes by importance (a residential
side-street intersection is low importance; a highway interchange is high
importance) and, offline, removes low-importance nodes one at a time,
adding **shortcut edges** that preserve shortest-path distances between the
node's remaining neighbors. After this preprocessing, an online query only
needs to search a small, mostly-high-importance subgraph — it can "jump"
over long stretches of highway using a single precomputed shortcut edge
instead of expanding through every intermediate intersection. This is the
single biggest reason production routing engines return continental
routes in milliseconds instead of seconds: the hard graph-search work
happened ahead of time, not during the request.

## From shortest path to accurate ETA

Finding *a* path is necessary but not sufficient — the harder, more
business-critical problem is producing an ETA users can trust. ETA comes
from blending three layers of signal on top of the graph:

1. **Static road attributes** — speed limit, road class (highway vs.
   residential), number of lanes. This is the floor: how fast this road
   could be driven with zero traffic.
2. **Historical speed patterns** — average observed speed on this segment
   for this time of day and day of week, built from aggregated past trips.
   A segment might free-flow at 60 mph at 2am and average 15 mph at 5pm on
   a weekday — historical data captures that pattern even before any live
   report comes in.
3. **Live traffic conditions** — current speed derived from anonymized,
   aggregated GPS traces of vehicles currently on that segment (the same
   location-ping stream from the requirements lesson feeds this). When
   live data is available and statistically confident (enough recent
   samples on that segment), it overrides the historical estimate; when a
   segment has too few recent samples to be confident, fall back to the
   historical estimate rather than a noisy live one.

Route selection and ETA computation are coupled but distinct steps: the
graph search finds one or more candidate paths using a traffic-aware edge
weight, then the ETA for the chosen path is the sum of each edge's
current best time estimate. It's common to compute a small set of
candidate routes (fastest, and one or two reasonable alternates) and
present the fastest by current ETA, since "shortest by distance" and
"fastest right now" frequently disagree — a highway detour a few miles
longer is routinely faster than a short cut-through during rush hour.

### Re-routing during an active trip

Once a driver accepts a route, the client keeps sending position pings
(recall the ~104,000 pings/sec system-wide estimate). The server compares
each ping against the expected route: if the driver deviates — misses a
turn, or a live traffic event makes the current route meaningfully slower
than an alternative — it triggers a fresh route computation from the
driver's current position to the same destination. This is not a special
algorithm; it's the exact same routing query as the original request, just
re-run with an updated source node and updated live-traffic weights. The
only added logic is deciding *when* to trigger it: comparing actual
progress against the expected route, and re-routing when the deviation or
the ETA delta crosses a threshold, rather than recomputing on every single
ping.

## Worked example: rush-hour reroute

A driver requests a route from downtown to the airport, 18 miles away. At
request time, historical data says this trip typically takes 28 minutes at
this hour, but live traffic reports show a cluster of slow-moving GPS
traces on the direct highway segment, consistent with an accident. The
routing service computes two candidates: the direct highway route, whose
ETA (using live-adjusted edge weights) comes out to 41 minutes, and a
surface-street alternate whose ETA is 33 minutes. It returns the alternate
as the primary route with a 33-minute ETA, and keeps the highway route
available as a secondary option.

Ten minutes into the trip, live data shows the accident has cleared and
the highway has returned to its historical speed. The client's next
several pings show the driver still on the surface-street route, so no
re-route triggers — but if the driver approaches a junction where the
now-faster highway is still reachable and meaningfully quicker, the
service flags it as a suggested re-route rather than forcing a silent
recalculation, since abruptly changing a driver's route without their
awareness is a poor experience even when the new route is faster on paper.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is treating ETA purely as a graph-shortest-path output
  and bolting traffic on as an afterthought applied only to the final
  route. If live traffic weights aren't part of the edge weights *during*
  the search itself, the algorithm can select a route that looks shortest
  under free-flow assumptions but is actually the slower choice once
  traffic is applied — you have to search with traffic-aware weights, not
  search first and adjust the ETA number after the fact.
</Callout>
