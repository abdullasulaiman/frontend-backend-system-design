You've now been through requirements gathering, capacity estimation, high-level architecture, and deep-dives across a dozen case studies — from URL shorteners to ride-hailing dispatch. The previous lesson, Interview-Day Strategy Recap, covered how to behave in the room: pacing, communication, and handling curveballs. This lesson is the opposite direction — a concrete checklist to run against your own preparation *before* you walk in, plus an honest answer to "what do I actually do with my remaining prep time."

The reason a checklist matters here, specifically, is that system design prep has no automatic feedback loop. Unlike a coding problem, you can't run your design against test cases and get a red or green result. Without a structured self-check, it's easy to spend weeks re-reading case studies and feel more familiar with the material while your actual interview performance — the ability to *produce* a design live, under time pressure, from a blank page — barely improves. The checklist below is built to catch that gap.

## The four-part readiness checklist

Run this against your last 2-3 practice sessions (real or mock). If you can't check a box, that's your next study target, not a reason to feel behind.

### 1. Requirements

- Can you list the 2-3 functional requirements you chose to focus on, and explain *why* you cut the others? A design that tries to handle everything the prompt could plausibly mean usually handles nothing well in 45 minutes.
- Did you state non-functional priorities explicitly — consistency vs. availability, read-heavy vs. write-heavy, a latency budget — rather than leaving them implicit in your diagram?
- Did you produce at least one real number (QPS, storage per day, cache size) and actually *use* it later, e.g. to justify sharding, or to argue a single database is fine for now?

### 2. Architecture

- Does every box on your diagram have a one-sentence justification for existing? "We have a cache because reads outnumber writes 100:1 and most reads hit a small hot set" is a justification. A box labeled "Cache" with no reason is decoration.
- Can you trace a single request end to end on your own diagram — where it enters, what validates it, where it's written, what serves the read path — without hand-waving over an arrow?
- Did you separate concerns that behave differently under load? A common miss is dumping high-churn data (live location pings, view counts) into the same store as durable records (orders, accounts) — this bit you if you didn't at least mention it as a trade-off.

### 3. Deep-dive depth

- Did at least one component get pushed past a labeled box into an actual mechanism? "We use consistent hashing with virtual nodes to keep shard rebalancing to O(1/n) of the keyspace" is a deep-dive. "We shard the database" is not.
- Can you name the specific failure mode your chosen mechanism handles, and what it costs you? Every real mechanism has a downside — if yours doesn't, you likely haven't gone deep enough to find it yet.
- Did you connect the deep-dive back to a number from your requirements phase, instead of picking a trendy technology because it's the "expected" answer for that case study?

### 4. Delivery

- Did you say an assumption out loud when you were guessing, rather than presenting a guess as a fact? Interviewers weight "I'm assuming X because Y, correct me if that's off" far higher than silent guessing.
- Did you revise an earlier decision when new information came up, and say so explicitly ("actually, given the write volume we just estimated, I'd rather queue this than write synchronously")? Static designs that never adapt read as memorized, not reasoned.
- Did you manage the clock yourself, or did the interviewer have to redirect you more than once? Self-correction on pacing is itself a signal being evaluated.

## Worked example: auditing one of your own mock interviews

Say your last mock interview was designing a URL shortener, and it went "fine" — you finished the diagram, but the feedback was vague ("could be more detailed"). Run the checklist:

- **Requirements**: you estimated 100 writes/sec and 10,000 reads/sec, and picked availability over strict consistency for redirects. Checked.
- **Architecture**: your diagram has an API layer, a key-generation service, a database, and a cache. You can explain why the cache exists (read-heavy, small hot set of popular links). Checked.
- **Deep-dive**: this is where it breaks down. You said "the key-generation service generates unique short codes" and moved on. You never specified *how* — random string, plus a collision check? A counter encoded in base62? Pre-generated key pool consumed by workers? That's the missing mechanism, and it's exactly the kind of gap that produces "could be more detailed" as feedback with no specifics.
- **Delivery**: you paced well and finished on time, so no issue there.

The fix isn't re-reading the whole case study again — it's spending 15 focused minutes on *one* thing: pick a concrete key-generation mechanism (say, a counter-based approach with base62 encoding, sharded by assigning counter ranges per worker to avoid a single point of contention), understand its trade-off (predictable codes are guessable/enumerable, so you'd add a permutation step or use them internally with a random-looking external mapping), and rehearse explaining it in under 90 seconds. That's a much higher-value 15 minutes than re-reading the whole lesson end to end.

## Planning your remaining time

How you use the time left before an interview depends heavily on how much of it you have:

- **One week or less**: stop consuming new material. Redo 2-3 case studies from a blank page under a strict 45-minute timer, out loud (record yourself if you can bear it). The skill being tested is production under pressure, not recognition of concepts you've already read — rereading feels productive but doesn't train the thing that's actually being evaluated.
- **Two to four weeks**: split time three ways — one weak deep-dive mechanism per session (pick from caching strategies, queueing/ordering guarantees, indexing structures, or consensus/replication, whichever you're shakiest on), estimation math drills until back-of-envelope numbers stop feeling effortful, and one full mock interview per week scored against the four-part checklist above.
- **More than a month**: this is the time to build breadth — work through case studies you haven't touched, especially ones with an unfamiliar deep-dive concern (e.g. if you've never designed anything with strong consistency requirements, do one). Save the last week for the "one week or less" plan regardless of how much runway you started with.

Across any timeline, keep a running list of your own failure patterns from mock interviews — not "I didn't know X fact," but behavioral patterns like "I always jump to boxes before finishing requirements" or "I freeze when asked to estimate storage." A repeated pattern across three separate mock interviews is a stronger signal than any single missed detail, and it's usually fixable with one or two deliberate reps once you've named it.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common failure mode at this stage is treating "I've read all the lessons" as equivalent to "I'm ready." Reading builds recognition, not production — you can nod along to a design you're reading and still freeze on a blank whiteboard for the same prompt a week later. If your practice time is mostly re-reading rather than producing designs from scratch under a timer, you are optimizing the wrong metric. Swap at least half of remaining prep time to active recall: closed-book, timed, out loud.
</Callout>
