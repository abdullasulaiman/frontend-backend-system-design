"Design YouTube" (or its short-form cousin, "Design TikTok") is one of the
most common system design prompts because it forces you to reason about
almost every hard problem in distributed systems at once: massive
write-once/read-often data, expensive asynchronous processing, global
content delivery, and a search/discovery layer on top. Most candidates lose
points in the first five minutes — not because they don't know CDNs or
transcoding, but because they jump straight to drawing boxes without
pinning down what they're actually building or how big it needs to be.

This lesson is about that first five minutes: turning a vague prompt into a
concrete, numbered problem statement. Everything in the rest of this module
— the data model, the upload pipeline, the CDN strategy — is a direct
response to the numbers and constraints you establish here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

A video platform is a huge surface area. In an interview you have roughly
45 minutes total, so the first job is to agree on scope with your
interviewer rather than silently assuming everything is in play. A
reasonable core scope to propose:

- **Upload**: a creator uploads a video file with a title, description, and
  thumbnail.
- **Watch**: any user can stream a video, with playback that adapts to
  their network conditions (adaptive bitrate).
- **Browse / discover**: a home feed and a search box that return relevant
  videos.
- **Engagement**: likes, comments, and view counts.

Explicitly call out what you're deferring, and say so out loud: live
streaming, monetization/ads, recommendation-ranking ML, and content
moderation are all real parts of YouTube, but each is a system design
problem on its own. Naming them and setting them aside is a signal of
seniority — it shows you can scope a problem instead of getting lost in it.

### Non-functional requirements

These matter as much as the feature list, because they drive every
architectural decision downstream:

- **Availability over consistency for watch traffic.** If a view counter
  is off by a few hundred for a minute, nobody notices. If playback is
  down, users notice immediately. Bias the read path toward AP (in
  CAP terms) — eventual consistency is fine for counters and comments.
- **Durability for uploaded content is non-negotiable.** Losing a
  creator's only copy of a video is a much worse failure mode than a
  stale count, so uploads need strong replication guarantees even while
  reads stay eventually consistent.
- **Low latency start-of-playback.** Users expect a video to start
  playing in a second or two; buffering or long start-up delay is the
  single biggest driver of abandonment.
- **Global reach, variable bandwidth.** Viewers are everywhere, on
  everything from fiber to spotty mobile data — the system has to serve
  the same video at multiple quality levels.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Vague requirements produce vague designs. Even rough, stated assumptions
let you reason concretely. Here's a workable set of numbers for a
large-scale (YouTube-sized, not a startup MVP) video platform — you'd
adjust these live based on what the interviewer tells you, but this is the
right *shape* of estimate to produce:

- 1 billion total registered users, 200 million daily active users (DAU).
- Each DAU watches an average of 5 videos per day, each video roughly 10
  minutes long.
- 1 in 10,000 DAU uploads a video per day (a very small fraction of users
  are creators).
- Average uploaded video: 10 minutes long, roughly 500 MB raw before
  transcoding.

<Callout type="tip">
  In an interview, always say your assumptions out loud before computing
  anything: "Let's assume 200 million DAU and a 100:1 read-to-write ratio —
  does that sound reasonable to you, or do you have numbers in mind?" This
  turns a monologue into a collaborative step and gives the interviewer a
  chance to redirect you if they had a different scale in mind.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive traffic and storage numbers

With those assumptions, work through the two traffic types separately —
they stress completely different parts of the system.

### Upload throughput

- Daily uploads: 200,000,000 DAU multiplied by 1/10,000 equals 20,000
  videos uploaded per day.
- Uploads per second (average): 20,000 divided by 86,400 seconds is about
  0.23 per second — trivially low on average, but uploads spike around
  regional evenings, so provision for a peak multiplier of 5-10x, i.e.
  roughly 1-2 uploads/sec at peak. This is a small number precisely
  because uploading is rare compared to watching.
- Daily raw upload volume: 20,000 videos multiplied by 500 MB equals 10 TB
  of raw video ingested per day.

### Watch (read) throughput

- Daily video views: 200,000,000 DAU multiplied by 5 views equals 1
  billion views per day.
- Views per second (average): 1,000,000,000 divided by 86,400 is about
  11,600 views/sec. At a 5x peak multiplier, that's roughly 58,000
  views/sec at peak — this is the number your CDN and edge caching layer
  has to absorb, and it dwarfs the upload path by four to five orders of
  magnitude.

This asymmetry is the single most important scale fact in this whole
problem: **you will design the read path for ~50,000+ requests/sec, and
the write (upload) path for a couple of requests/sec.** That's why video
platforms lean so heavily on CDNs and caching rather than trying to scale
a single origin to handle every view.

### Storage

Transcoding a source video into multiple resolutions and bitrates (say
144p through 1080p, plus maybe 4K for some content) for adaptive
streaming multiplies the stored footprint. A conservative rule of thumb is
that the full set of transcoded renditions plus the original costs
roughly 3x the original file size in storage — this varies a lot with
codec and rendition ladder, but 3x is a reasonable interview estimate.

- Daily stored volume: 10 TB raw multiplied by 3 is about 30 TB/day of
  total storage added (original plus renditions).
- Over one year: 30 TB multiplied by 365 is roughly 11 PB/year.

That number alone tells you object storage (not a traditional database or
local disk) is the only viable choice for video bytes, and that a
metadata layer (titles, owners, view counts, transcoding status) has to
be a separate, much smaller system pointing at those objects — a split
we'll build out in the next lesson.

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

Turn the numbers into constraints you'll defend in the rest of the
interview:

1. **Read:write ratio is roughly 50,000:1** at these assumptions
   (11,600 views/sec versus 0.23 uploads/sec, on average) — everything
   downstream should be optimized for cheap, cacheable reads over fast
   writes.
2. **Storage is dominated by encoded video bytes, not metadata** — a
   relational database sized for metadata (titles, users, comments) is
   irrelevant next to the petabyte-scale blob storage problem.
3. **Upload can tolerate latency; playback can't** — transcoding a video
   can reasonably take minutes in the background, but the user pressing
   play needs sub-second response.
4. **Global distribution is mandatory, not optional** — at tens of
   thousands of views/sec spread worldwide, a single-region origin would
   need an unrealistic amount of egress bandwidth and would add
   cross-continent latency to every playback.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is spending ten interview minutes computing precise
  storage and QPS numbers to two decimal places, as if precision itself
  earns credit. It doesn't — interviewers care that you picked the right
  *drivers* (DAU, watch time, read:write ratio) and derived a
  directionally correct order of magnitude, then used it to justify a
  design decision. A wildly wrong assumption stated clearly and used
  correctly beats a precise calculation that never gets used for anything.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sanity-checking a follow-up

Interviewers often probe with a follow-up like: "What if this were
TikTok-style short video instead — 30-second clips, uploaded much more
often?" Re-run the same method rather than starting over: if the average
watch length drops from 10 minutes to 30 seconds but users watch far more
clips per session (say 50 instead of 5), your views/sec estimate goes up
roughly 10x while your per-video storage cost drops by roughly 20x
(shorter clips, smaller files). Net effect: more requests hitting your
read path and metadata layer, but a lighter storage footprint per item —
which is exactly why short-video platforms invest even more heavily in
feed-ranking and caching than in storage engineering. That's a good
signal to give an interviewer: showing you can re-derive constraints
under a changed assumption, not just recite the first set of numbers.

## Where this leads

The read-heavy, upload-light, storage-massive shape we just derived
directly drives the high-level architecture in the next lesson: a
metadata service backed by a database, a separate blob storage tier for
video bytes, an asynchronous transcoding pipeline sitting between upload
and storage, and a CDN in front of everything read-related. Keep these
numbers in your head as you move on — every design choice from here
should trace back to "why" in terms of the constraints above.
