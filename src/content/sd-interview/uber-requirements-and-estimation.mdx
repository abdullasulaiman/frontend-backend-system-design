"Design Uber" is a favorite interview prompt precisely because it punishes
vague answers. It looks like a single app, but underneath it's several
systems with very different personalities: a matching engine that has to
decide in seconds, a location pipeline that never stops streaming, a
pricing engine that has to react to demand spikes, and a trip ledger that
has to get money and state transitions exactly right. If you jump straight
to "we'll use a message queue and a database," you skip the step that
actually determines the architecture: how much load each piece carries and
what it can and can't get wrong.

This lesson is that scoping step. Everything in the rest of this module —
the high-level design, the matching algorithm, the location pipeline, and
pricing — builds on the numbers and constraints established here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Propose a core scope out loud and let the interviewer redirect you rather
than silently guessing at what they want:

- **Request a ride.** A rider specifies a pickup location and destination
  and requests a trip.
- **Match rider to driver.** The system finds a nearby available driver,
  offers them the trip, and confirms the match.
- **Track the trip live.** Both rider and driver see the other's live
  position on a map for the duration of the trip.
- **Price the trip and estimate arrival.** The system quotes an upfront (or
  metered) price and an ETA, both for driver arrival and for trip
  completion.
- **Complete the trip lifecycle.** The trip moves through clear states
  (requested, matched, driver en route, in progress, completed) and ends
  with payment and receipt.

Explicitly defer: scheduled rides booked hours in advance, pooled/shared
rides with multiple riders per trip, driver incentive programs, and
in-app support/disputes. Each is a legitimate follow-up but would dilute a
45-minute session if tackled up front. (Food delivery is a deliberately
related but distinct system — this course covers it separately later,
since sharing a rider-facing app doesn't mean sharing a matching model.)

### Non-functional requirements

- **Matching must be fast.** A rider expects a driver assignment within a
  couple of seconds of requesting, not tens of seconds. This pushes the
  design toward pre-computed, geo-indexed driver locations rather than a
  live scan of every driver on every request.
- **Location updates favor availability over strict consistency.** A
  driver's position being a few seconds stale is invisible to the user;
  losing the entire location feed because one replica is down is not
  acceptable. Availability and low latency win here, not perfect ordering
  or durability of every single ping.
- **Trip assignment needs a correctness guarantee.** Exactly one driver
  gets assigned per trip request — two drivers accepting the same ride is
  a real-world failure, not a display bug. This one narrow slice of the
  system needs a stronger consistency mechanism (an idempotent
  claim/lock), even while everything around it stays eventually
  consistent.
- **The system is inherently geographic.** A ride in Austin never competes
  for drivers with a ride in Berlin. This isn't just a data-locality
  optimization — it should shape how you partition data and estimate load
  from the very first numbers you write down.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

State assumptions and invite correction rather than presenting them as
fact:

- 100 million monthly active riders worldwide; 25 million daily active
  riders (DAU).
- 5 million trips requested per day.
- 2 million drivers online (available or on a trip) at a given moment
  during a global peak window, out of a much larger registered driver
  base.
- An average trip lasts 15 minutes door to door.
- Every online driver's app sends a location ping every 4 seconds,
  whether or not they're currently on a trip — this matters, because most
  online drivers are idle/available far more of the time than they're
  actively driving a fare.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say it plainly: "I'll assume 25 million daily riders, 5 million trips a
  day, and roughly 2 million drivers online at peak, each pinging location
  every 4 seconds — does that match the scale you have in mind?" Getting
  a nod (or a correction) here means every number downstream is defensible.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive the numbers for each sub-system

### Trip request and matching QPS

- Daily trip requests: 5,000,000. Average QPS: 5,000,000 divided by 86,400
  seconds is about 58 requests/sec.
- Ride-hailing demand is spikier than most consumer traffic — commute
  hours, bar close, and bad-weather surges concentrate demand hard. Apply
  an 8x peak multiplier (higher than a typical 3-5x for browsing traffic):
  peak matching load is roughly 460 requests/sec globally.
- Each match isn't a simple lookup — it queries nearby available drivers,
  ranks candidates, and confirms an assignment, so treat this as
  moderately expensive per request even though the QPS is modest.

### Location ingestion QPS

- 2,000,000 online drivers, each sending one ping every 4 seconds, gives
  2,000,000 divided by 4 is 500,000 location pings/sec on average — and
  this is steady throughout business hours, not spiky like trip requests,
  because drivers stay online (searching for fares) far longer than any
  single trip lasts.
- 500,000 pings/sec dwarfs the roughly 460 matching requests/sec at peak.
  That gap is the single most important number in this lesson: **location
  ingestion, not matching, is the dominant load on the system**, purely
  because it's a continuous per-driver stream rather than a one-shot
  request.

### Storage: trip records vs. raw location history

- Trip records: 5,000,000 trips/day at roughly 1 KB each (rider ID,
  driver ID, timestamps, route summary, price) is about 5 GB/day, or
  under 2 TB/year — trivially small, easily handled by an ordinary
  relational or document store.
- Raw location pings, if you kept every single one: 500,000 pings/sec at
  roughly 100 bytes each is 50 MB/sec, which is about 4.3 TB/day. That's
  a very different order of magnitude from trip records, and it's why
  most systems keep only a rolling window of raw pings (for live tracking
  and safety/audit needs) and discard or downsample the rest rather than
  retaining every ping forever.

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **Location ingestion is the system's dominant QPS path** — at roughly
   500,000 pings/sec, this needs a horizontally partitioned, write-optimized
   pipeline (think a partitioned queue keyed by geographic cell), not a
   synchronous write per ping into a single database.
2. **Matching must query a geo-local subset, never a global scan** — even
   though 2 million drivers are online globally, a single match only ever
   considers drivers within a few kilometers, so the driver index should
   be partitioned by geographic cell (geohash/S2) from day one, not
   bolted on later.
3. **Trip assignment is the one place that needs a strong guarantee** —
   an idempotent claim on a trip request (so exactly one driver accept
   "wins") sits inside an otherwise eventually-consistent system.
4. **Trip metadata storage is a non-problem; raw location history is the
   real capacity decision** — decide explicitly what window of location
   pings you retain versus discard, because storing everything forever
   scales into terabytes per day for no clear benefit.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is sizing the whole system off one blended QPS number,
  or off monthly active users instead of concurrent online drivers. Both
  hide the real bottleneck: the roughly 460 requests/sec of matching
  traffic looks like the "main" load because it maps to a visible user
  action, but the roughly 500,000 pings/sec of location updates — an
  always-on background stream — is over three orders of magnitude larger
  and needs its own dedicated, geo-partitioned ingestion path.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sanity-checking a follow-up

Suppose the interviewer asks: "What if we tightened the driver ping
interval from 4 seconds to 2 seconds for smoother map movement?" Re-derive
rather than guess: pings per driver per second go from `1/4` to `1/2`, a
2x increase, so location-ingestion QPS jumps from 500,000/sec to about
1,000,000/sec — while trip-request QPS (still 58/sec average, 460/sec
peak) and trip-record storage are completely unaffected, since neither
depends on ping frequency. Naming exactly which number a changed
assumption touches — and which numbers it leaves alone — is what
distinguishes a candidate who understands the system from one who's just
re-multiplying numbers.

## Where this leads

The split established here — a moderate-QPS matching path that needs
geo-local queries and a strong assignment guarantee, versus a
high-QPS, availability-first location-ingestion path — is exactly the
shape of the architecture in the next lesson. It's also why matching and
dispatch, and real-time location updates, get their own dedicated lessons
rather than being folded into one generic "backend" discussion: they are
different problems wearing the same UI.
