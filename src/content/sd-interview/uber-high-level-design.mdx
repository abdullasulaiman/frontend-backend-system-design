The previous lesson pinned the numbers down: tens of millions of trips a day,
millions of drivers pushing a location update every few seconds, and a
matching decision that has to happen in well under a second or the rider
gives up and closes the app. Those numbers point at the shape of the design
before you draw a single box. A ride-hailing backend isn't one service with
one database — it's a handful of services with genuinely different load
profiles glued together by one thing they all care about: **the current
state of a trip**. Get that state machine right and the rest of the design
falls into place; treat the whole system as "a database of drivers and a
database of trips" and you'll struggle to explain how it survives Friday
night surge.

## Start from the two planes

Before naming services, separate traffic into two categories, because they
need opposite things from their storage:

- **The location plane** — a continuous stream of GPS pings from every
  active driver (and, during a trip, the rider too), used to answer "who's
  nearby" and "where's my driver." High write volume, short-lived data,
  tolerant of losing an occasional ping — nobody notices one dropped update
  out of a ping every 4 seconds.
- **The trip plane** — the handful of events that actually matter for
  billing, support, and correctness: request created, driver matched, trip
  started, trip ended, payment captured. Much lower volume, but every event
  must be durable — losing a "trip completed" event is a real incident, not
  a rounding error.

Conflating these two is the single biggest source of over-engineered (or
under-engineered) Uber designs. A store built for one is wrong for the
other: a durable relational trip ledger can't take a location write every
few seconds from millions of drivers, and an in-memory geospatial store
built for churn-y location data is the wrong place to keep a legally
important billing record.

## Major components

### Driver and rider apps

Both are thin clients. The driver app streams location pings and renders
incoming ride requests; the rider app renders nearby driver markers (from
periodic snapshots, not a raw firehose) and the current trip state. Neither
app talks to a single "backend" endpoint — both go through an API gateway
that fans requests out to the right service.

### API gateway / edge layer

Handles auth, rate limiting, and request routing for both apps. Nothing
architecturally special here beyond the rest of the platform, but worth
naming explicitly so it's clear driver and rider traffic doesn't hit
internal services directly.

### Location service

Ingests the location stream from every online driver and rider-in-trip,
writes it into a fast, geo-indexed store (an in-memory grid/geohash index
or a purpose-built geospatial database), and answers "which drivers are
within `n` km of point `(lat, lng)`" queries. This is covered in depth two
lessons from now — for this lesson, the key point is that it's a separate,
horizontally-shardable service that nothing else needs to understand the
internals of.

### Matching / dispatch service

Takes a ride request (rider location, destination, ride type), queries the
location service for nearby available drivers, ranks them, and — the hard
part — claims exactly one driver before a competing request can grab the
same car. This is a distinct problem from the location query itself: one is
"find candidates," the other is "resolve a race for a scarce resource." The
next lesson goes deep on this; here it's one box that reads from the
location service and writes a match into the trip service.

### Trip service

Owns the trip state machine and is the source of truth for "what is
happening with this ride right now." Every other service reacts to state
changes here rather than deciding trip state on its own:

`requested -> matched -> driver_en_route -> in_progress -> completed`
(with `cancelled` reachable from any state before `completed`).

Backed by a durable, transactional store — this is the one place in the
design where you want strong consistency and a real ACID story, because
double-charging a rider or losing a completed trip is unacceptable.

### Pricing / ETA service

Computes an upfront fare estimate and a driver ETA, factoring in distance,
current demand/supply imbalance (surge), and traffic. Deliberately kept
as a service the trip service *calls*, not logic baked into it — pricing
models change constantly (new surge algorithm, new promo logic) and you
don't want every change to risk the trip state machine. Covered in its own
lesson later in this module.

### Notification service

Pushes state changes out to devices: "driver assigned," "driver is 2
minutes away," "trip started." It subscribes to trip-state-change events
rather than being called synchronously by every other service — this keeps
notification delivery (which can be slow, retried, or occasionally
best-effort) from ever blocking a trip-state write.

### Payment service

Captures payment once a trip completes, using the fare computed by the
pricing service and the final trip record. Kept external (Uber doesn't
process card networks itself) but the trip service must not mark a trip
"paid" until this service confirms — another reason the trip plane needs a
durable, consistent store, not an eventually-consistent one.

## Worked example: a rider requests a trip

Trace one request through the boxes above. A rider taps "request ride" in
the app; the request hits the **gateway**, which calls the **pricing
service** for an upfront estimate (reading current demand/supply from the
location service) and shows it to the rider for confirmation. On
confirmation, the **trip service** creates a trip record in `requested`
state and hands off to the **matching service**, which queries the
**location service** for drivers within, say, 3 km, ranks them by ETA, and
attempts to claim the top candidate. The claim succeeds; the trip service
transitions the record to `matched` and the **notification service**
pushes "driver assigned" to the rider and "new trip" to the driver. As the
driver drives to pickup, their app keeps streaming pings to the **location
service**, which the rider app polls to animate the driver's marker. Once
the driver hits "arrived" and then "start trip," the trip service moves
through `driver_en_route -> in_progress`. On drop-off, the driver taps "end
trip," the trip service marks it `completed`, calls the **payment
service** to capture the fare the pricing service quoted (adjusted for
actual distance/time), and only then does the trip's status become
final. Nine services touched, and each one only needed to know its own
job — that's what keeps this tractable to draw and defend in 45 minutes.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is designing "the matching service" as if it does one
  thing: find the nearest driver. In practice it does two things with very
  different guarantees — a fast, approximate geospatial read (who's nearby)
  followed by an exclusive write (claim that driver before someone else's
  request does). If your design only talks about the read half, an
  interviewer will immediately ask "what stops two riders from being matched
  to the same driver at the same instant?" — have an answer ready (this is
  exactly what the next lesson covers) rather than discovering the gap live.
</Callout>

## Where this leaves you

This lesson's job was to name the services and justify the boundaries
between them, especially the split between the churn-heavy location plane
and the durable trip plane. The next three lessons go deep on the pieces
this design glosses over: how matching actually resolves the driver-claim
race under load, how location updates stay cheap and smooth at millions of
concurrent drivers, and how pricing and ETA get computed. The module wraps
up by assembling all of it into one interview-paced answer.
