The previous lesson established *why* a support bot needs retrieval-augmented generation and sketched the architecture at a high level: a knowledge base, a retriever, and a generator sitting behind a chat interface. That's the right shape, but "add a vector database and call an LLM" is not a design — it's a diagram with three boxes. The interview (and the real system) lives in the details: how documents get chunked and indexed, how a query turns into the right context, how the model is stopped from making things up, and what happens when it can't help.

This lesson designs each of those stages concretely, then works through a capacity estimate so the numbers behind "low latency, low hallucination rate" are grounded rather than assumed.

## Ingestion: turning documents into searchable chunks

The knowledge base — help-center articles, internal runbooks, past resolved tickets, product docs — arrives as long-form text. It has to be broken into chunks small enough to retrieve precisely and to fit inside the model's context window alongside several other chunks and the conversation itself.

Two decisions matter most:

- **Chunk by semantic boundary, not a fixed token count.** Splitting a "how to reset your password" article at an arbitrary 512-token cutoff can sever a step from its prerequisite. Chunking along headings, list items, or Q&A pairs keeps each chunk a coherent, self-contained unit — which also makes each chunk more useful as a standalone citation.
- **Attach metadata to every chunk.** Product line, locale, plan tier, document version, and last-updated timestamp all travel with the vector, not just the text. Retrieval later filters on this metadata before or alongside the similarity search — a French-speaking customer on the free tier shouldn't get an English enterprise-only runbook back just because it's semantically close.

Ingestion runs as an offline batch/streaming pipeline, decoupled from user requests: a document changes, a worker re-chunks and re-embeds it, and the vector store is updated asynchronously. Re-indexing must never block or slow down live chat traffic — that's a common design smell interviewers probe for.

## Retrieval: broad recall, then a narrow, accurate re-rank

A single similarity search against a vector store is a good first filter but a mediocre final answer. Two problems show up in practice: pure dense-vector search can miss exact-match terms (an error code, a SKU) that a keyword search would nail instantly, and top-k from a single fast index isn't always the k most *relevant* results, just the k most similar by one metric.

The fix is a two-stage retrieval design:

1. **Broad recall** — run a dense vector search (embeddings) and a lexical search (BM25/keyword) in parallel, each returning a wider set (say, 50 candidates) than you'll ultimately use.
2. **Re-rank narrow** — merge the candidate sets and pass them through a smaller, more accurate cross-encoder re-ranker that scores query-chunk relevance directly, then keep only the top handful (3-6 chunks) that actually go into the prompt.

This "retrieve wide, re-rank narrow" pattern trades a bit of extra latency in stage two for materially better precision, and it's the detail that separates a bot that finds the right paragraph from one that finds *a* paragraph.

Before retrieval even runs, it's often worth a lightweight query-rewrite step: expanding "it's not working" using the last two turns of conversation into something like "user cannot complete checkout, error 402, mobile app" — vague chat messages retrieve poorly on their own.

## Generation: grounding the answer in what was retrieved

The retrieved chunks, the rewritten query, recent conversation turns, and a system prompt instructing the model to answer *only* from the provided context all get assembled into the final prompt. Two things need explicit design attention here:

- **Context budget.** With a fixed context window, you're trading off how much conversation history versus how many retrieved chunks you keep. A common pattern: summarize older turns, keep the last one or two turns verbatim, and reserve the majority of the budget for retrieved context.
- **Forced citation.** Instructing the model to tag which chunk supports each claim (even informally, like a bracketed source ID) gives you two things for free: a citation to show the user ("Source: Refund Policy, updated June 2026") and a cheap downstream check — if a sentence in the answer can't be traced to any retrieved chunk, that's a hallucination signal.

## Guardrails and escalation as a designed path, not a failure mode

A support bot that occasionally can't answer is expected; a support bot that confidently invents a refund policy is a liability. The design needs an explicit gate between generation and the user:

- **Grounding check** — verify the answer's claims trace back to retrieved chunks (via the citation tags above, or a second, cheaper LLM call that scores "is this answer supported by this context").
- **Confidence/coverage threshold** — if retrieval returned low-similarity chunks across the board, that's a signal the knowledge base doesn't cover this question at all; don't let generation paper over a retrieval gap.
- **Scope guard** — refuse (gracefully) on anything outside the support domain, and on anything requiring an account action the bot isn't authorized to take (refunds over a limit, account deletion).

When any gate trips, escalation is the answer — and it should carry everything the human agent needs: the conversation transcript, the chunks that were retrieved, and a short bot-generated summary of what the customer is asking. Treat this handoff as a real API contract with your ticketing system (create a ticket, attach the context, route by category), not an afterthought.

## Worked example: sizing a knowledge base and a latency budget

Say the knowledge base has 8,000 help-center articles, averaging 1,200 words each. Chunking by semantic section produces roughly 4 chunks per article on average — about 32,000 chunks. At a typical 768-dimension embedding stored as 4-byte floats, each vector is about 3 KB; with index overhead and metadata, budget roughly 5 KB per chunk, putting the vector store around 160 MB — small enough to fit comfortably in memory on a single well-provisioned index node, with room to grow 10x before you need to think about sharding.

For latency, a reasonable per-turn budget for a chat-style bot is around 2-3 seconds to first token. A rough split: query rewrite (~100ms), parallel vector + keyword search (~150ms), re-ranking 50 candidates down to 5 (~150ms), prompt assembly (negligible), and LLM generation, which dominates the budget at 1.5-2 seconds for a first token with streaming for the rest. That leaves the retrieval pipeline needing to land well under 500ms combined — which is exactly why the two-stage "wide recall, narrow re-rank" design matters: doing a full cross-encoder pass over all 32,000 chunks per query would blow that budget by orders of magnitude, while doing it over 50 pre-filtered candidates does not.

<Callout type="warning">
  A frequent interview-answer mistake: describing retrieval as "embed the query, cosine-similarity search the vector DB, done" with no re-ranking, no metadata filtering, and no grounding check before the answer reaches the user. That design retrieves *something* fast but has no mechanism to catch a wrong document, an access-control leak (returning enterprise-only content to a free-tier user), or a hallucinated claim the model added on top of a legitimate chunk. Naming these explicit stages — recall, re-rank, filter, ground, escalate — is what shows an interviewer you've thought about failure modes, not just the happy path.
</Callout>
