Two numbers get shown to a rider within a second of tapping "request ride":
a price and a time. Get either one visibly wrong and the rider closes the
app before a driver ever gets assigned. Yet both numbers have to be produced
under the same tight latency budget as everything else in this design, at
a scale where the naive version of either calculation — walk every nearby
driver's exact route, or read live demand straight off the request table —
falls over immediately. This lesson covers how both get computed cheaply
and consistently: a fare built from deterministic components times a
demand-driven multiplier, and an ETA that comes from a road graph rather
than a ruler.

## Two different problems wearing one name

"Pricing and ETA" is really two related but separable calculations:

- **Price**: what will this ride cost? Made of a predictable part (distance,
  time, a flat fee) and a volatile part (surge — how tight is supply right
  now).
- **Time**: how long until pickup, and how long is the trip itself? Made of
  the same distance the pricing calculation needs, run through a routing
  model instead of a cost model.

They share an input (route distance/duration) but answer different
questions, and — as you'll see below — they have different freshness
requirements. It's worth stating this split explicitly in an interview:
it stops you from accidentally designing one service that does both badly.

## Computing ETA

### Pickup ETA vs. trip ETA

A rider sees two ETAs at different points in the flow: **pickup ETA** (how
long until the matched driver arrives) drives the "driver arriving in 4
min" banner right after matching, and **trip ETA** (how long the ride
itself will take) is part of the upfront quote and then the in-trip
progress bar. Both need the same underlying capability — turn two points
into a route and a duration — just applied to different point pairs
(driver-to-rider vs. rider-to-destination).

### From distance to time: routing vs. approximation

The tempting shortcut is straight-line (haversine) distance divided by an
assumed average speed. It's cheap, but it's wrong in exactly the cases
that matter most: a driver on the other side of a river, a one-way grid
downtown, a highway that adds distance but saves time. A real design needs
a **routing engine** — a road-graph service (think a specialized shortest-
path service over an OSM-style road network) that returns an actual route
polyline plus a duration, informed by road class and, ideally, historical
or live traffic conditions for that time of day.

That said, "call the full routing engine on every request" is itself a
scaling concern — routing a graph with tens of millions of edges is not
free. The practical mitigation, and a reasonable thing to say out loud in
an interview: cache route/duration lookups for common origin-destination
cell pairs (most short urban rides start and end in a small set of dense
geo-cells), and fall back to the full graph search only on a cache miss.

### Keeping ETA fresh during the trip

Pickup ETA isn't a one-shot number computed at match time — the driver
keeps moving, so the estimate has to update. It's tempting to recompute it
on every location ping, but that's overkill: location pings arrive every
few seconds purely to keep the map marker smooth (see the previous
lesson), while an ETA that changes by a few seconds isn't worth a
recompute. Recompute pickup ETA on its own, coarser cadence — every 10–15
seconds, or when the driver's position has moved enough to plausibly
change the route — and treat it as a separate concern from raw location
streaming, even though it reads from the same position data.

## Computing price

### The deterministic base fare

Most of a fare is arithmetic, not a service call: a flat **base fee**, a
**per-kilometer rate**, a **per-minute rate** (covers time stuck in
traffic, not just distance), and sometimes a flat **booking fee**. This
part is cheap, predictable, and the same for every ride of a given type in
a given city — it's a lookup of rate-card constants plus the route
distance/duration you already computed for the ETA.

### Surge: pricing for scarcity

The volatile part is a **surge multiplier** applied on top of the base
fare, meant to do two things at once: ration a scarce resource (available
drivers) toward the riders willing to pay for it, and pull more drivers
into an under-served area by making it temporarily more profitable to
drive there.

The naive implementation — for every incoming request, count open ride
requests and available drivers in the surrounding area, right then — is a
hot, contended read against the same driver pool the matching service is
also hammering, and it recomputes the same number thousands of times a
minute for requests in the same neighborhood. The better shape: a
**background aggregation job** partitions the city into geo-cells (the
same grid the location service already uses), continuously tracks open
requests and available drivers per cell, and writes a multiplier per cell
into a fast cache (something like Redis) every few seconds. The pricing
service, at request time, does a single cheap cache read by cell — no
live counting on the request path.

### Locking the quote

A rider confirms a quoted fare, but matching, driver acceptance, and the
actual trip can take a couple of minutes — long enough for the surge
multiplier in that cell to have moved by the time a driver is assigned.
The trip service should snapshot the fare (base components and the surge
multiplier read at quote time) into the trip record the moment the rider
confirms, and honor that snapshot rather than re-reading a live multiplier
later. The final charge is then reconciled against the *actual* GPS-traced
distance and time once the trip completes (routes deviate from the
estimate — traffic, a driver taking a different turn), but the surge
component of the price stays what was quoted.

## Worked example: quoting a downtown ride after a concert lets out

A concert venue lets out 8,000 people at 11pm into a 1 km² downtown cell
that normally has 40 available drivers and maybe 15 open requests at any
moment — comfortable supply. Within two minutes, open requests in that
cell jump to 300 while available drivers stay near 40, since drivers
already dropped off elsewhere haven't rerouted in yet.

The aggregator job, running every 5 seconds, recalculates that cell's
ratio: roughly 300 requests to 40 drivers, well past the threshold the
platform maps to (say) a 2.2x multiplier, and writes `2.2` into the surge
cache for that cell. A rider requesting a ride from just outside the venue
gets a quote of `base fare x 2.2`, computed as a cache read plus the
routing engine's distance/duration for their trip — no live scan of the
driver table on their request. They confirm; the trip service snapshots
`2.2` into the trip record. Even if the ratio drifts to `1.8` by the time
a driver is actually matched 90 seconds later (more drivers converge on
the area, chasing the surge), this rider's confirmed price doesn't change
— it was locked at quote time. The next rider requesting a ride now reads
the fresh `1.8` from the cache.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is treating the quoted fare as "advisory" and silently
  re-pricing the trip if the live surge multiplier changes before a driver
  is assigned. It feels more "accurate," but it's the fastest way to erode
  rider trust — people remember the number they confirmed, and a fare that
  moves after confirmation reads as a bait-and-switch, even when the
  underlying market genuinely shifted. Snapshot the multiplier into the
  trip record at confirmation and hold it there; let only the
  distance/time components reconcile against what actually happened during
  the trip.
</Callout>

## Where this leaves you

Pricing and ETA both boil down to the same shape: a cheap deterministic
calculation (rate card, or route distance/duration) combined with a value
read from a cache that a background process keeps warm, rather than
anything computed live and synchronously against a hot data store on the
request path. The next lesson pulls this together with matching, location,
and the trip state machine into one interview-paced answer for the whole
system.
