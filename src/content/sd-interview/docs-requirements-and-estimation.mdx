Picture three people editing the same paragraph of a shared document at
the same time: one fixing a typo near the top, one adding a sentence in
the middle, one deleting a clause near the bottom. Within a couple hundred
milliseconds, each of them needs to see everyone else's changes, in the
right place, without garbling the text or silently dropping anyone's edit.
That's different from a typical CRUD app, and different from the
read-heavy fan-out problems you'd design for a social feed — the audience
for any single edit is tiny (a handful of people looking at that one
document), but the latency and consistency bar is much higher.

This lesson pins down what "collaborative document editor" means before
designing anything, and derives the numbers that matter. We're
deliberately not choosing a conflict-resolution algorithm or drawing the
architecture here — those are the next two lessons. The job here is
scoping requirements and sizing the problem so those decisions have
something concrete to respond to.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

A reasonable core for an interview-sized version of this system:

- **Create, open, and edit a rich-text document**: headings, bold/italic,
  lists, and basic formatting; treat embedded images/tables as extensions,
  not the core problem.
- **Real-time collaborative editing**: two or more users editing the same
  document concurrently see each other's changes appear almost
  immediately, with the document converging to the same content for
  everyone.
- **Presence**: see who else has the document open and roughly where their
  cursor or selection is.
- **Revision history**: view past versions of a document and restore an
  earlier one.
- **Access control and sharing**: an owner assigns viewer, commenter, or
  editor permissions to specific people or via a shareable link.

Explicitly defer and say so out loud: comments/suggestion mode, offline
editing with later resync, and the exact conflict-resolution algorithm are
real parts of the product, but each deserves its own lesson — offline
support later in this module, OT vs. CRDT in the very next one. Naming
them and moving on, rather than solving everything in one pass, is a
strong signal in an interview.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Low edit-to-edit latency.** An edit made by one collaborator should
  appear for other active editors within roughly 100-300ms under normal
  network conditions — this is a live-interaction latency budget, not a
  page-load one.
- **Strong convergence, not strong consistency.** Every editor's local
  copy must eventually reach the exact same document content regardless
  of the order edits happen to arrive in — but a brief moment where two
  editors see slightly different intermediate states is acceptable.
- **Edits must never be silently lost once acknowledged.** A user who
  sees "saved" and closes their laptop must never come back to find that
  edit missing.
- **Available over strictly consistent for the editing path**, but
  durable and auditable for the revision history — losing a document's
  history entirely is a much worse failure than a temporary sync hiccup.
- **Scales by number of concurrently-active documents, not total users.**
  The overwhelming majority of stored documents are closed and idle at
  any moment; the system needs to handle spikes of concurrent editing on
  a small fraction of documents, not uniform load across all of them.

## Step 2: Establish the scale

State assumptions plainly rather than silently guessing. A workable set of
numbers for a large, widely used product:

- 50 million daily active users (DAU).
- Each DAU opens about 3 documents per day.
- About 1 in 20 document-opens (5%) becomes a genuinely multi-editor
  session at some point — meaning a second person joins while the first
  is still there — rather than a solo read or solo edit.
- During peak hours, roughly 500,000 documents are being edited by 2 or
  more people simultaneously, averaging about 2.4 concurrent editors on
  each of those documents.
- A person actively composing text produces a small edit operation (an
  insert or delete of a short run of characters, since the client
  coalesces fast keystrokes into batches roughly every 100-150ms) on
  average every 300-500ms while typing — but spends only about a quarter
  of their connected time actually typing, with the rest spent reading,
  scrolling, or thinking.
- A typical document body is about 20 KB of rich text; assume roughly 2
  billion documents exist in total across the product's lifetime.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say this out loud before computing anything: "Let's assume 50 million
  DAU and that about 5% of document-opens become truly concurrent
  multi-editor sessions — does that match the scale you had in mind, or
  should we assume a more enterprise-heavy, more collaborative mix?"
  Checking this with the interviewer matters more here than in a typical
  read-heavy system, because the concurrent-editor ratio is the number
  that drives the entire design.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive operation throughput and storage

### Concurrent editing sessions

- Concurrently-collaborative sessions at peak: 500,000 documents
  multiplied by 2.4 average concurrent editors is about 1.2 million
  live, connected editing sessions at any given peak moment.
- This is the number that sizes your connection layer (persistent
  WebSocket-style connections, presence tracking) — it has nothing to do
  with the 2 billion total stored documents, almost all of which are
  idle at any given instant.

### Edit-operation throughput

- Effective sustained op rate per connected session: roughly one op every
  2 seconds once you factor in the 25% "actually typing" duty cycle
  against a burst rate of one op every 300-500ms — call it about 0.5
  ops/sec per session as a round number.
- Peak system-wide incoming ops: 1.2 million sessions multiplied by 0.5
  ops/sec is about 600,000 ops/sec in aggregate, across the whole fleet.
- That aggregate number looks large, but it shards almost perfectly by
  document ID: with 500,000 documents actively receiving edits, the
  *per-document* rate is only around 1.2 ops/sec on average. No single
  document is ever a throughput problem — the challenge is latency and
  ordering for that document's small set of editors, not raw volume.

### Storage: document bodies vs. the operation log

- Total document body storage: 2 billion documents multiplied by 20 KB
  is about 40 TB — trivial, and barely grows day to day since most
  documents, once written, are edited rarely afterward.
- Now estimate the *raw* operation log if every micro-edit were kept
  forever: daily multi-editor sessions are roughly 50,000,000 DAU
  multiplied by 3 opens multiplied by 5% is 7.5 million sessions/day.
  Assume an average collaborative session lasts about 20 minutes (1,200
  seconds) at 0.5 ops/sec: 7.5 million multiplied by 1,200 multiplied by
  0.5 is about 4.5 billion ops/day.
- At roughly 100 bytes per stored op (the edit itself, author, timestamp,
  and a pointer into the document's revision chain), that's about 450 GB
  per day of raw operation data — well over 150 TB a year, dwarfing the
  40 TB of document content and growing without bound.

The contrast is the key takeaway: **per-document edit throughput is tiny,
document bodies are cheap to store, but a naive "keep every operation
forever" revision history grows far faster than the documents themselves
and becomes the dominant storage and cost concern if left unaddressed.**

## Step 4: State the resulting design constraints

1. **Shard by document ID, not by user or by shard-of-everything.** Since
   the workload is almost embarrassingly parallel across documents, a
   per-document (or per-shard-of-documents) ownership model for routing
   edits keeps each shard's load trivial.
2. **The connection layer must support persistent, low-latency
   delivery** (long-lived connections, not request/response polling) to
   hit the 100-300ms edit-to-edit latency target for the roughly 1.2
   million concurrent editing sessions at peak.
3. **Convergence, not locking, is the concurrency model.** With multiple
   people editing the same paragraph, blocking one editor until another
   finishes is unacceptable at this latency budget — the next lesson's
   choice between Operational Transformation and CRDTs exists specifically
   to satisfy the "everyone converges to the same result" requirement
   without locks.
4. **Plan for op-log compaction from day one.** The 450 GB/day raw
   operation volume is not sustainable to keep verbatim forever; revision
   history needs periodic snapshotting with older fine-grained ops pruned
   or rolled up, while still supporting "restore an earlier version."

## Worked example: sizing one busy document

Take a single document being actively co-edited by 4 people during a
one-hour planning session. At the per-document average of about 1.2
ops/sec (rounding up for a genuinely busy document, call it 3 ops/sec
during the busiest stretches), that's roughly 10,800 operations over the
hour. At 100 bytes/op, that's about 1 MB of raw operation-log data for
that one document, for one meeting.

Multiply that by even a modest 10,000 similarly busy documents on a given
day (a small slice of the 500,000 peak-concurrent figure, sustained
across a full day rather than one peak moment) and you get about 10 GB of
op-log data just from the "busy" tier — before counting the long tail of
documents with only occasional light edits. This is exactly why the
design constraint above (compact into snapshots, prune old fine-grained
ops once a document is quiescent) has to be part of the architecture, not
a later optimization: the raw log outgrows the actual content it
describes within days, not years.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is estimating this system the way you'd estimate a
  social feed or a URL shortener — total documents, total users, average
  QPS across the whole platform. Those numbers barely matter here. The
  numbers that actually drive the design are per-document concurrent
  editors, the resulting edit-to-edit latency budget, and the growth rate
  of the operation/revision log relative to document size. Skip those and
  you'll design a system that's fast for the wrong bottleneck (aggregate
  throughput) while being slow or unbounded in storage for the real one
  (per-document latency and history growth).
</Callout>

## Where this leads

The numbers derived here — tiny per-document throughput, a strict
sub-second latency budget for a small set of concurrent editors, a
convergence requirement instead of locking, and an operation log that
outgrows document content fast — are exactly what the next lesson
responds to when it picks between Operational Transformation and CRDTs.
The high-level architecture lesson after that builds the edit-routing and
connection layer this lesson's per-document sharding constraint implies,
and the final two lessons build the editing service and the
scaling/offline story on top of it. Keep the concurrent-editors-per-document
and op-log-growth numbers in mind — they're the justification you'll come
back to at every later step.
