You've already picked a geospatial index (geohash or quadtree) and sketched
the service around it. That design works fine for a demo with a few thousand
businesses on one machine. It falls over the moment you point real traffic at
it, for a reason that's specific to geospatial systems: the data and the
traffic are both wildly non-uniform across space. Manhattan has tens of
thousands of businesses in a few square miles and gets searched constantly;
most of Montana has almost none. Any scaling plan that assumes "just split
the data evenly across N machines" breaks against that skew. This lesson is
about the specific techniques that handle it.

## Where the load actually concentrates

Two asymmetries drive every decision here:

- **Reads dwarf writes.** Someone opens the app and searches "coffee near me"
  far more often than a business updates its hours or a new business opens.
  Design the read path for scale first; the write path just needs to stay
  correct and reasonably fast.
- **Both reads and writes are spatially skewed.** A geohash prefix covering
  downtown San Francisco can have 1000x the business density and query
  volume of a same-length prefix covering open desert. Anything you shard by
  raw geography will inherit that skew unless you actively counteract it.

Everything below is really about handling that second point without giving
up the first.

## Sharding the index without creating hot shards

The natural instinct is to shard by geohash prefix: shard 1 owns cells
starting `9q8`, shard 2 owns `9q9`, and so on. This works, and it keeps
range queries (a user's cell and its neighbors) mostly within one or two
shards. But fixed-length prefixes assign *equal address space*, not *equal
load* — a `9q8` prefix over central London and a same-length prefix over the
North Atlantic get wildly different traffic.

Two fixes, and you'll usually combine both:

1. **Adaptive cell granularity.** Split dense cells into more, smaller
   shards (deeper geohash prefixes, or deeper quadtree subdivision) and
   merge sparse cells into coarser ones. This is exactly the same idea used
   for the index structure itself, just applied one level up at the
   sharding boundary — a busy downtown gets many narrow shards; a sparse
   region gets one wide shard.
2. **A routing layer that tracks ownership, not a fixed formula.** Instead of
   computing "shard = hash(prefix) mod N" and hoping it balances, keep a
   small routing table (cell range → shard) that a lightweight router
   service consults per request. When a shard gets hot, split its range,
   move half to a new shard, and update the routing table — no client
   changes required, no rebalancing outage.

Quadtree-based designs get this almost for free, since the tree already
subdivides dense regions more finely; the natural sharding boundary is a
subtree, with router nodes forwarding a query to whichever shard owns the
subtree the query's coordinates fall into.

## Caching the read path

Nearby-search queries repeat heavily by geography — hundreds of users
standing within a few blocks of each other all search "restaurants nearby"
and get nearly the same answer. Cache results keyed by a coarse version of
the query (grid cell, category filter, radius bucket) with a short TTL, say
30-60 seconds. This absorbs the vast majority of read traffic before it ever
touches a shard, and a modest staleness window is invisible to users — a
restaurant that closes doesn't need to vanish from search results within a
second.

Cache invalidation should ride the same async pipeline as index updates
(below), not fire per-request: when a business in a cell changes, invalidate
that cell's cached query results rather than trying to patch them in place.

## Keeping the index fresh without blocking writes

A business's location, hours, or open/closed status changes far less often
than it's searched, so the write path can afford to be asynchronous:

1. A business update lands in the primary business-data store and emits an
   event onto a queue.
2. An async indexer consumes the event and updates the affected shard(s) —
   removing the business from its old cell if it moved, inserting it into
   the new one, and invalidating the relevant cache entries.
3. Search continues serving from the (briefly stale) index the whole time;
   there's no lock, no rebuild pause, no coordination with in-flight reads.

The one edge case worth calling out explicitly: a business moving across a
cell or shard boundary needs a delete-from-old-cell plus insert-into-new-cell,
not just an update-in-place — get this wrong and the business silently
duplicates in stale caches or vanishes from search entirely.

## Worked example: rebalancing a hot shard

Say your service shards by 5-character geohash prefix uniformly, ~150
shards total, and each shard is provisioned for roughly 2,000 queries/sec.
A prefix covering a dense few blocks of Manhattan starts receiving 40,000
queries/sec during lunch hour — 20x its provisioned capacity — while a
same-length prefix over rural Nevada gets 10 queries/sec.

Fixed sharding has no answer here short of over-provisioning every shard for
worst-case load, which is wasteful at the scale of "every rural shard also
gets Manhattan-level capacity." With adaptive splitting, the router detects
the Manhattan prefix is hot, splits it into 8 finer sub-cells (deeper
prefixes), and spreads those across 8 shards — each now handling roughly
5,000 queries/sec, within capacity. The routing table is updated to reflect
the new finer boundaries for that region; the rural shard is untouched, or
could even be merged with a neighbor since it's far under capacity. The
result is capacity that tracks actual demand instead of raw map area.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common interview mistake: proposing "shard by geohash prefix, done" and
  moving on. An interviewer will immediately ask what happens to the shard
  covering Times Square versus the one covering open ocean — if you don't
  have an answer (adaptive splitting/merging, or a routing layer that can
  rebalance), you've described a design that works in a demo and falls over
  in production. Always name the hot-shard mitigation explicitly.
</Callout>
