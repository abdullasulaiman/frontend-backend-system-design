"Design a recommendation system" shows up in interviews for feed-based
products (video, music, e-commerce, social) because almost every large
consumer product eventually needs one, and it's a genuinely different kind
of design problem from a CRUD service. The hard part isn't storing data or
scaling reads — it's that the "correct" output isn't well-defined ahead of
time. You're not looking up a record; you're predicting which of millions
of items a specific user is most likely to engage with, in under 200ms,
using signals that go stale the moment you compute them. That combination
of scale, latency, and a fuzzy correctness bar is what makes this lesson
worth its own design, distinct from a straightforward ranked-feed problem.

## Requirements

**Functional**

- Given a user (and optionally a context — home feed, "similar items,"
  post-purchase), return a ranked list of items they're likely to engage
  with.
- Incorporate multiple signal types: past interactions (clicks, watches,
  purchases), item metadata (category, content), and social/collaborative
  signals (what similar users liked).
- Handle cold start: new users with no history, new items with no
  interactions.
- Support feedback: today's recommendations should get worse or better
  based on how users respond to them.

**Non-functional**

- Latency: p99 under roughly 100-200ms for the online request, since
  recommendations usually sit on a page's critical path.
- Scale: tens to hundreds of millions of users, a catalog from thousands
  (e-commerce categories) to hundreds of millions of items (video,
  social posts).
- Freshness: a user's last five minutes of activity should be able to
  influence the next thing they're shown, even if the underlying model
  was trained yesterday.
- Explore/exploit balance: the system must occasionally surface things
  outside a user's established pattern, or it ossifies.

<Callout type="note">
  This lesson assumes the model itself (what to train, which loss function)
  is out of scope for a systems interview — the interviewer wants the
  *pipeline* that gets features to a model and predictions to a user within
  budget, not a machine-learning research discussion. Say that framing out
  loud early; it keeps the conversation on architecture.
</Callout>

## Why you can't rank the whole catalog

The naive design is: take every item in the catalog, score it against the
user with your best model, sort, return the top N. That's correct in
theory and impossible in practice. A model expressive enough to capture
real signal (deep cross-features between user and item) costs single-digit
milliseconds *per item* to score. Scoring 50 million items per request
would take minutes, not milliseconds — off by four to five orders of
magnitude from the latency budget.

The fix every production recommender uses is a **funnel**: cheap, coarse
filtering first to cut the candidate set down by orders of magnitude, then
progressively more expensive and more accurate scoring on the survivors.

## High-level architecture: a two-stage funnel

**Candidate generation** narrows millions of items to a few hundred or
thousand plausible ones, cheaply. Common approaches:

- **Collaborative filtering / embeddings**: represent users and items as
  vectors in the same space (a "two-tower" model trains a user tower and
  an item tower so that a user's vector sits near the vectors of items
  they'd engage with). Item embeddings are precomputed offline and loaded
  into an approximate-nearest-neighbor index (HNSW, IVF); at request time
  you compute the user's vector and do a sub-millisecond ANN lookup.
- **Rule-based / heuristic sources**: "trending in your region," "same
  category as your last purchase," "followed by people you follow." These
  run in parallel with the embedding retrieval and their outputs get
  merged — multiple candidate sources feeding one pool is normal and
  healthy, because each source covers a blind spot of the others.

**Ranking** takes that merged pool (thousands of items) and scores each
one with a heavier model that can afford to look at richer features —
user-item cross-features, recency, session context — because it's now
only scoring hundreds to low-thousands of items instead of millions.

**Re-ranking / business rules** is a final cheap pass: remove items the
user already bought/watched, enforce diversity ("no more than two items
from the same seller in a row"), inject a small exploration slot, apply
policy filters (age-restricted content, region blocks).

## The feature store: the seam that breaks in practice

Both candidate generation and ranking need features about the user and
the item — recent click count, average session length, item popularity
decayed over the last hour. These features are computed two places:
**offline**, in bulk, when training the model on historical logs; and
**online**, per-request, when serving a live prediction. If the offline
and online code paths compute "average session length" even slightly
differently, the model sees different-shaped inputs in training versus
production and its accuracy silently degrades — this is **train/serve
skew**, and it's one of the most common causes of "the model works in
notebooks but underperforms in production."

The standard fix is a **feature store**: a system that defines each
feature once and serves it two ways — a batch view for building training
datasets, and a low-latency online view (usually a key-value store like
Redis or a wide-column store) for serving requests. Both paths read the
same feature definition, so training and serving see identical
transformations.

## Freshness: batch vs. online recompute

Recomputing full personalized recommendations for every user on every
request is expensive. Most systems split by how much freshness actually
matters:

- **Batch precompute**: for the majority of users, run candidate
  generation and ranking as an offline job (hourly or few-times-a-day),
  store the resulting list in a cache keyed by user ID, and serve it
  directly — a cache read, no model inference on the request path at all.
- **Online recompute**: for users where the last few minutes matter (an
  active shopping session, a user who just finished a video and is picking
  the next one), recompute at request time using the freshest features,
  accepting the extra latency.

A common middle ground: serve the batch-precomputed list as a base, then
apply a lightweight online re-rank using only session-level signals from
the last few minutes, without rerunning the full candidate generation
step.

## Feedback loops and exploration

Every impression and its outcome — click, watch-through, purchase, or
explicit skip — gets logged and feeds back into training. Two mistakes
are worth calling out explicitly:

1. **Logging only positive signals.** If you only log clicks, the model
   never learns what users saw and *ignored*, so it can't distinguish
   "genuinely irrelevant" from "never shown." Log impressions and skips
   alongside clicks so negative examples exist.
2. **Pure exploitation.** A model trained only on past engagement, then
   used to pick what to show next, then retrained on the outcomes of what
   it showed — reinforces its own biases. Items it never surfaces never
   get engagement data, so they never get recommended, forever. This is
   why production systems reserve a small slot (often single-digit
   percent of impressions) for exploration: recommending an item outside
   the model's confident set, sometimes via a multi-armed bandit, purely
   to gather signal on it.

## Worked example: estimating the funnel

Say a video platform has 80 million daily active users and a catalog of 5
million videos, with a 150ms latency budget for the home-feed request.

- **Candidate generation**: an ANN lookup over 5 million item embeddings
  (each maybe 128 dimensions) using an HNSW index returns the top 2,000
  candidates in single-digit milliseconds, merged with roughly 500 more
  from a "trending" heuristic source and 500 from "same creator you
  follow" — call it a pool of around 3,000 candidates after de-duplication.
- **Ranking**: a model with richer user-item features scores those 3,000
  candidates. At roughly 0.05ms per item on a batched GPU/CPU scoring
  pass, that's about 150ms worth of naive per-item scoring — too slow — so
  production systems batch the scoring call (score all 3,000 in one
  vectorized pass) rather than looping item by item, bringing it down to
  low tens of milliseconds.
- **Re-rank**: filtering already-watched items and injecting 2-3
  exploration slots into the top 20 is negligible cost, sub-millisecond.
- **Total**: roughly 10-15ms candidate generation + 20-40ms ranking +
  under 5ms re-rank, leaving comfortable headroom under 150ms for network
  and serialization overhead.

The number that matters isn't any single stage's latency — it's that each
stage's *output size* determines the next stage's affordable *cost per
item*. Get that ratio backwards (an expensive model scoring millions of
items) and no amount of hardware fixes the budget.

<Callout type="warning">
  A frequent interview mistake is presenting the ranking model as if it
  were the whole system — "we train a model, it ranks items, done." That
  skips candidate generation entirely, which is the piece that actually
  makes the latency budget achievable, and it skips the feature store,
  which is where real production systems spend a surprising amount of
  their engineering effort. If you only describe the model, you've
  described 20% of the design; walk through the funnel, the feature
  serving path, and the feedback loop explicitly.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs to raise proactively

- **Precision vs. latency**: a bigger ranking model is more accurate but
  slower — this is exactly why candidate generation exists, to buy the
  ranking stage room to be expensive on a small set.
- **Freshness vs. cost**: batch precompute is cheap but can be hours
  stale; online recompute is fresh but costs a model call on every
  request. Segment users by activity level rather than picking one
  approach for everyone.
- **Personalization vs. cold start**: a pure collaborative-filtering
  approach has nothing to work with for a brand-new user or item;
  content-based similarity and simple popularity fallbacks cover that gap
  until enough interaction data accumulates.
- **Relevance vs. diversity**: ranking purely by predicted engagement
  produces a narrow, repetitive feed over time; diversity constraints and
  exploration slots trade a little short-term click-through for long-term
  retention and catalog coverage.
