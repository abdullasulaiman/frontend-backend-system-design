You've now covered requirements and estimation, the high-level architecture, message delivery and ordering, presence/groups/notifications, and media plus end-to-end encryption for a WhatsApp-style system. None of that matters in an actual interview unless you can assemble it live, under time pressure, in response to a single open-ended prompt and a series of follow-ups you can't predict in advance.

That's the skill this lesson drills. A real system design interview isn't a quiz on facts — it's a 45-60 minute conversation where the interviewer is watching how you structure ambiguity, what you choose to clarify versus assume, and whether you can go deep on the one or two things they probe without losing the rest of the design. This lesson gives you a realistic prompt, a time budget, and a rubric so you can run the exercise yourself before doing it live with another person.

## How to run this mock interview

Treat it like the real thing:

1. Set a timer for 45 minutes.
2. Read only the prompt in the `<MockInterview>` block below — resist the urge to peek at the rubric first.
3. Talk out loud (or write notes as if explaining to an interviewer) as you work through requirements, architecture, and at least one deep dive.
4. Only after you've produced an answer, reveal the rubric and self-score honestly against each point.

The value comes from doing the thinking cold, the same way you will in the actual interview — not from reading a model answer passively.

## A time budget that works for most chat-system prompts

Interviewers don't grade on a stopwatch, but candidates who blow the whole session on requirements, or jump straight to Kafka and Cassandra without any structure, run out of time before the design is complete. A workable allocation for a 45-60 minute slot:

- **5 minutes — requirements and scale.** 1:1 and group chat? Media? Presence and read receipts? Roughly how many users, messages/day, group size cap? Online vs offline delivery expectations.
- **10 minutes — high-level architecture.** Clients, a stateful gateway/chat-server tier holding WebSocket connections, a durable message store, a connection/presence registry, and a push-notification path for offline devices.
- **15-20 minutes — one or two deep dives.** The interviewer usually steers this: message ordering and deduplication, multi-device sync, group fan-out at scale, or how encryption changes server-side search and backup. Go deep on what's asked rather than re-explaining the whole system.
- **5-10 minutes — scaling and failure modes.** What happens when a chat server crashes mid-connection? How do you shard the message store? What if a user has 200 groups?
- **5 minutes — wrap-up.** Summarize the design, name the trade-offs you made explicitly, and mention what you'd do differently with more time.

## What a strong candidate actually sounds like

The difference between a pass and a borderline result is rarely knowledge of a specific technology — it's whether the candidate reasons about trade-offs out loud. Concretely, listen for (and produce) these habits:

- **States assumptions before designing.** "I'll assume group size caps around 256 members and we need at-least-once delivery with client-side dedup — let me know if that's off" beats silently assuming and building on shaky ground.
- **Separates concerns clearly.** The message store (durable, append-only per conversation) is a different component with different guarantees than the delivery path (chat servers routing to currently-connected sockets). Conflating them is the single most common architectural muddle in this design.
- **Names the hard part before being asked.** A strong candidate volunteers "the interesting part here is ordering when a sender is on two devices and the recipient is offline" rather than waiting to be steered there.
- **Quantifies instead of hand-waving.** "With 2 billion messages/day that's roughly 23,000 messages/sec average, so we need to plan for maybe 5-10x that at peak" is a concrete, checkable claim.

This is the same skeleton from the high-level design lesson, redrawn to emphasize the split that matters most in an interview: the store is the durable source of truth, and delivery (live socket or push) is a separate, retryable concern layered on top of it. Multi-device sync reads from the same per-conversation log, just tracking its own cursor per device.

## Worked example: handling a follow-up mid-interview

Suppose you've drawn the skeleton above and the interviewer asks: "A user is signed into both their phone and laptop. How do you guarantee they see the same messages in the same order on both?"

A strong live answer sounds like this: "Each conversation has its own durable, append-only log with a monotonically increasing sequence number per message. Every device the user owns keeps its own delivery cursor — the last sequence number it has acknowledged — against that same log, not against each other. When a device reconnects, it asks the store for everything after its cursor, so a laptop that was closed for two days just replays from where it left off. Because ordering comes from the log's sequence number rather than from wall-clock arrival time, both devices converge on the identical order even if push delivery to one of them was delayed or retried."

That answer works because it names the mechanism (per-conversation sequence number, per-device cursor) instead of just the property ("they'll be consistent"). If pressed further — "what if the phone is offline for a month?" — the next layer is pagination/backfill limits and possibly a compaction or summarization strategy for very old backlogs, which you can mention as a known extension without designing it fully unless asked.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mock-interview failure in this module isn't a wrong answer — it's an incomplete one delivered as if it were complete. Candidates draw chat servers and a message queue, say "and then we use Kafka for ordering," and move on without explaining what "ordering" actually means here: ordering per conversation, not globally; a defined sequence source (not just "the queue handles it"); and an explicit dedup key so retries don't create duplicate messages on the recipient's screen. If you can't say the sentence "ordering is scoped to X and guaranteed by Y," you haven't actually designed ordering — you've named a technology and hoped it covers the gap.
</Callout>
