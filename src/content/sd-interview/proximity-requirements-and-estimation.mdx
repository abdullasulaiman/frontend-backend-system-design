"Find good sushi near me" looks like a trivial database query until you
have to make it fast at scale. A user opens the app expecting a ranked
list of restaurants sorted by how close they are — not by a text match on
the word "sushi," and not from a table scan over every business on the
continent. That's the core of a proximity service like Yelp: locate
businesses (or drivers, listings, any geotagged entity) within a radius of
a point, and rank them by distance and relevance, fast and at volume.

Before touching any indexing structure or service design, scope what "the
system" covers and put real numbers on how much traffic and data it
handles. Those numbers decide almost everything downstream: whether a
simple index suffices or you need a specialized geospatial one, whether
the bottleneck is storage or query throughput, and how the write path
(adding and updating listings) differs from the read path (searching).
This lesson is that scoping step — the high-level design, indexing, and
scaling lessons that follow all lean on the constraints set here.

## Step 1: Scope the functional requirements

Propose a core feature set and confirm it with the interviewer rather than
assuming you both mean the same thing by "Yelp":

- **Nearby search**: given a user's location (or a searched location) and
  a radius, return a ranked list of businesses within that radius,
  optionally filtered by category ("coffee," "plumber," "sushi").
- **Ranking**: results are ordered primarily by distance, though a real
  system blends in rating and relevance — for this lesson, treat distance
  as the primary signal and note that ranking blending is a design detail
  for later.
- **Business detail view**: viewing a single business's full profile
  (hours, address, phone, aggregate rating).
- **Business listing management**: a business owner (or an admin pipeline)
  can add a new listing or update an existing one's location, hours, or
  category.

Explicitly defer, and say so out loud: review text and photo storage,
personalized recommendations, real-time chat, and ride-hailing-style live
driver matching are all real extensions of "find something nearby," but
each is its own design problem. Naming what's out of scope signals you
understand the size of the problem, not just its name.

### Non-functional requirements

- **Low, predictable read latency.** A search should return in well under
  a second — treat 200ms as the budget for the search path end to end.
  Users abandon a search UI that feels sluggish.
- **Read-heavy by a wide margin.** Businesses open, close, and move
  rarely; users search constantly. Design for a read:write ratio in the
  hundreds-to-one range, not something closer to 1:1.
- **Availability and eventual consistency over strict consistency.** If a
  business updates its hours and it takes a minute to show up for every
  searcher, nobody notices. Favor an always-available search path over one
  that blocks on strong consistency guarantees.
- **Geospatially skewed load.** Search volume clusters where businesses
  cluster — dense urban cores generate far more query and data density per
  square mile than rural areas, and this skew is spatial, not just
  temporal (it also has a strong time-of-day component: lunch and dinner
  hours spike volume in a given city).

## Step 2: Establish the scale

Pick concrete numbers and state them as assumptions to be corrected, not
facts:

- 50 million monthly active users (MAU), 10 million daily active users
  (DAU).
- Each DAU performs an average of 5 nearby searches per day (browsing
  categories, re-searching after moving, checking a few options before
  picking one).
- 50 million business listings in the system.
- 20,000 new business listings added per day; 100,000 existing listings
  updated per day (hours, address corrections, category changes).
- Default search radius is 5 miles, returning the top 20 ranked results
  per query.

<Callout type="tip">
  Say the numbers out loud before computing anything: "I'll assume 10
  million DAU, 5 searches per user per day, and 50 million total business
  listings — does that match the scale you have in mind?" This turns
  estimation into a checkpoint with the interviewer instead of a silent
  monologue they have to evaluate after the fact.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Derive the core numbers

### Search QPS

- Daily searches: 10,000,000 DAU multiplied by 5 searches is 50,000,000
  searches/day.
- Average QPS: 50,000,000 divided by 86,400 seconds is about 580
  queries/sec.
- Peak QPS: search traffic spikes hard around lunch and dinner in any
  given time zone. A 4x peak multiplier is reasonable, putting peak load
  at roughly 2,300 queries/sec.

### Write QPS

- Daily writes: 20,000 new listings plus 100,000 updates is 120,000
  writes/day.
- Average write QPS: 120,000 divided by 86,400 is about 1.4 writes/sec.

That puts the read:write ratio at roughly 580 to 1.4 — over 400:1. This is
the single most important shape of the problem: almost all engineering
effort goes into making the read (search) path fast and scalable, while
the write path can be comparatively simple and doesn't need to optimize
for throughput.

### Storage

- A core business record (ID, name, category, address, latitude/longitude,
  phone, hours, aggregate rating) runs about 500 bytes. 50,000,000 records
  multiplied by 500 bytes is 25 GB.
- A geospatial index entry per business (the structure that maps a
  location to nearby business IDs, covered in the next lesson) adds
  roughly another 100 bytes per business, or about 5 GB.

Both numbers are small — tens of gigabytes total, not the petabyte scale
of map tiles or video. That's a useful, counter-intuitive takeaway: **a
proximity/listings service is rarely storage-bound.** The challenge is
answering "what's near this point" fast at a few thousand queries per
second, not fitting the data somewhere — the core dataset comfortably
fits in memory across a modest cluster.

## Step 4: Turn estimates into design constraints

1. **The read path dominates every design decision.** At a 400:1
   read:write ratio, optimize aggressively for search latency and
   throughput; the write path just needs to be correct and eventually
   consistent, not blazing fast.
2. **The dataset is small enough to live in memory.** 25-30 GB of business
   data plus index overhead means a well-designed system can serve most or
   all of this from RAM across a small cluster — the bottleneck is query
   pattern, not disk I/O or total bytes.
3. **A naive bounding-box or full-table filter won't hit the 200ms
   budget.** At ~2,300 peak queries/sec against a growing candidate set
   per query, you need a structure purpose-built for "find nearby points"
   rather than a linear scan with a distance check on every row.
4. **Writes can propagate on a relaxed schedule.** Since business updates
   are rare and staleness of a minute or two is invisible to users, the
   write path can favor simplicity and availability over synchronous,
   strongly consistent index updates.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is reaching straight for `WHERE lat BETWEEN x1 AND x2
  AND lng BETWEEN y1 AND y2` and calling it a proximity search. Two
  problems: first, a naive bounding box on raw latitude/longitude columns
  can't be indexed efficiently by a standard B-tree once you filter on
  both dimensions at once, so it degrades toward a full scan as the table
  grows. Second, a degree of longitude represents a different physical
  distance depending on latitude — a box that's correct in size near the
  equator is far too wide near the poles. Both problems point to the same
  fix: use a structure designed for two-dimensional locality (covered in
  the geospatial indexing lesson), not a plain range filter on two
  independent columns.
</Callout>

## Worked example: what changes if the radius triples?

Suppose an interviewer asks: "What if we increase the default search
radius from 5 miles to 15 miles?" The number of searches per day doesn't
change — users still search 5 times a day on average — so average and
peak QPS stay at roughly 580/sec and 2,300/sec. What changes is the amount
of work each query does.

Search area scales with the square of the radius: tripling the radius (5
to 15 miles) multiplies the covered area by 3 squared, or 9x. Assuming
businesses are distributed with roughly similar density, the number of
candidate businesses a query has to consider before ranking the top 20
also grows roughly 9x. The response still returns the same 20 results, but
the underlying index now has to prune a much larger candidate set within
the same 200ms budget.

This is the distinction worth stating explicitly in an interview: **QPS
alone doesn't capture query cost.** A radius change that leaves request
volume untouched can still meaningfully increase the work per request,
and that's exactly the kind of change that should push you toward a
tiered or adaptive indexing structure rather than a fixed-resolution grid
that either wastes cells in sparse areas or gets overloaded in dense ones.

## Where this leads

Two numbers from this lesson carry into the rest of the module: the
roughly 400:1 read:write ratio, which justifies optimizing almost
exclusively for the search path, and the fact that the core dataset (tens
of gigabytes) is small enough to keep largely in memory. Both point toward
the same next question — what data structure actually answers "which
businesses are near this point" efficiently at a few thousand queries per
second — which is exactly what the geospatial indexing lesson covers next.
