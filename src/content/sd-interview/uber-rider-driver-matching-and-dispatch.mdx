The high-level design gave dispatch a box on the diagram labeled "matching
service" and moved on. This lesson opens that box. It's usually where an Uber
interview earns or loses its "strong hire" — anyone can say "find the nearest
driver," but the interesting part is what happens when three riders in the
same neighborhood request a ride within two seconds of each other, the
nearest driver just went offline mid-offer, and the system still has to
respond in under a second without double-booking anyone.

Matching and dispatch is fundamentally a real-time assignment problem under
uncertainty: driver locations are seconds stale, drivers can reject or ignore
offers, and the "best" match has to be computed fast enough that the rider
isn't staring at a spinner. Get the structure right and it scales cleanly;
get it wrong and you either starve riders of drivers who are technically
available, or you double-assign the same driver to two rides.

## Two-phase matching: find candidates, then rank them

The core idea that makes matching tractable at scale is splitting it into
two phases with very different cost profiles:

1. **Candidate generation** — cheaply find a small set of drivers who are
   plausibly close to the rider. This has to be fast and approximate.
2. **Scoring and assignment** — take that small set (tens of drivers, not
   thousands) and rank them by a more expensive, more accurate function,
   then offer the ride to the top candidate.

Skipping phase one and scoring every driver in the city against every
incoming request doesn't scale: a city with 50,000 active drivers and 500
requests per second can't afford 25 million distance calculations a second,
let alone anything richer than distance. Phase one exists purely to shrink
the problem before the expensive part runs.

### Candidate generation: geospatial indexing

Driver location updates (from the location-updates pipeline covered in the
next lesson) get written into an in-memory geospatial index — a geohash
grid or quadtree, keyed by driver ID, holding current lat/lng and an
"available" flag. A match request queries this index, not a database table:
a live radius query against a row-store scan is far too slow for a
sub-second dispatch budget.

The search itself should widen progressively rather than fix one radius for
the whole city:

- Start at a small radius (say `500m`) around the rider.
- If fewer candidates than needed come back (fewer than roughly 5-10),
  expand to the next ring and query again.
- Stop once there are enough candidates, or a max radius is hit — at which
  point the honest answer is "no drivers available nearby" rather than an
  indefinitely expanding search.

This matters because driver density varies enormously: a downtown request
at rush hour might have 40 available drivers within 500m, while a suburban
request at 2am might need a 5km radius before finding one. A single global
radius either wastes work scoring too many drivers downtown or fails
outright in the suburbs.

### Scoring and assignment

Once you have, say, 15-30 candidates, score each one and pick a winner. A
naive design scores purely on straight-line distance, but that's a weak
proxy for what riders actually experience — a driver 800m away separated by
a highway with no nearby exit can take longer to arrive than a driver 1.5km
away on a straight road. A more realistic score combines:

- **Estimated time to pickup** — ideally from the routing/ETA service (a
  road-network estimate, not straight-line distance).
- **Driver acceptance-rate history** — a driver who ignores or rejects most
  offers is a worse bet even if they're geographically closest, because
  offering to them burns time before falling back to the next candidate.
- **Driver rating** — a tie-breaker and a product-quality signal.
- **Idle time** — some designs weight toward drivers who've been waiting
  longest, for fairness and driver retention, at some cost to pure
  efficiency.

The system offers the ride to the top-scored candidate first, not to all
candidates at once — broadcasting to many drivers simultaneously creates a
race where multiple drivers might accept the same ride, which then requires
an arbitration step to undo. Sequential offering with a short timeout (a
few seconds) avoids that race entirely, at the cost of slightly higher
worst-case latency when the top candidate doesn't respond.

## Reservations, races, and idempotency

Between "offer sent" and "driver accepts," that driver has to be held out
of consideration for other requests, or two riders can both get assigned
the same driver. The standard fix is a short-TTL soft lock (a Redis key
with an expiry, or an equivalent) placed on the driver as soon as they're
selected as the top candidate:

- The lock has a TTL of a few seconds — long enough for the driver's app to
  respond, short enough that a dropped connection or crashed client doesn't
  strand the driver indefinitely.
- If the driver accepts, the lock converts into a real assignment and the
  driver is marked unavailable in the index.
- If the driver rejects, ignores, or the lock expires, it's released and
  the driver becomes eligible for the next request's candidate search.

This is also where dedup and idempotency earn their keep. A retried
dispatch message (from an at-least-once queue, a network retry, or a
duplicate event) must not create two separate assignments for the same
ride request — attach an idempotency key (ride request ID) to the
assignment write, so a duplicate message is a no-op rather than a second
booking. The same discipline you'd apply to a payment write applies here:
assigning a driver is a side effect with real-world consequences (a driver
physically drives somewhere), so "at least once delivery, exactly once
effect" is the target.

## Worked example: a request during a surge

A rider requests a ride in a dense downtown grid cell during evening rush.
The candidate generation step queries the geospatial index at a `500m`
radius and gets back 22 available drivers immediately — no widening
needed. The scoring step computes ETA for each (using cached road-network
segments rather than a live routing call per candidate, to keep this step
fast), factors in acceptance rate and rating, and ranks them. The top driver
gets an offer with a 4-second TTL lock. They don't respond in time — maybe
their phone lost signal in a tunnel — so the lock expires, that driver goes
back into the pool, and the system immediately offers to the second-ranked
driver, who accepts in 1.2 seconds. Total time from request to confirmed
driver: under 6 seconds, most of it spent waiting out the first driver's
timeout rather than doing computation. A well-tuned system keeps that
timeout short (a few seconds, not tens) specifically because this fallback
path is common at scale, not an edge case.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common interview mistake: describing matching as "query all drivers,
  sort by distance, assign the closest one" and stopping there. That design
  has no answer for what happens when the closest driver rejects the offer,
  no protection against two ride requests racing for the same driver, and
  no mechanism to prevent a driver from being offered a ride while another
  offer to them is still pending. Naming the candidate-generation step, the
  scoring step, and the reservation/lock mechanism separately — and being
  able to say what happens on rejection and duplicate delivery — is what
  separates a surface-level answer from a design that would actually work.
</Callout>

## Where this leaves you

Dispatch now has a concrete shape: a geospatial index feeds fast candidate
generation, a scoring function ranks a small candidate set on more than
raw distance, and short-TTL locks plus idempotent assignment writes keep
the system safe under retries and rejections. The next lesson goes one
layer deeper on the input this all depends on — how driver location updates
actually flow from phone GPS into that index in near real time, and the
trade-offs in update frequency, batching, and staleness that make the index
in this lesson trustworthy in the first place.
