"Design the frontend for a video streaming platform" is one of the most
common frontend system design prompts, and one of the easiest to answer
badly by rushing straight into "so we'll use HLS and a video tag." The
prompt is deliberately broad — it could mean YouTube, Netflix, Twitch, or an
internal training-video portal — and each of those implies a genuinely
different architecture. The first five minutes of this interview are about
narrowing "video streaming platform" down to a specific, buildable product,
not about picking a manifest format.

This lesson is the scoping pass for the case study. The next lessons
(architecture, deep dives, mock interview) all assume you've already pinned
down the scope decisions covered here — so treat this as the foundation, not
a throwaway warm-up.

## Why scoping this prompt matters more than usual

Video is unusual among system design prompts because the "hard part" moves
depending on which product you're building:

- A **YouTube-style** platform is dominated by adaptive bitrate playback,
  a recommendations feed, and comments/engagement UI.
- A **Netflix-style** platform adds DRM-protected premium content, multi-
  profile accounts, and cross-device resume.
- A **Twitch/live** platform is dominated by low end-to-end latency, live
  chat concurrency, and a fundamentally different buffering strategy (you
  can't scrub past the live edge).

If you don't ask which of these you're designing, you'll either over-build
(designing DRM for a prompt that never needed it) or under-build (missing
live-specific latency requirements entirely). Interviewers use this prompt
specifically to see whether you scope before you architect.

## Functional requirements

Once you've picked a concrete product shape (see clarifying questions
below), the core functional requirements for a video streaming frontend
are:

- **Video playback** — render a video stream with standard transport
  controls: play/pause, volume, fullscreen, and a progress indicator.
- **Adaptive quality** — the player selects and switches between multiple
  encoded quality levels (e.g. 360p to 4K) based on the viewer's network
  and device, ideally without a visible stutter.
- **Seek / scrub** — the viewer can drag the scrubber to jump to an
  arbitrary point (VOD) or within a live/DVR window (live), and playback
  resumes quickly from the new position even if that segment range wasn't
  buffered yet.
- **Recommendations rail** — a list of related or "up next" videos shown
  alongside or after the current video, driving continued watch time.
- **Comments** — a paginated, possibly-threaded discussion feed attached to
  the video, loaded and rendered without blocking the player.
- **Playback controls beyond the basics** — captions/subtitles toggle,
  playback speed, and quality override are common enough to mention as
  "likely in scope" even if the interviewer doesn't ask for them
  explicitly.

Cross-device resume (starting a video on a phone and continuing on a TV
from the same position) is functional too, but it's specifically a data-
sync requirement — worth flagging as a clarifying question rather than
assuming it's needed.

## Nonfunctional requirements

The nonfunctional requirements are what actually make this prompt hard, and
they're where most of your design decisions later will be justified from:

- **Startup latency** — time from "user clicks play" to "first frame is
  rendered." For VOD this is typically budgeted in the low seconds; for
  live, viewers are even less patient because they expect "instant," and a
  slow join is often read as broken. A concrete target you can state out
  loud: "under 2 seconds to first frame on a good connection" is a
  reasonable bar to defend.
- **Smooth playback on variable networks** — the player must keep frames
  coming with minimal rebuffering as available bandwidth fluctuates, which
  is the entire reason adaptive bitrate streaming exists. Rebuffer ratio
  (fraction of watch time spent stalled) is the metric to name here.
- **Cross-device support** — the same watch experience needs to work across
  phone, tablet, desktop browser, and often TV/set-top apps, each with
  different input models (touch scrub vs. D-pad) and different hardware
  decode capabilities.
- **Resource efficiency** — video decode and rendering are CPU/GPU and
  battery intensive; a frontend that also runs a heavy recommendations rail
  and live comment stream needs to budget main-thread work so none of that
  UI causes the video itself to drop frames.
- **Accessibility** — captions/subtitles are frequently a legal requirement,
  not a nice-to-have, and keyboard-operable controls matter for a full-
  screen player where a mouse may not be in play.

## Clarifying questions and how they shift scope

Ask these early — each one changes the architecture you'd propose, not just
a small detail.

### Live vs. on-demand?

This is the single biggest fork. On-demand means the whole video's manifest
and duration are known upfront, so scrubbing anywhere and precise buffer
health tracking are straightforward. Live means the manifest is a moving
window (new segments keep appearing), latency to the "live edge" becomes a
top metric, and scrubbing is usually limited to a bounded DVR window rather
than the entire broadcast. If the interviewer says "both," treat it as two
variants of the same player shell and say so explicitly, rather than
designing one generic system that quietly only fits one case.

### Is DRM or premium/paid content in scope?

If some or all content is protected, the player can't just point a
`<video>` element at a plain file URL — you need an encrypted media
pipeline (conceptually: Encrypted Media Extensions and a content decryption
module talking to a license server) before playback can even start. That's
a meaningfully larger design than open playback, and it affects your
startup-latency budget too, since a license request adds a round trip
before the first frame. If DRM is out of scope, say so and move on — don't
spend interview time designing key rotation for a prompt that didn't ask
for it.

### Are offline downloads required?

Downloads mean storing encrypted or plain video segments locally, tracking
storage quota, renewing time-limited licenses for downloaded DRM content,
and reconciling "downloaded but stale" content when a video is updated or
removed. This is a genuinely separate subsystem from the streaming
architecture core lessons cover — worth a single sentence acknowledging it
exists, but not worth designing in depth unless the interviewer confirms
it's in scope.

### What's the recommendations/comments load pattern?

Is the recommendations rail personalized per-viewer (implying a
server round trip per page load) or largely static per-video? Are comments
expected to update in near-real-time (implying a subscription or polling
channel) or just paginated on load? These answers decide whether the watch
page needs extra live-update infrastructure alongside the player.

### What device/platform surfaces are in scope?

"Cross-device" could mean "responsive web only" or "web plus native TV
apps sharing a design." If native TV/console apps are explicitly out of
scope, say that up front — it's fine to focus the whole interview on web
and mention that a TV app would reuse the same ABR and metadata concepts
behind a different UI shell.

## Worked example: scoping a concrete answer

Suppose the interviewer opens with: "Design the frontend for a video
streaming platform, similar to YouTube." A strong opening isn't "okay,
we'll need a video player and adaptive streaming" — it's a scoping pass:

> "Before I design anything, a few questions. Is this purely on-demand, or
> is live streaming also in scope? I'll assume on-demand only for now unless
> you say otherwise. Is any content DRM-protected or paywalled, or is
> everything freely playable? Do we need offline downloads, or is this
> online-only? And for cross-device — are we designing responsive web only,
> or do native TV/mobile apps matter for this exercise?"

Say the interviewer answers: on-demand only, no DRM for this exercise,
no downloads, web-responsive only (desktop and mobile browser, not native
apps). That's now a fully scoped, buildable problem: a single-page watch
experience with an adaptive video player, a scrub bar over a known-duration
manifest, a recommendations rail, and a comments feed, targeting fast
startup and smooth playback on typical consumer networks, across desktop
and mobile browser viewports. Everything in the next three lessons builds
on exactly this scoped version — see
`video-streaming-high-level-architecture` for how these requirements map to
components, and `video-streaming-deep-dives` for how adaptive quality
switching and network resilience actually work under the hood.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake on this prompt is skipping straight to "we'll use
  HLS/DASH with a video tag" before establishing live vs. on-demand, or
  before checking whether DRM is in scope. Both of those answers change
  your architecture enough that designing first and clarifying later means
  redoing work live, in front of the interviewer. Spend the first two or
  three minutes narrowing scope out loud — it's the single highest-leverage
  thing you can do in this case study, and it's exactly what separates a
  senior answer from a junior one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
  It's fine — expected, even — to explicitly narrow scope and state your
  assumption ("I'll assume on-demand, no DRM, web only, unless told
  otherwise") rather than waiting for the interviewer to answer every
  question. This shows judgment and keeps the interview moving, and the
  interviewer can always correct you if your assumption is wrong.
</Callout>
