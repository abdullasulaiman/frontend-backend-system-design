"Design a calendar application" is a deceptively small-sounding prompt.
Most people picture a grid of boxes and start sketching a month view
within the first minute. But a calendar frontend has to get three genuinely
hard things right at once: rendering a lot of small events into a
constrained grid without the layout collapsing, expanding recurring events
into concrete instances correctly, and displaying times correctly across
timezones and daylight-saving transitions. Any one of these done sloppily
produces a calendar that looks fine in the demo and is subtly wrong in
production — an event that silently shows at the wrong hour after a DST
change, or a "every Tuesday" meeting that vanishes from one attendee's view
because of an off-by-one in the recurrence math.

This lesson is the scoping pass. Before touching architecture or
component design, we'll separate what the app has to *do* (functional
requirements), what has to be *true* about how it behaves (nonfunctional
requirements), and the handful of clarifying questions that determine how
big each of those two lists actually is for your specific interview.

## Why scope swings so hard on this prompt

A calendar app can be a tiny personal to-do grid or it can be
Google-Calendar-scale: multiple visible calendars layered together, shared
events with attendees in different timezones, recurring series with
per-instance overrides, and offline-first editing that has to reconcile
later. The functional shape looks similar at every scale — a grid, events,
an editor — but the *hard parts* only show up once you add recurrence,
multiple timezones, or shared calendars. An interviewer who says "just a
single user's personal calendar, no sharing" is asking a much shorter
question than one who says "team calendars with overlapping events across
timezones." Spending two or three minutes locking down scope tells you
which of those interviews you're actually in.

## Functional requirements

For the canonical "design a calendar app" prompt, a solid functional scope
covers:

- **Multiple views: day, week, and month.** Each view is a different
  rendering problem. A day view has plenty of vertical space per event; a
  week view compresses seven days side by side; a month view has to fit an
  entire day's events into a cell a few dozen pixels tall, which usually
  means showing the first few events plus a "+3 more" affordance.
- **Event CRUD.** Create, read, update, and delete an event, including the
  core fields: title, start/end time, location, description, and
  attendees. The editor is typically a modal or side panel, not a full
  page navigation.
- **Recurring events.** "Every Monday at 9am," "the last Friday of every
  month," "every day until a given date." This is one axis where scope can
  legitimately be cut — "no recurrence, every event is one-off" is a valid
  answer that removes a large chunk of complexity.
- **Multi-timezone display.** At minimum, rendering all events correctly
  in the viewer's local timezone; at a more advanced scope, showing a
  secondary timezone (e.g., a "world clock" strip) or letting a user view
  the calendar as if they were in a different timezone.
- **Shared and overlapping calendars.** Viewing more than one calendar's
  events overlaid on the same grid — your own calendar plus a teammate's,
  or a personal calendar plus a work calendar — which means the grid has
  to merge events from multiple sources and lay out ones that overlap in
  time without visually stacking them illegibly.

What's usually **out of scope** unless stated otherwise: the actual invite
and notification delivery system (emailing attendees, push reminders),
calendar-account provisioning (OAuth linking Google/Outlook accounts), and
free/busy scheduling-assistant features like "find a time that works for
everyone." Naming these out loud — "I'll treat sending invite emails as a
solved backend concern and focus on rendering and editing" — keeps the
interview focused on the frontend problem.

## Nonfunctional requirements

The functional list above is fairly stable across calendar products. The
nonfunctionals are where a calendar app earns its place as a distinct case
study — they're less about raw scale and more about correctness and
responsiveness under a surprisingly tricky data model.

### Fast view switching

Clicking from month to week to day, or paging to the next week, has to
feel instant — this is a direct-manipulation UI, and a visible loading
spinner every time someone clicks "next week" reads as broken. That
pushes you toward prefetching and caching event data for date ranges
adjacent to what's currently visible, so a view switch is usually a cache
hit rather than a fresh network round trip.

### Correctness of recurrence math

A recurring event has to expand into exactly the right set of concrete
instances for whatever date range is on screen — no duplicates, no
missing instances, and instances that respect exceptions (a single
occurrence moved or deleted without affecting the rest of the series).
Getting this wrong doesn't crash anything; it quietly shows the wrong
data, which is worse because nobody notices until a meeting gets missed.

### Correctness of timezone math

Every event has to render at the correct wall-clock time for whoever is
viewing it, including across a daylight-saving transition — an event
created before a DST change has to still land on the correct hour when
viewed after one. This is a common source of real production bugs and a
common interview probe.

### Offline editing

Calendars get checked and edited from a phone with inconsistent
connectivity, so offline edits (create, move, delete) should apply
locally and sync once connectivity returns rather than failing outright.
That implies optimistic local writes plus a conflict strategy for when a
synced edit collides with a change made elsewhere in the meantime.

## The clarifying questions to ask

### "Is this a single user's personal calendar, or do we need shared / overlapping calendars?"

A single-user scope means one event source feeds the grid — comparatively
simple. Shared calendars mean the grid renders events merged from multiple
sources (your calendar, a teammate's, a room's booking calendar), which
forces two extra problems: color-coding events by which calendar they
belong to, and laying out events that overlap in time from *different*
sources without becoming an unreadable pile. If the interviewer says
"shared," say you'll design the single-calendar rendering path first and
then generalize to merged sources — stronger sequencing than designing
for N calendars from the first sketch.

### "Are recurring events in scope?"

This is the single biggest scope lever in this case study. "No recurrence"
means every event is a standalone row with a start and end time — no
recurrence-rule storage, no instance expansion, no per-instance exception
handling. "Yes" pulls in a real data-modeling decision (do you store a
rule like "every Monday" and expand it on demand, or precompute and store
every instance?) that the architecture and deep-dive lessons in this
module spend real time on. If recurrence is in scope, also ask whether
editing a single instance of a series (as opposed to the whole series)
needs to be supported — that's a meaningfully different editor UX.

### "Which views are required — day, week, month, or all three?"

Month view alone is the cheapest: you're mostly solving "fit a summary of
a day's events into a small cell." Add week or day view and you now need
fine-grained overlapping-event layout — two 3pm meetings need to render
as side-by-side columns within the same hour, not stacked illegibly. If
only one view is required, say so and skip designing layout logic the
other views would need; if all three are required, note that the
underlying event-expansion logic should be shared across views, with only
the rendering/layout differing per view.

### A few second-tier questions worth asking if time allows

- "Do events need per-attendee timezones, or just correct display for the
  single viewer?" (Adds a "shown in attendee's local time" annotation.)
- "Is drag-to-resize/reschedule in scope, or is editing form-only?" (Adds
  direct-manipulation gesture handling on top of CRUD.)
- "Do all-day and multi-day events need support?" (Both need distinct
  rendering from a normal timed event, especially in week/month views.)

## Worked example: how two sets of answers diverge

Suppose the interviewer says: "single user, no shared calendars; recurring
events are in scope; only week and month views are required."

That tells you:

- You skip multi-calendar merging and overlap-by-source color-coding
  entirely — one event source feeds the grid.
- You do need a recurrence-rule data model and an expansion step that
  turns "every Tuesday at 2pm" into concrete instances for whatever date
  range each view is currently showing, including handling a single
  edited-or-deleted occurrence as an exception to the rule.
- You need overlapping-event layout for week view (side-by-side columns
  within an hour) and dense-cell "+N more" handling for month view, but
  you can skip day view's finer-grained layout concerns.

Now contrast with: "shared team calendars — up to five overlaid at once;
no recurring events; day view only." That's a very different fifteen
minutes: no recurrence-rule modeling at all, but a real design problem
merging five event sources into one grid, color-coding by source, and
solving overlap layout at the granularity a single busy day demands (many
short meetings back to back). Same opening prompt, two different scopes,
purely from three answers.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is jumping straight into "I'll store `start` and `end`
  as local date-time strings and render them directly" without asking
  about timezones or recurrence first. Local-string storage breaks the
  moment a second timezone or a DST transition is in scope — you'll need
  to redo your entire data model mid-interview. Ask about single-vs-shared
  calendars, recurrence, and required views *before* you commit to a data
  model, not after you've already sketched one.
</Callout>
