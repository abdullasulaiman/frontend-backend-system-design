"Design the frontend for a ride-hailing app" sounds like a single prompt,
but it's really three or four different products wearing the same map.
Uber's rider app, Uber's driver app, and Uber Eats all share a location
pipeline and a state-machine mental model, yet each has a different UI, a
different battery budget, and a different tolerance for stale data. If you
start sketching screens before pinning down which of these you're actually
building, you'll spend half the interview redesigning something the
interviewer never asked for.

This lesson is the scoping pass for that prompt. We'll separate the
functional requirements (what the app has to let a rider or driver do),
the nonfunctional requirements (what has to be true about location data,
mobile resource use, and network conditions for the app to feel
trustworthy), and the clarifying questions that pin down which version of
the problem you're solving in the first few minutes.

## Why scope matters more here than in most case studies

Most frontend system design prompts are primarily about data and rendering.
A ride-hailing app adds a third axis that most other case studies don't
have at all: **physical reality drifts and lags**. A phone's GPS reading is
never exactly correct, a driver's phone can lose signal in a tunnel, and a
location update sent two seconds ago is already stale by the time it
renders. None of that is a bug to be fixed — it's the baseline condition
the whole design has to be built around. An interviewer using this prompt
is listening for whether you treat "the map shows a driver where they
currently are" as an engineering problem with real constraints, not as a
solved primitive you can wave past.

Two or three minutes of scoping up front — rider, driver, or both; how
precise the ETA needs to be; how many ride types exist — tells you exactly
how hard that problem needs to be for your specific interview.

## Functional requirements

For the canonical "design a ride-hailing app" prompt, a solid functional
scope covers:

- **A map-based matching UI.** The rider sees a map, drops or confirms a
  pickup location, and — once a driver is assigned — sees that driver's
  position moving toward them in near-real time.
- **Live location tracking.** Both the rider's own position (to set
  pickup) and the assigned driver's position (to show progress) need to be
  reflected on the map as they change, not just fetched once.
- **Fare estimation.** Before confirming a request, the rider sees an
  estimated price (and often a price range or multiple ride-type options),
  computed from pickup, destination, and current conditions like surge
  pricing.
- **Trip-state transitions driving the UI.** The screen a rider or driver
  sees changes as the trip moves through distinct states — requesting a
  ride, matched with a driver, driver en route to pickup, trip in
  progress, trip completed — and each state has its own map view, its own
  available actions (cancel, contact driver, rate trip), and its own
  copy.

What's usually **out of scope** unless the interviewer says otherwise:
payment processing itself (card entry, charging), driver
onboarding/background-check flows, in-app chat or support ticketing, and
promotions/referral systems. Say this explicitly — "I'll treat payment
capture as a solved backend integration and focus on the trip and
location experience" — so the interviewer can redirect you if they
disagree.

### Rider app vs. driver app: not the same problem

A rider app is mostly passive: watch a map, wait for state to change,
occasionally tap a button. A driver app is active and always-on during a
shift: it needs to keep tracking location even when the screen is off or
another app is in the foreground, it has a persistent turn-by-turn
navigation view, and it needs a "go online/offline" toggle with no rider
equivalent. If the prompt is "design the rider experience," background
location and continuous-navigation battery use are secondary concerns. If
it includes the driver side, they become first-class requirements.

## Nonfunctional requirements

The functional list above is fairly stable across ride-hailing products.
The nonfunctionals are where this case study earns its place as a distinct
lesson from a newsfeed or chat app — they're dominated by the realities of
a mobile device tracking physical movement, not by data volume.

### Low-latency location updates

The core promise of the UI is "this map reflects where the driver actually
is, right now." That means:

- Updates need to arrive frequently enough that driver movement on the map
  reads as continuous motion, not a car teleporting between points every
  few seconds.
- The gap between an update arriving and the map reflecting it needs to
  stay small enough that a rider watching the screen doesn't notice a lag
  between "driver turns onto my street" and the map showing that turn.

### Resilience to GPS and network flakiness

Phones lose GPS lock in tunnels, parking garages, and dense urban canyons,
and lose network connectivity switching between cell towers or entering a
dead zone. A ride-hailing frontend has to keep the UI coherent through
all of that:

- A missed or delayed location update shouldn't make the driver's marker
  visibly jump or disappear — the UI needs a strategy for "we haven't
  heard from the driver in N seconds" that's graceful, not alarming.
- A trip's state has to survive a dropped connection and reconnect cleanly
  — a rider who loses signal for ten seconds mid-trip should come back to
  the correct current state, not a stale or broken one.

### Battery and network efficiency on mobile

This app runs for the entire duration of a trip (rider side) or an entire
shift (driver side), often as the foreground app on a phone that also
needs enough battery left to, say, get the rider home. That constrains the
design:

- Location can't be sampled and transmitted at maximum GPS precision and
  frequency at all times — the update cadence and precision need to trade
  off against battery and data cost deliberately, not by accident.
- Map re-renders triggered by location updates need to be cheap; a map
  that repaints heavily on every update burns battery and can visibly
  stutter on a mid-range phone.

### "Are we designing the rider app, the driver app, or both?"

This is the highest-leverage question, and it's specific to this case
study. A **rider-only** scope means you can treat location tracking as
"receive updates and render them" — a comparatively contained problem. A
**driver app**, or both sides, pulls in background location tracking
(keeping GPS live while the screen is off or the app is backgrounded),
a persistent navigation view, and a distinct state machine for the
driver's own availability (`offline` to `online` to `en route to pickup` to
`on trip`). If the interviewer says "both," say out loud that you'll
design the shared trip-state machine and location pipeline once, then
note where each app's UI diverges — that's a stronger answer than
designing two apps from scratch.

### "How precise does the real-time ETA and driver position need to be?"

An ETA that only needs to be right to within a couple of minutes tolerates
infrequent, coarse location updates — say, every 10-15 seconds — with the
UI just showing a number that updates occasionally. An ETA (and driver
marker position) that needs to feel accurate to within a city block
requires far more frequent samples and, critically, **client-side
interpolation** between samples so the car appears to move continuously
on the map rather than jumping. This single answer determines whether your
location-pipeline deep dive is a paragraph or a significant chunk of the
interview.

### "Is there one ride type, or does the rider choose among several (economy, XL, pool, etc.)?"

A single ride type keeps the request flow to "confirm pickup and
destination, see one fare, request." Multiple ride types add a selection
UI showing several options side by side, each with its own fare estimate
and ETA, and usually a comparison view. It's a meaningful but contained
addition — it changes the request screen and the fare-estimation data
shape, not the underlying trip-state machine or location pipeline.

### A few second-tier questions worth asking if time allows

- "Does the rider see multiple nearby drivers before a match, or does
  matching happen entirely server-side?" (Changes whether the map ever
  shows more than one moving driver marker at once.)
- "Is scheduling a ride for later in scope, or only on-demand rides?"
  (Adds a distinct pre-trip flow with its own state before "requesting.")
- "Does the app need to degrade gracefully on a very poor connection, as
  a baseline expectation in the target market?" (Raises how aggressively
  you cache trip state and handle gaps.)

## Worked example: how two sets of answers diverge

Suppose the interviewer says: "rider app only; ETA precision at the
minute level is fine, this isn't a delivery-tracking app; and yes, there
are three ride types to choose from."

That tells you:

- You can skip background-location and driver-navigation design entirely
  — the whole lesson is the rider's map, matching, and trip states.
- Your location pipeline can be comparatively simple: periodic updates
  (say, every 8-10 seconds) rendered directly, without needing to build
  interpolation logic for smooth continuous motion.
- You do need a clean ride-type selection UI and a fare-estimate data
  shape that scales to several simultaneous options, which becomes a
  meaningful chunk of your component design.

Now contrast with: "both rider and driver apps; ETA needs to feel
accurate to the block; single ride type only." That's a much heavier
location-and-state-machine interview: real time on background location
tracking, an interpolation strategy so the map reads as smooth motion,
and the shared trip-state machine driving two different UIs — and
little time on ride-type selection, since there's only one. Same opening
prompt, two very different fifteen-minute deep dives, from three answers.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is skipping straight to "I'll poll the driver's location
  every few seconds and update the marker" without asking how precise the
  ETA needs to be. If the interviewer actually wants block-level accuracy,
  raw periodic updates will make the driver's marker visibly teleport
  between positions — you'll need interpolation between samples to get
  smooth motion, which is a meaningfully different design than "just
  poll and re-render." Ask the precision question before committing to an
  update strategy.
</Callout>
