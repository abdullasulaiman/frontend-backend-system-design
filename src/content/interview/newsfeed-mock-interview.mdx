If it's been a while since you read those lessons, skim them again before starting the timer below — the mock interview assumes you can produce that content cold, the way a real interviewer would expect, not that you're re-deriving it for the first time under pressure.

The point isn't to memorize a script. It's to practice the thing that actually separates a strong system design answer from a weak one: managing your own time, structuring an answer before you start talking, and proactively surfacing trade-offs instead of waiting to be asked. Treat this like the real thing — set a timer, talk out loud (or write as if you were), and only look at the rubric after you've committed to an answer.

## How to run this mock interview

Give yourself 35-40 minutes, uninterrupted. Structure your time roughly like this:

- **Minutes 0-5**: Ask clarifying questions and state the requirements you're designing for.
- **Minutes 5-15**: Present a high-level architecture — component tree, data flow, rendering strategy.
- **Minutes 15-30**: Go deep on two or three of the hardest sub-problems (pagination, virtualization, optimistic updates, media loading) and explicitly name edge cases.
- **Minutes 30-40**: Discuss performance and accessibility trade-offs, and leave room for the interviewer's follow-up questions.

A common failure mode is spending 20 minutes on requirements and diagrams and then rushing the deep dive, which is usually where the real signal is. Watch your own clock.

Read the prompt in the `<MockInterview>` box below once, then start your timer before you look at anything else on this page. Jot notes as you go — the component below gives you a scratchpad for that. Only click "reveal" once you've talked through a complete answer, even a rough one. When you're done, score yourself honestly against each rubric point rather than against a vague "did I do okay" feeling — the self-score is only useful if it's calibrated to specifics.

## What "strong" looks like, briefly

Before you dive in, here's the shape a strong answer takes, without giving away the content: it opens with 2-3 sharp clarifying questions (not five generic ones), states the resulting scope as constraints ("I'll design for a ranked, media-heavy feed with near-real-time updates"), walks through a component/data architecture with a diagram, picks a pagination strategy and defends it against the alternative, names the specific edge cases that strategy creates, and closes with concrete performance numbers or accessibility considerations rather than "make it fast and accessible."

Weak answers tend to do the opposite: they skip clarifying questions and design for an imagined "generic" feed, they describe components without describing how data moves between them, and they mention "we'd use pagination" and "we'd virtualize the list" without ever explaining why or what breaks if you don't.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake in this exact interview is treating pagination and virtualization as a single throwaway sentence ("we'd paginate and virtualize the list") and then spending all remaining time on visual design or styling. Both of those are genuinely hard problems with real trade-offs — cursor vs offset pagination interacts directly with the "new posts inserted at top" edge case, and virtualization interacts directly with variable-height media cards. An interviewer who hears the throwaway sentence will almost always follow up by asking you to go deeper — have that depth ready rather than discovering the gap live.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
  If you get stuck mid-answer, narrate your uncertainty instead of going silent: "I'm trading off freshness for simplicity here by refetching page one on pull-to-refresh instead of merging a live delta — that's simpler but can duplicate items the user already saw." Interviewers consistently rate candidates who reason out loud about a trade-off higher than candidates who go quiet while deciding, even when the final decision is the same.
</Callout>

## Worked example: how a strong answer opens

To make the abstract advice above concrete, here's roughly how the first two minutes of a strong answer sound (paraphrased, not a script to copy verbatim):

"Before I design anything, three questions. First, is this a chronological feed or an algorithmically ranked one — that changes whether I can trust a simple cursor on `createdAt`, or whether I need a ranking service in the loop. Second, do we need near-real-time updates, like a 'new posts' banner, or is periodic refresh acceptable? Third, is this feed media-heavy — images and video — or mostly text, since that changes my loading and virtualization strategy significantly."

Suppose the interviewer answers: ranked feed, no hard real-time requirement but a "new posts available" affordance is expected, and yes, media-heavy with mixed image and video posts. A strong candidate immediately restates this back as design constraints before touching architecture: "Okay — so I'll design for a ranked feed where the server decides order, an eventually-consistent 'N new posts' banner instead of live streaming, and a media pipeline that has to handle images and video without blocking scroll." That one sentence proves the clarifying questions weren't decorative — everything that follows visibly depends on the answers.
