You've scoped a calendar application, sketched an architecture around a date-range query and an event-expansion layer, and gone deep on recurrence storage, timezone/DST correctness, and dense-view rendering across the previous three lessons. This lesson is where all of that gets tested under interview conditions: one open-ended prompt, a strict time box, and a rubric you'd actually be graded against.

The goal isn't reciting the previous lessons back verbatim. It's practicing the specific skill that separates a strong calendar-app answer from a mediocre one: naming a concrete recurrence data model instead of waving at "recurring events," and having a real answer for what happens to a `9:00 AM` meeting when the clock changes for daylight saving time instead of saying "we'd convert timezones." Set a timer, talk out loud as if an interviewer is sitting across from you, and only look at the rubric after you've committed to a full answer.

## How to run this mock interview

Give yourself 35-40 minutes, uninterrupted. A reasonable time split:

- **Minutes 0-5**: Clarifying questions and stated scope.
- **Minutes 5-15**: High-level architecture — view/grid rendering layer, event data layer, and how a visible date range turns into rendered event chips.
- **Minutes 15-30**: Deep dive on two or three of the hardest sub-problems: recurring-event data model and expansion, timezone/DST correctness, and rendering performance for dense views.
- **Minutes 30-40**: Edge cases, trade-offs, and leaving room for follow-ups.

The most common failure mode on this specific prompt is spending most of the interview drawing a clean grid of days and weeks, then discovering with a few minutes left that you haven't said anything concrete about how "every weekday at 9am, except the one you moved to 10am next Tuesday" actually gets stored and rendered. The grid is necessary but not sufficient — the deep dive on recurrence and timezones is where the signal is.

## What "strong" looks like, briefly

A strong answer opens with two or three questions that actually change the design — not "what browsers do we support" as a throwaway, but things like "is this a single-user calendar, or do I need shared calendars with overlapping events across multiple people's schedules, since that changes both data fetching and rendering" and "do recurring events need to be in scope, since a recurrence engine is a meaningfully different problem from single events." It then restates the answers as constraints before drawing anything.

From there, it presents an architecture with a clear seam between two layers: a grid/view-rendering layer responsible for day, week, and month layouts, and an event data layer responsible for turning a requested date range into concrete event instances the grid can place. A strong candidate is explicit that these are separate concerns — the grid doesn't know or care whether an event chip came from a one-off event or a recurrence rule; it just receives instances with a start and end.

The deep dive is where weak answers fall apart. On recurrence, a strong candidate names the actual storage shape: the recurrence rule (frequency, interval, days of week, end condition) is stored once per series, plus a small table of per-instance overrides for edits like "move just this Tuesday's occurrence to 10am" or "cancel just this one." Expansion — turning the rule into concrete instances with real start/end times — happens only for the date range currently visible, and the candidate states whether that expansion runs on the server (better for shared calendars and consistency across clients) or the client (fine for a small personal range, but doesn't scale to years of history or heavy sharing) and why they picked one.

On timezones, a strong candidate states the one rule that makes everything else consistent: every instant is stored in UTC, the event also stores the timezone it was authored in (needed to correctly recompute recurring instances across a DST boundary), and conversion to the viewer's local time happens only at render time, never baked into storage. They can explain, concretely, why a recurring 9am meeting must be recomputed instance-by-instance against the calendar's timezone rather than storing one fixed UTC offset for the whole series — otherwise the meeting silently drifts to 8am or 10am the moment DST kicks in.

On performance, a strong candidate names a concrete layout algorithm for a dense month view with many overlapping events: bucket events into overlap groups by interval, assign each event a column/lane within its group, and cap the number of visibly rendered chips per day cell with a "+N more" affordance rather than rendering every event and letting the cell grow unbounded.

Weak answers substitute vague verbs for mechanisms: "we'd handle recurring events," "we'd convert timezones," "we'd optimize rendering for busy days" — each true in spirit, none of it demonstrating that the candidate has thought through what actually breaks and how the design handles it.

<Callout type="warning">
  The single most common gap in this interview is answering "how do you handle a recurring event across a DST transition" with just "we store the UTC time and convert it for display." That breaks the moment the recurrence crosses the DST boundary: a fixed UTC instant for a "daily at 9am local" meeting will render as 9am before the transition and 8am or 10am after it, because the UTC offset for that local time changed. A strong answer recomputes each instance's UTC time from the recurrence rule and the calendar's timezone at expansion time, so the *local* time stays fixed at 9am and the UTC instant shifts instead — that's the correct direction for the invariant to hold.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
  If the interviewer pushes on shared calendars ("what if two people's calendars need to render side by side, and one event is time-blocked while another is optional?"), don't freeze — narrate the trade-off out loud: "I'd fetch each calendar's instances for the visible range independently, tag each instance with its source calendar, and let the grid's layout algorithm treat them as one combined set of intervals for overlap purposes, while keeping a per-calendar color or lane so the viewer can still tell whose event is whose. The cost is that the client now needs to merge and re-sort combined results from multiple sources instead of trusting one ordered response, but that's what makes side-by-side comparison actually work." Reasoning about the cost, not just naming the feature, is what interviewers listen for.
</Callout>

## Worked example: how a strong answer opens

Here's roughly how the first two minutes of a strong answer might sound (paraphrased — not a script to copy):

"Before I design anything, a few questions. First, is this a single-user calendar, or do people need to view and overlay shared or team calendars — that changes both the data-fetching shape and how I lay out overlapping events. Second, are recurring events in scope, since expanding recurrence rules into instances is a meaningfully different problem from just rendering a list of fixed-time events. Third, which views do I need to support — day, week, month, or all three — since month view is where dense-rendering performance actually becomes a concern."

Suppose the interviewer answers: shared calendars are in scope for viewing (not editing others' events), recurring events are in scope with the ability to edit a single instance, and all three views are required. A strong candidate restates that immediately: "Okay — so I'm designing a grid that renders day, week, and month views from a shared pool of possibly-overlapping events, where some events are recurring series that need to expand correctly per visible range and correctly across DST, and viewers can see but not edit calendars that aren't their own." Every architectural decision that follows should visibly trace back to that sentence — the recurrence engine, the timezone handling, and the overlap layout all get justified by it rather than appearing out of nowhere.
