The architecture from the previous lesson tells you *what* pieces exist:
a manifest, a set of bitrate renditions, a buffer, a player shell. This
lesson is about the runtime decisions that actually determine whether
playback feels smooth or janky — which quality level to request right
now, how far ahead to buffer, what happens when the network drops mid-play,
and how to keep a 2-hour video resumable across a phone, a laptop, and a
smart TV. These are the questions interviewers push on once the high-level
diagram is on the whiteboard, because they separate "I know HLS/DASH
exist" from "I understand why players stutter and how to stop it."

## Adaptive bitrate switching logic

Every ABR algorithm answers one question on a loop: given what I know
right now, which of the available renditions (240p/500kbps up to
4K/25mbps, say) should I request next? Two signals dominate that decision.

### Signal 1: measured bandwidth

The player times how long each segment download takes and divides bytes
by seconds to get a throughput sample. Because a single sample is noisy
(a segment might download fast because it briefly raced ahead of a
congestion event, or slow because of an unrelated background sync), real
players use a moving average — often an exponentially weighted one that
favors recent samples but doesn't overreact to a single blip. The
resulting estimate then gets discounted (many players target 70-90% of
estimated throughput as the ceiling for the next rendition) to leave
headroom, because segment download time competes with other traffic on
the same connection.

### Signal 2: buffer health

Throughput alone is a trap: it tells you what the network did in the
past few seconds, not what it's about to do. Buffer health — how many
seconds of already-downloaded video are sitting ahead of the playhead —
is the signal that tells you how much room you have to be wrong. A
buffer-based approach (conceptually similar to what's used in real HLS/DASH
clients) maps buffer level to a target quality: below a low watermark
(say 5s), always request the lowest safe rendition regardless of
bandwidth, because the priority is refilling the buffer before it empties.
Above a high watermark (say 20-30s), it's safe to be more aggressive,
because even a bad next segment has time to be corrected before playback
catches up.

### Combining both

Production ABR logic blends the two: bandwidth estimate sets an upper
bound on what's fetchable in time, buffer trend decides how conservative
to be within that bound. A rising buffer with healthy bandwidth allows
stepping up; a falling buffer — even with bandwidth that still looks
okay — should trigger a step down *before* the buffer actually runs out.
This is the detail that separates "I read the acronym ABR" from
understanding it: reacting only after a rebuffer already happened is too
late — the goal is to downgrade on a falling buffer trend, pre-emptively.

## A buffering and preloading strategy

Buffer target size is a trade-off, not a fixed number. A larger forward
buffer (30-60s) absorbs longer network dips before the user sees a spinner,
but costs more data and memory, and makes quality switches feel "laggy"
since a lot of already-buffered video is at the old quality. A smaller
buffer (5-10s) switches quality faster and wastes less data if the user
abandons the video early, but has almost no cushion against a network
blip. Most players target something like a 10-15s steady-state buffer,
with logic to grow it opportunistically when bandwidth is abundant and
shrink the target on metered/cellular connections.

Preloading extends past the current buffer:

- **Within the current video**: keep 2-3 segments ahead fetched at all
  times so a single slow segment doesn't stall playback — this is what
  the buffer *is*, effectively a queue of pre-fetched, pre-decoded chunks.
- **Across videos**: on a watch page with an autoplay-next queue or a
  recommendations rail, prefetching just the manifest (not full segments)
  of the next likely video lets playback start near-instantly if the
  user does move on, without spending bandwidth on segments that might
  never play. This mirrors the "fetch metadata eagerly, fetch heavy bytes
  lazily" pattern from the Image & Media Optimization lesson
  (`image-and-media-optimization`) — apply the same instinct here: the
  manifest is cheap and worth prefetching, the video bytes are expensive
  and should wait for real intent.
- **Connection-aware budgets**: on a detected slow or metered connection
  (via the Network Information API where available, or simply a low
  measured throughput), shrink both the buffer target and the
  cross-video prefetch aggressiveness — the same adaptive-loading
  philosophy covered in the Network Optimization lesson
  (`network-optimization`) applies directly to video: don't spend a
  scarce, slow connection's budget on content the user hasn't asked for.

## Resuming playback across devices

Cross-device resume needs two things: a durable position record, and a
policy for reconciling conflicts when the same video was watched on two
devices.

- **Write cadence**: persist `{ videoId, positionSeconds, quality,
  updatedAt }` to the backend periodically (every 5-10 seconds), on pause,
  and on page unload (`visibilitychange`/`beforeunload`) — not only on an
  explicit "stop" action, since tabs get closed and phones lose signal
  far more often than users click a stop button.
- **Local-first, sync-on-reconnect**: write position to local storage
  immediately (so a same-device reload resumes instantly) and sync to the
  server asynchronously; if the sync fails, retry rather than drop the
  update.
- **Conflict resolution**: if a user watched further on their phone after
  their laptop's last sync, "most recent `updatedAt` wins" is the simplest
  correct policy for on-demand video — there's no need for anything fancier
  than last-write-wins, since watch position isn't collaboratively edited
  data.

## Edge cases

### Network drop mid-stream

When a segment request stalls or fails, the player shouldn't tear down —
it should move to a distinct "stalled" state: keep the buffered content
playing until it's exhausted, show a subtle loading indicator only once
the buffer actually runs dry, and retry the failed request with capped
exponential backoff. Once a request succeeds again, resume fetching
forward from wherever the buffer left off, at a conservative (low)
rendition first — treat a just-recovered connection as untrusted until a
couple of segments confirm real throughput, then let the ABR logic step
back up.

### Switching quality without a visible stutter

The key rule: switch renditions **only at a segment boundary**, never
mid-segment. Segments are encoded to be independently decodable
(each starts at a keyframe), so appending a new-quality segment right
after the old one is a clean cut the decoder handles natively. Fetching
a different rendition mid-segment either means wasting the half-downloaded
old segment or producing a frame that can't decode against the prior
GOP. In practice this means the ABR decision loop runs *between* segment
fetches, not inside one.

### Keeping captions in sync under variable latency

Captions/subtitles must be driven off **media time** (the video element's
current playback position within its own timeline), not wall-clock time.
If captions were scheduled by `setTimeout` against real-world elapsed
time, any rebuffer, quality switch, or clock drift would desync them
immediately. Cue text should be looked up against the current media
timestamp on every timeupdate-equivalent tick, so a caption is always
correct for whatever frame is actually on screen — even if that frame
arrived late, or the player is mid-recovery from a stall.

## Worked example: a bandwidth dip during a bus commute

A user is 40 minutes into a video on a train, playing at 1080p with a
healthy 25s buffer. The train enters a tunnel gap in coverage: the current
segment request stalls entirely. The player has ~20s of buffer left
(enough for 20 more seconds of playback) — it keeps playing normally and
starts retrying the request with backoff (1s, 2s, 4s...) rather than
immediately flashing a spinner. At 8 seconds of remaining buffer, still no
response — the UI shows a "reconnecting" indicator, but doesn't reset the
player or drop the manifest. Twelve seconds later, connectivity returns.
The very next segment is requested at a conservative low rendition (480p)
regardless of the pre-tunnel bandwidth estimate, since that estimate is
now stale. Two segments download quickly, the throughput estimate updates
to reflect the recovered connection, buffer climbs back past the high
watermark, and the ABR loop steps back up to 1080p over the next few
segments rather than jumping straight there — avoiding a second stall if
the recovery turns out to be brief.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is tuning ABR purely off bandwidth and ignoring buffer
  trend, then "fixing" the resulting stutter by adding a bigger fixed
  buffer everywhere. That just delays the same problem and wastes data on
  users with fine connections. The actual fix is to make the buffer level
  itself a first-class input to the quality decision — force conservative
  choices when the buffer is shrinking, and only reward a rising, healthy
  buffer with a step up in quality.
</Callout>
