The high-level architecture from the previous lesson gets you a feed that
renders. It doesn't get you a feed that survives an interview follow-up
question, and it definitely doesn't get you a feed that survives production
traffic. Every experienced interviewer will push past "we fetch pages of
posts and render them" into the parts that actually distinguish a strong
answer: how pagination behaves when the feed is changing underneath the
reader, how you keep a list with thousands of rendered posts scrolling
smoothly, how a like button feels instant without lying to the user, and
what happens when a post disappears or the network drops mid-scroll. This
lesson is about those specifics.

## Pagination: cursor-based versus offset-based

**Offset-based pagination** asks the server for "give me posts 40 to 60"
(`page=3&size=20` or `offset=40&limit=20`). It's simple to implement and
easy to reason about — until the underlying data changes between requests.

**Cursor-based pagination** asks instead for "give me the 20 posts after
this specific post" (`after=post_88213`), where the cursor is an opaque
pointer tied to a stable ordering key (typically a timestamp plus post ID
to break ties). The server has no notion of "page number" at all.

For a newsfeed, cursor-based pagination wins almost every time, because the
core failure mode of offset pagination is exactly what a feed does
constantly: new items get inserted at the top while the reader is scrolling
through older ones.

Walk through the offset failure concretely. You've fetched posts 0-19
(offset 0, limit 20). Three new posts get created by people you follow.
You scroll and request offset 20, limit 20 — but the server's "position 20"
has shifted by three, because three new rows were inserted above it. You now
either re-see three posts you already saw (duplicates) or skip three posts
entirely (gaps), depending on which direction the offset drifted.

A cursor sidesteps this because it's anchored to a specific post, not a
row count. "Give me 20 posts after `post_88213`" returns the same answer
regardless of how many posts were inserted above `post_88213` in the
meantime.

### New posts inserted at the top

Cursor pagination solves *backward-scroll consistency*, but it doesn't
automatically solve the separate problem of *new posts appearing above
the reader's current position*. That's typically handled as its own
mechanism: a lightweight poll (or a push notification over a WebSocket)
that checks "are there posts newer than the newest one I currently have?"
On a positive answer, don't inject those posts into the DOM immediately —
that would yank content up and disorient anyone mid-read. Instead, buffer
them and show a small "3 new posts" banner pinned above the feed; only
merge them into the rendered list when the reader taps it (or scrolls to
the top themselves).

## Virtualizing the feed list

A chronological or algorithmic feed is, by design, meant to be scrolled
indefinitely. If you render every post card the user has ever fetched as a
real DOM node, two things go wrong as the session goes on: scroll and
layout work grows with total posts loaded (not just visible posts), and
memory for images/video elements climbs without bound. A session that's
been open for twenty minutes with 400 posts loaded shouldn't be five times
heavier to scroll than one with 80 posts loaded.

**List virtualization** fixes this by rendering only the posts currently
in (or near) the viewport, plus a small overscan buffer above and below —
enough rows to cover 1-2 extra screens' worth of scrolling — and recycling
DOM nodes as the user scrolls past them. The list keeps a lightweight
in-memory record of every post's data, but only a small, constant-size
slice of that data actually becomes DOM.

The main complication specific to a newsfeed (versus a virtualized table
of uniform rows) is **variable row height**: a text-only post might be
120px tall, a post with an image might be 480px, and one with an embedded
video plus a long caption might be 640px. A naive virtualizer that assumes
fixed row height will miscalculate scroll position and cause visible
jumps. The fix is to measure each row's real height after it first
renders, cache that measurement keyed by post ID, and use cached (or an
estimated) height for scroll-offset math on rows not yet measured.

<Callout type="tip">
  Virtualization interacts with "restore scroll position on back
  navigation" — a common newsfeed requirement. Keep the post ID the user
  was viewing (not just a raw pixel offset) as the scroll anchor, since
  pixel offsets become wrong the moment row heights are re-measured or new
  posts are prepended above.
</Callout>

## Optimistic UI for likes and comments

Waiting on a network round trip before a like button visibly updates feels
broken — users expect the heart to fill in immediately. The standard fix
is optimistic UI:

1. On tap, update local state immediately (increment the like count, fill
   the icon, mark "liked by me" `true`) and re-render.
2. Fire the mutation request in the background.
3. On success, reconcile with the server's actual response (usually a
   no-op if your optimistic guess matched).
4. On failure, roll back to the prior state and surface a small,
   non-blocking error (a toast, not a modal) so the user knows the action
   didn't stick.

The same pattern applies to comments: append the new comment to the list
immediately (often visually marked as "sending" via reduced opacity),
then either confirm it in place once the server returns the persisted
comment (swap in the real ID/timestamp) or remove it and show a retry
option on failure.

The part interviewers probe hardest here is the rollback: keep enough of
the *previous* state around to restore it cleanly, and handle a user
tapping "like" then "unlike" rapidly before either request resolves. A
robust approach tracks a small pending-mutation queue per post and
reconciles actions in order, rather than trusting a single boolean that a
fast double-tap can race.

## Media loading strategy

A media-heavy feed is dominated by image and video bytes, not JSON. The
core techniques (responsive image sizing, modern formats, lazy-loading,
video-specific concerns like poster frames and adaptive bitrate) are
covered in depth in the <a href="/concepts/image-and-media-optimization">Image
& Media Optimization</a> concept lesson — bring that lesson's toolkit into
this design rather than re-deriving it:

- Request images already sized to the rendered card width via a CDN
  transformation parameter, not a full-resolution original scaled down by
  the browser.
- Lazy-load media below the fold, and pair it with virtualization so
  images scrolled out of the overscan window can be safely unmounted and
  their memory reclaimed.
- Never autoplay video with sound; only start loading video data (beyond a
  poster frame and metadata) once a card crosses some visibility
  threshold, and pause/unload it once it scrolls back out.

The one newsfeed-specific twist: because cards get virtualized in and out,
an image that finished loading once shouldn't reload every time its card
remounts. Cache resolved CDN URL and load status at the store level, keyed
by post ID, so scrolling back up to a previously-viewed post doesn't
refetch media or show a loading flicker for content the user just saw.

## Edge cases

### Duplicate posts after a cursor shifts

Even with cursor pagination, duplicates can creep in: a post you already
have gets re-returned because a top-of-feed poll and a bottom-of-feed
pagination request race, or a retried request after a timeout succeeds
twice. The fix is to treat the client-side store as the source of truth
for dedup: every time a batch of posts arrives, merge by post ID into a
normalized store (a map keyed by ID plus an ordered ID list for rendering)
instead of naively concatenating arrays. A `Map` merge makes duplicate IDs
a non-issue by construction.

### A post is deleted while it's in view

If the author (or a moderator) deletes a post the reader is currently
looking at, a hard delete-from-array-and-re-render can be jarring — the
whole list shifts. Handle it as a soft removal: mark the post `deleted` in
the store, render a brief "this post is no longer available" placeholder
with a short collapse transition, then drop it from the ID list shortly
after. Also guard in-flight like/comment mutations against a now-deleted
post — check the post still exists before applying a server response, and
silently drop the action rather than throwing if it doesn't.

### Slow or offline networks

Two distinct situations need distinct handling. A **slow network** should
degrade gracefully: show skeleton placeholders for posts still loading
rather than a blank screen, cache the last-fetched feed so a reload isn't
a blank slate, and set a reasonable request timeout with a visible retry
action instead of an infinite spinner. A **fully offline** state should be
detected explicitly (via the browser's online/offline events, not
inferred from failed requests) and surfaced clearly — disable
pull-to-refresh and new-page fetches, keep already-loaded posts readable,
and queue like/comment actions taken while offline to replay once
connectivity returns, rather than dropping them silently.

## Worked example: scrolling into a burst of new posts

Say a reader loaded the feed, scrolled down 15 screens (600 posts fetched
across 30 cursor pages), and has been reading for two minutes. During that
window, 12 new posts were created above the original top of the feed, and
one post the reader scrolled past 90 seconds ago just got deleted by its
author.

A well-built feed handles this without the reader noticing anything
break: the top-of-feed poll detects the 12 new posts and shows a "12 new
posts" banner (not injected into the scroll), the virtualizer has already
unmounted the DOM nodes for posts far above the current viewport so
memory hasn't grown unbounded across those 600 posts, and the deleted post
— currently outside the overscan window — gets soft-marked in the store so
that if the reader scrolls back up to it, they see a graceful "no longer
available" slot instead of a broken card or a console error. None of this
requires the reader to refresh the page.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake: implementing "check for new posts" by re-fetching page
  one of the feed and diffing it against what's rendered. This reintroduces
  every offset-pagination problem (shifted rows, duplicates) even if your
  main pagination is cursor-based, and it wastes bandwidth re-fetching
  posts you already have. Use a dedicated "is there anything newer than
  cursor X" check that only asks for genuinely new items, and merge them
  through the same ID-keyed dedup path as every other batch of posts.
</Callout>
