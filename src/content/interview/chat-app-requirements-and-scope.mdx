"Design a real-time chat application" sounds narrower than a newsfeed or a
video platform, but it packs in more distributed-systems-flavored edge
cases per minute of interview time than almost any other frontend prompt.
A feed can get away with "eventually consistent, refresh if unsure." A
chat app can't — users notice immediately when a message shows up twice,
arrives out of order, or gets stuck in "sending..." forever. That's
exactly why interviewers like this prompt: it forces you to reason about
ordering, retries, and state sync under bad network conditions, not just
component layout.

Before touching WebSockets or a component tree, spend the first few
minutes of the interview scoping the problem. This lesson covers the
functional and nonfunctional requirements a strong candidate states out
loud, and the clarifying questions whose answers meaningfully change the
rest of the design.

## Why chat is unusually unforgiving of vague scope

Most frontend system design prompts tolerate some ambiguity — you can
sketch a newsfeed and adjust ranking assumptions later without redoing
your architecture. Chat doesn't give you that room. Whether the app needs
to support groups, whether encryption is in scope, and whether a user's
messages need to sync across a phone and a laptop each change the data
model, not just a visual detail. Committing to an architecture before
asking these questions means real risk of re-deriving your whole design
mid-interview.

Two to three minutes of scoping, spoken out loud, is what separates "I
designed a toy 1:1 messenger" from "I designed the thing the interviewer
actually asked for."

## Functional requirements

For the canonical "design a chat app" prompt, a strong functional scope
looks like this:

- **1:1 messaging.** Two users exchange text messages in a conversation
  thread, ordered by time sent.
- **Group messaging.** A conversation with more than two participants;
  every member sees every message in order, and membership can change
  (people join/leave).
- **Delivery and read receipts.** The sender sees whether a message has
  left their device, reached the recipient's device, and been seen —
  typically rendered as sent/delivered/read states (think single check,
  double check, colored double check).
- **Typing indicators.** A lightweight, ephemeral signal ("Alex is
  typing...") shown to other participants while someone is composing a
  message.
- **Media attachments.** Images, short video, and files sent inline in
  the conversation, with some kind of upload progress and thumbnail/preview
  behavior distinct from plain text.

What's usually **out of scope** unless the interviewer says otherwise:
voice/video calling, a bot or third-party integration platform, message
search infrastructure, and admin/moderation tooling. Say this out loud —
"I'll assume calling and admin tooling are out of scope, and focus on the
core messaging and sync path" — so the interviewer can redirect you if
they disagree.

## Nonfunctional requirements

The functional list is fairly stable across interviewers. The
nonfunctionals are where chat gets interesting, and where a frontend-
specific answer (not a backend replication story) is expected.

### Real-time delivery latency

A chat app that takes three seconds to show a sent message on the
recipient's screen feels broken, not just slow. The frontend needs a
persistent connection strategy (not polling) so that new messages, typing
signals, and receipt updates arrive with sub-second perceived latency
while the app is in the foreground. Equally important: the **sender's own
UI** should show the message immediately via optimistic rendering, before
any server acknowledgment arrives — the perceived latency budget for "my
message appeared" is close to zero, even though the true round trip to
the server and back is not.

### Message ordering guarantees

Messages can arrive out of order — a slow network path, a reconnect after
a drop, or two people sending near-simultaneously in a group chat can all
cause this. The UI has to guarantee a **stable, consistent per-conversation
order** regardless of arrival order, which means ordering can't simply be
"append whatever arrives, whenever it arrives." This is a first-class
nonfunctional requirement to state explicitly, because it drives real
design decisions (client-generated IDs, sequence numbers) that the deep-dive
lesson covers in detail.

### Offline message queuing

Mobile networks drop constantly — a subway tunnel, an elevator, an airplane
mode toggle. A message the user tries to send while offline shouldn't be
lost or silently fail; it should queue locally and send automatically once
connectivity returns, with the UI reflecting a distinct "queued, will send"
state rather than looking identical to "sent." This has to survive not just
brief disconnects but the app being killed and relaunched later.

## The clarifying questions to ask

### "Is there a limit on group size?"

This changes more than it sounds like it would. A group capped at a few
dozen members can reasonably track and render **per-member** read
receipts ("seen by Priya, Alex, and 3 others") and per-member typing
indicators. A group of hundreds or thousands can't — rendering and
transmitting per-member state for that many participants doesn't scale,
so the design shifts to an aggregate signal ("seen by 214 people") and
typing indicators are often dropped entirely for large groups. If the
interviewer says "up to a few hundred," say explicitly that you're
switching from per-member to aggregate receipts past some threshold —
that's a senior-level scoping call, not a cop-out.

### "Is end-to-end encryption in scope?"

If E2EE is required, the server can only ever relay opaque ciphertext —
it cannot read message content to power server-side full-text search,
generate link previews, or scan attachments. Those features either move
entirely to the client (search over locally-decrypted history, client-side
preview generation) or get scoped out. If E2EE is explicitly **not**
required, you have a much simpler story: the server can assist with
search indexing, moderation, and preview generation, and you can spend
your interview time elsewhere. This is worth asking early because
retrofitting E2EE onto a design that assumed server-side search is a
rewrite, not a patch.

### "Does state need to sync across a user's multiple devices?"

If a user is logged into the same account on a phone and a laptop
simultaneously, "has this message been read" stops being a simple
per-user boolean — it becomes per-device state that needs to be
reconciled ("read on phone, not yet opened on laptop, so is the overall
conversation read?"). The same applies to typing indicators and message
drafts. If multi-device sync is out of scope, your client can treat its
local state as close to authoritative; if it's in scope, the server needs
to be the source of truth for read/receipt state, and every device
needs to reconcile against it on reconnect. This is a data-model decision,
not just a UI one, so it's worth asking before you sketch a component
tree.

### A few second-tier questions worth asking if time allows

- "Do messages need to be edited or deleted after sending, and if so, for
  how long?" (Affects whether message state is mutable and how that
  propagates to already-delivered copies.)
- "Is there a maximum attachment size, and are attachments required to be
  previewable inline, or is a download link acceptable?" (Changes the
  upload/preview pipeline's complexity.)
- "Do conversations need to be searchable, and if so, just by the current
  user or across the group?" (Determines whether search runs against
  local history, a server index, or both.)

## Worked example: how the answers reshape the design

Suppose the interviewer answers: "groups are capped at 20 people; no
end-to-end encryption required; and yes, users can be logged in on phone
and desktop at the same time."

That set of answers already tells you a lot:

- A group of 20 is small enough to justify full per-member read receipts
  and typing indicators — you don't need to design an aggregate-count
  fallback.
- Without E2EE, the server can own search indexing and link-preview
  generation, which simplifies the attachment/media pipeline
  significantly — you don't need a client-side crypto or search story.
- Multi-device sync means "read" state must live server-side as the
  source of truth, and each device needs to reconcile its local read
  cursor against the server on connect/reconnect — this is a real data-flow
  concern you should flag now, even before you've drawn the architecture
  diagram, because it affects what the local cache stores.

Now contrast with an interviewer who instead says: "groups can be up to
2,000 people, like a large team channel; end-to-end encryption is
required; single device only." That's a meaningfully different system —
per-member receipts are out, aggregate "seen by" counts are in; search
and previews move client-side or get scoped out entirely; and you get to
skip multi-device reconciliation altogether, freeing up interview time for
the encryption-aware media pipeline instead. Same opening prompt, two very
different fifteen-minute deep dives — driven entirely by three questions
asked in the first two minutes.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake here is treating "delivered" and "read" as the
  same signal, or worse, collapsing sent/delivered/read into a single
  boolean `isSent`. They're three distinct events with three distinct
  transports: sent is a local optimistic update, delivered is an ack from
  the recipient's device (or a server queue, if the recipient is offline),
  and read is a signal the recipient's client sends only once the message
  is actually visible on screen. Conflating them either shows a false
  "read" before the recipient has opened the app, or never shows
  "delivered" for offline recipients until they're read — both are bugs
  interviewers will probe on if you gloss over the distinction.
</Callout>
