"Design a social media newsfeed" is one of the most common frontend system
design prompts, and also one of the easiest to botch in the first five
minutes. Candidates who jump straight to "I'll use a virtualized list and
cursor pagination" skip the part that actually separates a senior answer
from a junior one: deciding what you're building before deciding how. The
same prompt can mean an Instagram-style ranked feed, a Twitter-style
chronological timeline, or a LinkedIn-style hybrid — and each of those
implies a different component tree, a different data-fetching strategy,
and a different set of edge cases to defend later in the interview.

This lesson is the scoping pass. We'll define the functional requirements
(what the feed does), the nonfunctional requirements (what "good" looks
like under load and on bad networks), and — most importantly — the
clarifying questions that let you pin down the version of the problem
you're actually solving before you commit to an architecture.

## Why scoping first matters here specifically

A newsfeed touches almost every hard problem in frontend system design at
once: pagination, list virtualization, optimistic updates, media loading,
and real-time-ish freshness. That breadth is exactly why interviewers use
it — it's a forcing function to see whether you can triage. If you spend
your first ten minutes deciding whether comments support nested replies,
you'll run out of time before touching performance. If you spend zero
minutes on scope, you'll design something that doesn't match what the
interviewer had in mind, and you'll have to backtrack mid-interview.

Two to four minutes of scoping, spoken out loud, buys you the rest of the
interview.

## Functional requirements

For the core "design a newsfeed" prompt, a strong functional scope usually
looks like this:

- **An infinite-scrolling feed of posts.** The user opens the app, sees a
  page of posts, and scrolling down loads more — with no "page 2" button.
- **Mixed post content.** Posts can contain text, a single image, an image
  gallery, or a video, sometimes combined with text. The feed has to
  render all of these in one scrollable list.
- **Engagement actions.** Users can like and comment on a post directly
  from the feed, and see aggregate counts (like count, comment count)
  update.
- **Some notion of new content arriving.** Either new posts silently
  appear at the top on next refresh, or the client is notified that new
  posts exist ("12 new posts") without forcing a jarring re-render of
  what the user is currently looking at.

What's usually **out of scope** unless the interviewer says otherwise:
direct messages, stories/ephemeral content, an ads insertion pipeline,
content moderation tooling, and the post-composition/upload flow itself.
Say this out loud — "I'm going to assume post creation and DMs are out of
scope, focusing on the read/render/engage path" — so the interviewer can
correct you if they disagree.

## Nonfunctional requirements

The functional list above is nearly identical across every version of this
question. The nonfunctionals are where the interesting engineering
trade-offs live, and where interviewers expect a frontend-specific answer
rather than a backend one.

### Performance on constrained networks and devices

A newsfeed is a mobile-first, high-frequency-use surface. That means:

- Time to first meaningful content matters more than total load time — the
  first screenful of posts should render fast even if the rest of the feed
  is still arriving.
- Images and video are usually the dominant payload weight, so a
  bandwidth-aware media strategy (lazy loading below the fold, responsive
  image sizes, deferring video until it's actually in view) is a
  first-class nonfunctional requirement, not an afterthought.
- The feed has to stay smooth while scrolling through potentially thousands
  of items in a session — a naive unbounded DOM list will degrade badly on
  a mid-range phone.

### Scale: huge post and event volume

Feeds are read far more often than they're written, and each post can
generate a large fan-out of likes and comments. From the frontend's
perspective, this shows up as:

- Pagination has to work correctly even as the underlying dataset is
  being written to concurrently (someone posts while you're scrolling).
- Engagement counts (likes, comments) update at high frequency for popular
  posts — the UI can't literally re-render on every single event at
  scale, so some batching or "close enough" number is expected.

### Freshness vs. consistency

This is the trade-off worth naming explicitly, because it's the one
interviewers listen for: a newsfeed does **not** need strong consistency.
A like count that's a few seconds stale, or a comment that takes a moment
to appear for other users, is an acceptable and expected trade-off in
exchange for a feed that feels fast and responsive. Contrast that with,
say, a bank balance — where staleness is unacceptable. Saying "I'm
optimizing for perceived freshness and low latency over strict
consistency, because a slightly stale like count is a fine trade for a
snappy UI" is a strong, senior-level framing to state up front.

## The clarifying questions to ask

Before touching architecture, a strong candidate asks questions that
materially change the design — not questions for the sake of looking
thorough. Here are the ones that matter most for a newsfeed, and what
changes depending on the answer.

### "Is this a single chronological feed, or algorithmically ranked?"

This is the single highest-leverage question. A **chronological** feed
(think a simple following-only timeline) means pagination is
straightforward — you're always fetching "everything older/newer than
cursor X" in a stable order. A **ranked** feed (think a relevance-scored
"For You" feed) means the server is reordering content per-request based
on a scoring model, which breaks naive cursor pagination (item at position
20 on your next fetch might not be the item that was at position 20 a
minute ago), and introduces a design concern around explaining or at
least tolerating "why is this post here" inconsistency. If the
interviewer wants ranking, you should flag that pagination needs to be
designed around stable page tokens rather than raw offsets, and move on —
the ranking algorithm itself is a backend/ML concern, not yours to design.

### "Do updates need to be real-time, or is refresh-on-demand acceptable?"

If real-time is required, that usually means a live connection (WebSocket
or long-poll) delivering a lightweight signal — "there are new posts" or
an updated like/comment count — not the interviewer asking you to stream
full post payloads continuously. This is an important distinction to make
out loud: you want a cheap, low-frequency signal driving a "New posts"
button, not the entire feed re-rendering on every event. If real-time
isn't required, a simple refresh-to-fetch (pull-to-refresh, or refetch on
app foreground) is sufficient, and you can skip live-connection design
entirely, saving real time in the interview.

### "Is this feed mostly text, or heavily media (images/video)?"

A text-first feed (think a professional network's post feed) has a
render-performance profile dominated by DOM size and virtualization. A
media-heavy feed (think a photo/video-sharing app) has a profile
dominated by network payload, image/video decode cost, and layout shift
from late-loading media — meaning your nonfunctional performance story
needs to lead with the media pipeline, not the list virtualization. Ask
this early, because it changes which deep dive you should spend the most
time on later in the interview.

### A few second-tier questions worth asking if time allows

- "Do comments need to support nested replies, or is a flat list fine?"
  (Changes data shape and UI complexity, but not the core architecture.)
- "Is this single-device only, or does state need to sync across a user's
  devices?" (Affects whether client-side cache can be the source of
  truth or needs to defer to server state more aggressively.)
- "What's the expected post volume per user session — tens of posts, or
  could a user realistically scroll through thousands?" (Confirms whether
  list virtualization is a "nice to have" or a hard requirement.)

## Worked example: how the answers reshape the design

Say the interviewer answers: "single chronological feed of people you
follow, no ranking; real-time isn't required, refresh is fine; and yes,
it's media-heavy — think photos and short videos."

That single set of answers already tells you:

- Pagination can be simple, cursor-based on post timestamp/ID — no need
  to design around a reordering ranking service.
- You can skip designing a live-update connection entirely; a
  pull-to-refresh or "check for new posts on app foreground" flow covers
  the freshness requirement, which simplifies your data-flow story a lot.
- Your performance deep dive should center on the media pipeline:
  responsive image sizing, lazy-loading media below the fold, and
  deferring video decode until a post scrolls into view — because that's
  where most of the user-perceived latency and jank will come from, not
  list virtualization by itself (though you'll still want that too, at
  scale).

Now contrast with a second interviewer who instead says: "it's a ranked,
'For You'-style feed; real-time like/comment counts matter; text-first,
minimal media." That's a meaningfully different problem — you'd spend
more time on pagination-token stability across re-ranked requests, a
lightweight live-count-update channel, and comparatively less on media
optimization. Same prompt, two different fifteen-minute deep dives,
entirely because of three clarifying questions.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake candidates make here is skipping straight to
  "I'll virtualize the list and use cursor pagination" without ever
  asking whether the feed is ranked. If it turns out to be ranked, cursor
  pagination as you originally described it (`offset`/simple next-cursor
  by ID) silently breaks — a re-ranked feed can shuffle items between
  requests, causing duplicates or skipped posts. Ask the ranking question
  first; it changes the pagination design you'll present a few minutes
  later.
</Callout>
