The first three lessons in this module got you a working mental model: what a CDN is, how content gets onto it, and how caching and invalidation keep it from serving garbage forever. That's enough to answer "would you use a CDN here?" in an interview. It is not enough to answer the two follow-up questions that separate a pass from a strong pass: *what does a user actually see during the seconds after you publish a change*, and *what stops a CDN — a system explicitly designed to serve your content to anyone, fast, from thousands of locations — from serving it to people you didn't intend, or being used against you*.

Those two concerns, consistency and security, are where CDN design conversations get real. This lesson covers both, then ties them together into a short framework for reasoning about a CDN layer end to end in an interview.

## Consistency: what "up to date" means across thousands of edges

A CDN's whole value proposition is having your content sitting in hundreds or thousands of physical locations. That same fact makes strict consistency impossible in practice — there's no way to atomically update every POP (point of presence) worldwide at the exact same instant, and even a global cache-purge broadcast takes real propagation time across the CDN's internal network.

The practical consequence: after you push a change, there's a window — commonly single-digit seconds, sometimes tens of seconds for a slow or oversized purge — where a POP in Frankfurt is serving the new version while a POP in São Paulo is still serving the old one. This isn't a defect in a particular CDN vendor; it's the physics of "replicate to everywhere, then converge."

Two ways to design around it:

- **Versioned URLs as the primary consistency tool.** Instead of updating `app.js` in place and asking every POP to purge it, ship `app.abc123.js` and change the reference to it. The new URL is a cache miss everywhere the instant a client requests it — no purge, no propagation lag, no stale-version window at all. This is the same cache-busting idea from the previous lesson, but worth calling out here specifically as a *consistency* mechanism, not just a caching one: it sidesteps the propagation problem entirely rather than trying to make purges faster.
- **Purge/refresh for content that must keep its URL.** Pages, API responses, and anything referenced by a stable, human-visible URL can't be versioned away — you have to purge and accept the propagation window. Know that window's rough size for your CDN and decide whether your product tolerates it (a blog correction: fine; a price change mid-flash-sale: probably not).

For anything where staleness is genuinely unacceptable — account balances, inventory counts, one-time tokens — the right answer is usually "don't cache it at the edge at all," `Cache-Control: no-store` or a TTL of zero, and eat the extra origin load. Fighting a CDN's eventual-consistency model for data that needs strong consistency is a losing battle; routing it around the cache instead is not.

## Security: the edge is a wide-open front door

A CDN is deliberately built to serve requests from anyone, fast, without hitting your origin. That's exactly the property an attacker or a freeloader wants too. A serious CDN design has to answer four questions.

### Who's allowed to fetch this?

Public assets (a marketing site's CSS) need no gating. Anything private, paid, or licensed needs it at the edge, not just at your app server, or you've defeated the purpose of caching it there. The standard mechanism is a **signed URL or signed cookie**: the origin generates a token (often an HMAC over the resource path plus an expiry timestamp) and the CDN verifies that signature locally, at the POP, before serving from cache. A video-streaming platform issuing a link that's only valid for 6 hours, or an image CDN only serving thumbnails to requests carrying a valid session cookie, are both this pattern.

### Is the transport itself trusted?

TLS should terminate at the edge (so nearby users get a fast, geographically close handshake) with a second, CDN-to-origin TLS leg behind it — "TLS all the way," not "TLS to the edge, plaintext behind it." Certificate management at hundreds of POPs is one of the concrete reasons teams pick a managed CDN over rolling their own edge fleet.

### Can someone bypass the edge and hit origin directly?

This is the single most common gap. If your origin's IP or hostname is discoverable and accepts any request, an attacker skips your signed-URL checks, your rate limiting, and your DDoS scrubbing entirely by going straight to origin. **Origin shielding** — restricting the origin to accept traffic only from the CDN's published IP ranges, or requiring a shared secret header the CDN injects on every forwarded request — closes this. Treat "is the origin locked to CDN-only traffic" as a mandatory checklist item, not an optional hardening step.

### Is the edge itself being used as a weapon?

Because a CDN absorbs huge request volumes by design, it's also your first line of defense against volumetric DDoS: a well-configured CDN can absorb and rate-limit a flood at the edge, where capacity is enormous, rather than letting it reach a comparatively tiny origin fleet. The flip side is **hotlinking** — someone else's site embedding your image or video URL directly, so your CDN bill pays to serve their traffic. Checking the `Referer`/`Origin` header at the edge and rejecting unexpected ones is the standard cheap fix. Geo-restriction (blocking or allowing by country, for content-licensing reasons) is the same idea applied to location instead of referrer.

<Callout type="warning">
  A very common design mistake: teams implement signed URLs correctly at the CDN layer, feel done, and never lock down the origin. The origin's hostname often leaks — through DNS history, a misconfigured CNAME, error pages, or just being guessable — and once it does, every security control you built into the edge is void, because the origin will happily serve unsigned, unauthenticated requests to anyone who asks. Signed URLs and origin shielding are a pair; shipping one without the other is not actually secure.
</Callout>

## Worked example: publishing a price change on a global storefront

Say a retailer runs a flash sale and needs a price drop live everywhere within 2 seconds, while product images can lag by up to a minute with no harm. Two different consistency strategies, on purpose:

- **Price**: served from an API response marked `no-store` (or a 1–2 second TTL at most), so every request either bypasses the edge cache or refreshes almost immediately — the "acceptable staleness" here is near zero, so you pay the origin-load cost.
- **Product images**: served with a long TTL and versioned filenames per image revision (`shirt-v3.jpg`). A photo update ships as a new URL, so there's no purge to wait on and no stale-image window — old pages referencing `shirt-v2.jpg` simply keep working until they're re-crawled or re-rendered.

Security-wise, the storefront's checkout API responses are gated behind signed, short-lived tokens tied to the logged-in session (so a cached response for user A is never replayed to user B), while the product images stay public and cacheable by anyone, with only hotlink protection to stop other sites embedding them directly. One system, two different consistency SLAs and two different access-control postures, chosen deliberately per asset type rather than applied uniformly.
