Every backend eventually needs something to run later, or run again and again: a nightly billing job, an hourly report export, a "retry this failed webhook in 30 seconds" task, a "send this reminder email in 3 days" delay. The naive answer — a cron entry on one box — works until that box reboots mid-job, until two people schedule jobs on it without knowing about each other, or until "one box" can't keep up with a million tenants each wanting their own schedule. A distributed task scheduler is the system that replaces that single crontab: it durably tracks what should run and when, fires jobs across a cluster of workers instead of one machine, and keeps running correctly through worker crashes, deploys, and scheduler restarts.

This is the first lesson in the module, and its only job is to pin down what "correct" means here before any architecture gets drawn. The lessons that follow — data model and queues, designing the scheduler, and scaling and fault tolerance — all answer questions this lesson raises. Get sloppy here (for example, promising "exactly-once execution" without noticing that's a hard distributed-systems promise) and you'll spend the rest of the design fixing a requirement that was wrong from the start.

## Scoping the problem: scheduler vs. workflow engine

Two questions decide how big this system is, and both are worth asking an interviewer directly rather than assuming:

- **Independent jobs, or dependent workflows?** A scheduler that runs `run_report(tenant_id)` every day at 3am is a different (simpler) system than one that runs a DAG — "extract, then transform, then load, then notify" — where each step depends on the previous one succeeding. The DAG version is closer to Airflow or Temporal; this module assumes the simpler case (independent scheduled jobs) unless stated otherwise, and calls out DAG support as a possible extension.
- **What triggers a run?** Time-based triggers come in a few flavors worth naming precisely: a **cron expression** (`0 3 * * *` — recurring, calendar-aware), a **fixed interval** (every 5 minutes from creation time), and a **one-off** future timestamp (run once, at 2026-08-01 09:00 UTC). Some designs also support event-based triggers (run when an upstream file lands), but that pulls in a pub/sub component and is worth flagging as a separate concern rather than folding it in silently.

Also fix the vocabulary, since it gets reused for the rest of the module: a **job definition** is the durable "what and when" record a user registers; a **trigger** or **schedule** is the timing rule attached to it; an **execution** (or "run") is one concrete firing of that job at a point in time, with its own status and result.

## Functional requirements

- **Job registration API.** Create, update, pause, resume, and delete a job definition — including its trigger, a reference to the work to perform (a queue topic, a container image, an RPC endpoint), a timeout, and a retry policy. Updating a job's schedule shouldn't require deleting and recreating it.
- **Execution guarantee.** State this explicitly rather than assuming: **at-least-once** (a job may run more than once after a failure, so it must be idempotent) is the realistic default for a distributed scheduler. **Exactly-once** is not free — it needs an idempotency key per intended execution plus a dedup check, and even then only holds if the job's side effects go through that same dedup path. **At-most-once** (skip rather than risk a duplicate) is easier but means some scheduled work silently never happens after a crash.
- **Overlap / concurrency policy.** If a job is still running when its next trigger time arrives, what happens — skip this firing, queue it to run right after the current one finishes, or allow both to run concurrently? Each is a legitimate choice depending on the job (a stock-price sync job might allow overlap; a "close the books" billing job must not).
- **Retry and failure handling.** A configurable retry count with backoff (fixed or exponential), and a defined outcome after retries are exhausted — typically a dead-letter record and an alert, not a silent drop.
- **Cancellation and manual trigger.** Users need to cancel an in-flight execution and manually fire a job "now" outside its schedule (for testing or catch-up), without that manual run confusing the next scheduled run's bookkeeping.
- **Visibility.** Query next-run time, execution history, and current status for any job — this is what makes the system operable, and it's the first thing an on-call engineer reaches for at 3am when a job didn't fire.

## Non-functional requirements

- **Scale.** Two different numbers matter: the number of distinct job definitions the system holds (could be millions, one or more per tenant in a multi-tenant SaaS product), and the trigger throughput — how many jobs come due per second. These are not proportional to each other in a simple way, because schedules cluster at common times.
- **Timing precision.** Don't promise exact-time firing. State a tolerance instead — "a job scheduled for 3:00:00 fires by 3:00:30" is realistic and lets the scheduler batch trigger checks instead of busy-polling every job every millisecond.
- **Durability.** A job definition, its schedule, and its last-successful-run bookkeeping must survive a scheduler process crash without loss — otherwise a restart either forgets to run jobs or re-runs everything from scratch. This is a requirement on the system, not yet a design (that's the next lesson).
- **Availability of the scheduler itself.** The component deciding "what's due right now" cannot be a single point of failure; a crash there should not stop new triggers from firing after a short failover, and should not double-fire triggers that already fired.
- **Fault tolerance on the execution side.** A worker crashing mid-job should result in that job being retried elsewhere, not silently lost — this implies some kind of lease or heartbeat so the system can tell "still running" apart from "died and needs reassignment."
- **Independent scalability.** Trigger evaluation (lightweight, frequent) and job execution (can be heavy, slow, resource-hungry) should scale independently — a burst of long-running report jobs shouldn't degrade the scheduler's ability to keep firing everyone else's jobs on time.
- **Multi-tenancy and fairness.** In a shared system, one tenant scheduling 500,000 jobs for the same minute shouldn't starve every other tenant's jobs — this points toward per-tenant quotas or fair-share dispatch, a topic picked up when workers and queues are designed.

## Worked example: sizing the problem before designing anything

Take a concrete prompt: a SaaS platform with 2 million tenants, each configuring an average of 5 scheduled jobs (report exports, digest emails, sync tasks), so roughly 10 million job definitions live in the system at once.

- **Average trigger rate:** if a typical job runs about once a day, that's 10,000,000 / 86,400 seconds ≈ 116 triggers per second on average — a manageable number for a single well-indexed store to evaluate.
- **Peak trigger rate:** schedules cluster. If a large fraction of daily jobs are set for common times (midnight UTC, top of the hour, 9am local time rounded to a handful of timezones), the peak can easily be 50-100x the average — meaning the system needs to comfortably handle bursts of 5,000-10,000 triggers per second, not just the daily average, or every top-of-hour becomes an incident.
- **Execution duration spread:** most jobs (status checks, small syncs) finish in under a second; some (bulk report generation, data exports) run for 10+ minutes. This spread is exactly why trigger evaluation and job execution must be decoupled — the scheduler's job is to notice "this is due" and hand it off quickly, not to block on the job's actual runtime.

Those three numbers — job count, peak trigger rate, and execution duration spread — are the inputs the next lesson uses to decide how job definitions and pending executions actually get stored and queued.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common mistake at this stage is treating "at-least-once execution" and "exactly-once execution" as interchangeable, or assuming exactly-once is simply a matter of trying hard enough. At-least-once means a job can and will occasionally run twice (after a crash right after execution but before the success record is durably saved) — so every job handler in the system needs to be idempotent, or wrapped with a dedup key the scheduler checks before re-running. If a design promises exactly-once without an idempotency mechanism anywhere in it, that promise is fiction. Say "at-least-once, with idempotent handlers" out loud early — it's the honest default and it simplifies everything downstream.
</Callout>

## Putting the requirements together

A solid requirements pass here leaves you able to state, in a few sentences: whether the system schedules independent jobs or dependent workflows, what trigger types it supports (cron, fixed-interval, one-off), the execution guarantee (at-least-once, with idempotent handlers, as the default), the overlap policy for slow jobs, a timing precision tolerance instead of an exact-time promise, and rough scale numbers for job count and peak trigger rate. Everything in the rest of this module — how jobs and schedules are modeled and queued, how the scheduler and workers are actually built, and how the system stays correct through crashes at scale — is just engineering in service of the requirements written down here.
