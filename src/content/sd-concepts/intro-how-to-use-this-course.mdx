The previous two lessons made the case for *what* modern system design is and *why* it's worth learning even outside interview prep. This one is shorter and more practical: how to actually move through the course so the effort you put in turns into skill you can use under pressure — in an interview room or in a real design review — rather than a stack of half-remembered diagrams.

That distinction matters because system design content is unusually easy to feel like you've learned without actually having learned it. Reading a clear explanation of consistent hashing feels like understanding it. Being asked "how would you shard this and rebalance it when you add a node" cold, in front of another engineer, is a different skill entirely. This course is built around closing that gap, and using it well means leaning into the parts that create that gap deliberately — the quizzes, the mock interviews, the worked examples — not skimming past them to get to "the next topic."

## Two tracks, and why the order matters

The course is split into two tracks that map to two different jobs:

- **Foundations & Building Blocks** (where this lesson lives) — concept lessons covering the individual pieces: load balancing, caching, database replication, partitioning, queues, consensus, and the reasoning framework for combining them. Each lesson stands mostly on its own and teaches one idea precisely.
- **Design Problems** — full case studies ("design a URL shortener," "design a rate limiter," "design a chat system") where you combine several building blocks into one coherent architecture under a specific set of constraints, ending in a timed mock-interview exercise.

The Design Problems track is where the building blocks actually get exercised together, which is also why it's tempting to jump there first — it feels more like "the real thing." Resist that. A case study on designing a news-feed service will casually reference fan-out-on-write vs. fan-out-on-read, cache invalidation, and read replicas as if you already have working definitions for them, because the course expects you to. Reading the case study first, without the building blocks, means either getting lost in vocabulary or — worse — nodding along without the words meaning anything specific, which is exactly the "feels learned but isn't" trap from above.

The practical rule: finish (or at least skim-and-quiz) the Foundations lessons relevant to a case study before attempting that case study. You don't need every single Foundations lesson before starting Design Problems — but you do need the ones a given case study leans on.

## What each piece of a lesson is actually for

Every concept lesson in this course has the same shape on purpose, and each part earns its place:

- **The prose body** teaches the concept and its trade-offs — this is the "first exposure" pass. Read it for understanding, not memorization.
- **The diagram** exists because some relationships (data flow, request paths, state transitions) are genuinely faster to absorb visually than in a paragraph. If a diagram doesn't click immediately, re-read the paragraph right above it — the two are meant to reinforce each other, not stand alone.
- **The worked example** is where the abstract idea meets specific numbers. This is usually the part worth re-reading a second time, because interview prompts and real design problems are always specific ("500K writes/sec," not "a lot of writes"), and the worked example is your rehearsal for translating a vague concept into concrete numbers.
- **The pitfalls callout** is a shortcut past a mistake that normally takes a bad code review or a production incident to learn the hard way. Don't skip it because it's short.
- **The quiz** is the active-recall checkpoint. Reading comprehension and answer-under-quiz-conditions are different skills; getting a question wrong here, while it's cheap, is far more useful than getting the equivalent point wrong in an interview.
- **The cheatsheet bullets** in the frontmatter are deliberately not a summary of the lesson — they're written to be useful stripped of context, the night before an interview when you don't have time to re-read six paragraphs. Use them for review, not first-pass learning; if a bullet doesn't make sense on its own, that's a sign to go back to the full lesson, not to just memorize the sentence.

## A concrete study plan

Suppose you have three weeks before a system design interview loop and roughly five hours a week. A reasonable way to spend that:

- **Week 1**: Go through the Introduction module (these three lessons) and then the core Foundations lessons — networking basics, load balancing, caching, database fundamentals, replication and partitioning. Take every quiz. Don't start Design Problems yet, even if it's tempting.
- **Week 2**: Do three or four Design Problems case studies that combine what you just learned, doing the Mock Interview exercise for each one on an actual timer, out loud (even alone). After each one, jump back to any Foundations lesson that felt shaky during the mock and re-read it — you'll get more out of it now that you've seen it fail to come to mind under pressure.
- **Week 3**: Do the remaining case studies, revisit cheatsheets across both tracks for a fast pass, and redo the Mock Interview exercise for the one or two case studies where your first attempt felt weakest. Repetition on your weakest points beats a first pass through everything you haven't touched yet.

The specific numbers (three weeks, five hours) aren't the point — the shape is: foundations first, then combine them under timed practice, then go back and shore up the weak spots you only discover by practicing.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common way to waste time in a course like this is passive re-reading: going back over a lesson's prose a second or third time because the first pass "didn't quite stick," instead of testing yourself with the quiz or trying to explain the concept out loud from memory. Re-reading feels productive because the material feels familiar on the second pass — but familiarity when the text is right in front of you says nothing about whether you can produce that reasoning from scratch when it isn't. If a lesson didn't stick, retake its quiz and try to state the trade-off in your own words before rereading the paragraph that explains it.
</Callout>
