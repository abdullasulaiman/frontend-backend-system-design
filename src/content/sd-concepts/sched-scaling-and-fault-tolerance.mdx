The design from the previous lesson works fine on a whiteboard and on a single machine. It falls apart the moment you ask two harder questions: what happens when the number of scheduled jobs grows past what one coordinator can track, and what happens when that coordinator — or the worker running a job — crashes at the worst possible moment? A scheduler that quietly drops a due job, or one that runs the same billing job twice because two nodes both thought they owned it, is worse than no scheduler at all: it fails silently, and nobody notices until a customer does. This lesson covers the two things every production task scheduler has to get right that a single-node prototype can skip: scaling the coordination layer horizontally, and staying correct through crashes, restarts, and network partitions.

## Scaling: split "deciding" from "doing"

A task scheduler actually has two distinct jobs, and they scale differently:

- **Trigger evaluation** — deciding *when* a job is due, based on its cron expression or delay, and handing it off. This is low-volume, CPU-light work: scanning a sorted set of "next fire time" entries and comparing against the clock.
- **Execution** — actually running the job's payload, which can be arbitrarily expensive (a report generation, an API call, a batch data job).

Coupling these on one node is what caps a naive design. The fix is to scale them independently:

- **Shard trigger evaluation** by job ID (consistent hashing works here the same way it does for a distributed cache — see the caching module) across a set of scheduler nodes, each owning a disjoint slice of the job table. A cluster with 10 million recurring jobs might split them across 50 scheduler shards, each responsible for roughly 200,000 jobs' worth of trigger checks.
- **Scale execution as a separate worker pool** that pulls ready-to-run tasks off a queue (the queue design from lesson 2 of this module). Workers don't need to know anything about cron expressions or schedules — they just process tasks, so you scale that pool purely on job volume and duration, independent of how many distinct schedules exist.

This separation also isolates failure: a burst of slow-running jobs saturating the worker pool doesn't stop new triggers from being evaluated on schedule, and a scheduler shard restarting doesn't stop workers from draining whatever's already queued.

## Leader election: one owner per shard, not per cluster

Sharding trigger evaluation solves the CPU-scaling problem, but introduces a correctness problem: for a given shard of jobs, exactly one node must be responsible for firing them at any moment. Two nodes both believing they own the same shard means a job can fire twice; zero nodes owning a shard means its jobs silently stop firing.

The standard fix is a **lease-based ownership** model backed by a strongly consistent store (etcd, ZooKeeper, or a database with compare-and-swap): a node acquires a time-bounded lease on a shard, renews it periodically via heartbeat, and only evaluates triggers for shards it currently holds a valid lease on. If a node crashes or partitions away, its lease expires and another node acquires it after a bounded timeout.

The subtle danger here is the **zombie leader**: a node that experiences a long GC pause or a slow network blip can believe it still holds a lease that has, in fact, already expired and been reassigned. If it resumes and fires a trigger without checking, you get a duplicate. The fix is a **fencing token** — a monotonically increasing number issued alongside each lease grant. Every write the "leader" makes (marking a job as fired, enqueuing a task) must include its fencing token, and the durable store rejects any write carrying a token older than the latest one it has seen. The old leader can keep believing it's in charge all it wants; its writes simply get rejected.

## Worker fault tolerance: heartbeats, retries, idempotency

Once a task is handed to a worker, the coordinator needs to know if that worker is still alive and making progress. It can't distinguish "still running a long job" from "hung" or "crashed" except by **heartbeat**: workers periodically report progress on their claimed task, and the coordinator treats a task as abandoned once its heartbeat deadline passes, regardless of whether the process actually died, deadlocked, or just lost network connectivity. The task then goes back on the queue for another worker to pick up.

That re-delivery means the system is fundamentally **at-least-once**, not exactly-once — a task can be picked up twice if a worker's heartbeat is delayed right as it finishes, or if a crash happens after the job completed but before the completion was recorded. Rather than chasing exactly-once delivery (which requires distributed transactions spanning your queue and every side effect the job has — rarely worth it), design job handlers to be **idempotent**: keyed by `job_id` plus the specific scheduled run's timestamp, so running the same logical execution twice is a safe no-op the second time (an upsert instead of an insert, a dedup check before sending an email, checking a "already processed" marker before charging a card).

Retries also need two more things to be production-safe: **exponential backoff with jitter**, so a downstream outage doesn't turn into a self-inflicted thundering-herd retry storm, and a **dead-letter path** after a retry budget (say, 5 attempts) is exhausted, so a job that fails deterministically — bad payload, revoked permissions — gets quarantined for manual inspection instead of endlessly consuming worker capacity.

## Recovering from a scheduler restart

A scheduler node that restarts (deploy, crash, OOM-kill) must not trust in-memory state about "what's already fired." On startup it re-derives its due list from durable storage: for its assigned shard, scan jobs whose next-fire time falls in a bounded catch-up window (e.g., the last 15 minutes), fire the ones genuinely missed, and skip ones already marked fired for that run. This is why the data model from lesson 2 — a durable, queryable record of each job's schedule and last-fired timestamp — matters: an in-memory timer wheel with no durable backing simply loses every job due during the outage.

## Worked example: sizing a scheduler tier for failure

Say you're running 2 million recurring jobs with a mean interval of 5 minutes, sharded across 50 scheduler nodes (40,000 jobs/shard). You configure a 10-second lease with a 3-second heartbeat and a 30-second acquisition timeout on failover. If a scheduler node dies, its shard's jobs pause for at most ~40 seconds (lease expiry plus acquisition) before another node picks up the shard and fires the catch-up window — an acceptable blip for 5-minute-interval jobs, but you'd tune the lease shorter for a shard holding 10-second-interval jobs, where a 40-second gap means eight missed runs instead of one. This is the concrete trade-off leader election forces: shorter leases mean faster failover but more heartbeat traffic and a higher chance of a healthy node losing its lease during a transient blip and triggering an unnecessary handoff.

<Callout type="warning">
  A common design mistake: building a scheduler that "detects" failed workers by having them log an error or call a `/report-failure` endpoint before dying. Crashes don't RSVP. If a worker is killed by an OOM reaper, loses power, or gets network-partitioned mid-task, it never gets to call anything. Failure detection has to be based on the *absence* of a heartbeat within a deadline, observed by the coordinator — never on the failing node self-reporting its own death.
</Callout>
