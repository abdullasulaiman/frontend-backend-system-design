A blob store's entire value proposition is "give us your data and we will not lose it, at a cost you can afford." That sounds like the same replication problem a key-value store solves, but blobs break the usual playbook in two ways: objects range from a 2KB thumbnail to a 50GB video file, so you can't route "the object" to one shard the way you'd route a small key-value pair, and the durability bar is much higher — losing someone's only copy of a video isn't a stale read you retry, it's gone forever. This lesson covers how a blob store partitions objects for placement, and how it picks between replication and erasure coding to hit a durability target like "eleven nines" without tripling every storage bill.

## Partitioning below the object, not at the object

A key-value store partitions by routing each *key* to a shard. A blob store can't do that cleanly for large objects — a 50GB video routed to a single node makes that node a bottleneck for every upload and download of that object, and a bounded amount of hardware failure destroys the whole thing at once.

Instead, blob stores split each object into fixed-size **chunks** (implementations vary, but tens of MB per chunk is typical) and place each chunk independently, usually by hashing a chunk identifier onto a set of storage nodes. This buys three things:

- **Parallelism** — uploading a large object writes many chunks to many nodes concurrently instead of streaming everything through one node's disk and network interface.
- **Bounded blast radius** — losing a node only affects the chunks it held, not entire objects; recovery work is chunk-sized, not object-sized.
- **Uniform placement logic** — a 2KB object is just "one chunk," so the same placement and repair machinery handles every object size without a special case.

The object's chunk list — which chunk IDs make it up, and in what order — lives in the metadata layer (covered in the previous lesson), not in the data path itself.

## Placement across failure domains

Where a chunk's copies (or shards — more on that below) land matters as much as how many there are. If three copies of a chunk all sit in the same rack, a single top-of-rack switch failure or a power event can take out all three at once — the replication count was real, but the independence it's supposed to buy wasn't.

Production placement policies are explicit about spreading copies across failure domains: one per rack at minimum, one per availability zone where the deployment spans multiple AZs, and increasingly one full replica set in a separate region for disaster recovery against a whole-region event. This is the same failure-domain-aware placement idea you'd apply to any replicated system, just applied to chunks instead of whole records.

## Replication vs. erasure coding

Once chunks are placed, the store needs redundancy so a single failed disk or node doesn't lose the chunk. There are two standard approaches, and most large blob stores use both, chosen per object class.

**Replication** stores `N` full copies of a chunk (3 is a common default, matching the reasoning behind key-value replication factors). It's simple: repairing a lost copy means copying one full chunk from a surviving replica. The cost is storage: 3 copies means 3x the raw bytes on disk for every byte of logical data.

**Erasure coding** splits a chunk into `k` data shards and adds `m` parity shards computed from them (Reed-Solomon codes are the common choice), such that *any* `k` of the `k + m` shards are enough to reconstruct the original chunk. A common configuration is `6 + 3`: tolerating the loss of any 3 shards — the same fault tolerance as 3x replication — at a storage overhead of only `9/6 = 1.5x` instead of `3x`.

The trade-offs erasure coding introduces:

- **CPU cost** — encoding on write and decoding on repair (or on read, if a shard is missing) takes real compute, unlike replication's plain byte copy.
- **Repair cost** — reconstructing one lost shard requires fetching `k` other shards across the network, not just one replica's worth of bytes. A single-shard loss under `6 + 3` pulls 6x the data of that shard to rebuild it.
- **Read latency on the degraded path** — if a shard needed for a read is missing, the read has to fetch enough other shards and decode, which is slower than reading a plain replica.

Because of that repair and latency cost, erasure coding tends to be applied to large, infrequently-accessed objects where the storage savings are big and the CPU/network cost of an occasional repair is easily absorbed. Small or frequently-read objects are often still replicated, since the fixed overhead of erasure coding per object and the repair-path latency aren't worth it at that size.

## Durability is a probability, and it depends on repair speed

"Eleven nines of durability" (`99.999999999%`) means the store expects to lose roughly one object out of 10^11 per year — not "we never lose data," but "the probability of permanent loss is vanishingly small." That number comes from multiplying failure probabilities, and it only holds if the copies/shards sit in independent failure domains, and the store repairs a lost copy fast enough that a *second* independent failure is unlikely before repair finishes.

Say a disk has a 1% annual failure rate (AFR) and failures are independent across 3 replicas in 3 different racks. The naive yearly loss probability for "all 3 fail simultaneously" looks like `0.01^3 = 0.000001%` — already six nines. But the real risk is losing a second and third replica *during the repair window* after the first fails, which can be hours, not a year. If a failed replica typically takes 2 hours to detect and fully re-replicate, the relevant probability window shrinks from "a year" to "a couple of hours" — what pushes achievable durability from six nines toward eleven. This is why fast failure detection and fast re-replication are durability features, not just operational hygiene — the same replication factor with slower repair has materially worse durability, even though the replica count on paper looks identical.

## Catching corruption before you need the data

Replica count and repair speed protect against a node disappearing outright. They don't protect against **bit rot** — a disk silently returning corrupted bytes for a read it reports as successful. Every chunk is stored with a checksum (a hash of its contents), and a background **scrubbing** process continuously reads chunks, recomputes the checksum, and compares. A mismatch triggers the same repair path as a lost replica: rebuild the chunk from a good copy or from `k` other shards, and replace the corrupted one. Without active scrubbing, a corrupted chunk can sit undetected for months — discovered only when a read actually needs it, at which point you may find every other copy already rotted too.

## Worked example: sizing storage for a 10 PB object store

Say a photo-and-video store holds 10 PB of logical data and needs the equivalent of 3-failure tolerance. Two options:

- **3x replication**: 10 PB x 3 = 30 PB of physical storage.
- **6+3 erasure coding**: 10 PB x 1.5 = 15 PB of physical storage.

At a rough $18/TB/month for durable object storage hardware and operations, that's roughly $540,000/month (replication) versus $270,000/month (erasure coding) — a $3.2M/year gap on the same logical 10 PB, for the same fault tolerance. That gap is why large blob stores invest in erasure coding despite the added complexity: at this scale, storage savings dwarf the extra CPU and repair-network cost, especially for the "write once, read rarely" objects that dominate blob store volume (backups, archived media, cold log data).

<Callout type="warning">
  A common mistake is treating "replication factor 3" as an automatic durability guarantee regardless of how it's deployed. Three copies on three disks in the same server give you almost none of the protection three copies spread across racks and AZs give you — a single power supply or switch failure can take all three at once. The same applies to repair speed: a system that detects a failed node in 6 hours and takes 2 more days to re-replicate has meaningfully worse durability than one with the same replication factor but a 10-minute detection and 1-hour repair window, because the "second failure during the repair window" risk is proportional to how long that window stays open. Durability comes from replica count *and* failure-domain independence *and* repair speed together — quoting just the replication factor in an interview is incomplete.
</Callout>
