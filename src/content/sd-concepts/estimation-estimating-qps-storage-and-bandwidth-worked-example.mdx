The previous lesson made the case for estimating at all — it tells you whether
you need a distributed system in the first place, and which resource (CPU,
storage, or bandwidth) is going to be your bottleneck. This lesson is the part
where you actually do the arithmetic. Interviewers care less about the final
number than about watching you get there: which assumptions you state out
loud, which units you're tracking, and whether you catch your own errors. This
lesson works through one system end to end — a photo-sharing service — so you
have a template you can adapt to whatever prompt you get.

## The four numbers you're almost always asked for

Nearly every back-of-the-envelope pass in a system design interview reduces to
four related numbers:

1. **QPS (queries per second)** — how many requests per second hit your
   system, split by read vs. write since they often need different scaling
   strategies.
2. **Storage** — how much data accumulates, usually expressed as
   total-over-some-retention-period plus a growth rate.
3. **Bandwidth** — how many bytes per second move across the network, again
   split by read vs. write (ingress vs. egress).
4. **Memory / cache size** — how much hot data you'd want in RAM to keep a
   given fraction of requests off disk (this one depends on the other three,
   so we treat it as a follow-on).

The trick is that these aren't four independent calculations — QPS multiplied
by payload size gives you bandwidth, and daily write QPS multiplied by record
size and retention gives you storage. Get QPS right and the rest mostly falls
out of it.

## Step 1: Turn user counts into QPS

Start from whatever traffic number the interviewer gives you or you agree to
assume — usually daily active users (DAU). Convert to actions per day, then
divide by the number of seconds in a day.

A day has 86,400 seconds. Round that to `10^5` for mental math — you're
rarely trying to be more precise than 15% in this exercise, and rounding
early keeps the arithmetic in your head instead of on a calculator.

Average QPS = (DAU x actions/user/day) / 86,400

That's your **average** load. Real traffic isn't flat — it peaks around
lunch and evenings and troughs overnight. Unless the prompt gives you an
actual traffic curve, a standard move is to assume peak load is **2 to 3
times** average. State that assumption explicitly rather than silently
baking it in — an interviewer may push back with "assume peak is 5x, this is
a live sports app," and you want to be able to recompute quickly.

## Step 2: Storage needs three inputs, not one

Storage estimates fail most often because candidates forget one of three
required inputs:

- **Write rate** — new records created per day (often just your write QPS x
  86,400).
- **Record size** — bytes per record, including any metadata you're storing
  alongside the payload.
- **Retention** — how long you keep the data (forever, 1 year, 30 days).

Total storage = writes/day x bytes/record x retention (days)

Then apply a **replication factor**. Almost no real system keeps a single
copy of data — 3x replication (common in systems like HDFS or a typical
Cassandra/DynamoDB deployment) is a reasonable default to state unless told
otherwise. Multiply your total by that factor at the end, not the beginning,
so you can clearly separate "logical data size" from "physical storage
footprint."

## Step 3: Bandwidth is QPS times payload size

Once you have read and write QPS, bandwidth is a single multiplication per
direction:

Bandwidth (bytes/sec) = QPS x average payload size (bytes)

Do this separately for reads and writes — a social feed might have 100x more
reads than writes, but each write (e.g. uploading a photo) might be 50x
larger than each read (fetching a thumbnail URL). Conflating the two hides
which direction actually dominates your network cost.

## Worked example: a photo-sharing service

Let's size a service where users upload and view photos. Assume the
interviewer gives you: 50 million DAU, each user uploads 1 photo every 10
days and views 20 photos/day. Average photo size: 500 KB. Metadata per
photo (owner, timestamp, caption, tags): roughly 1 KB. Retention: keep
photos indefinitely (this is the product).

**Write QPS.** Uploads/day = 50M / 10 = 5 million. Average write QPS = 5M /
86,400 ≈ 58. Round to ~60. Peak write QPS (x3) ≈ 180.

**Read QPS.** Views/day = 50M x 20 = 1 billion. Average read QPS = 1B /
86,400 ≈ 11,600. Round to ~12,000. Peak read QPS (x3) ≈ 36,000.

Already this tells you something: reads outnumber writes by roughly
**200:1**. That single ratio should steer your whole design toward
aggressive read caching (CDN for photo bytes, an in-memory or edge cache for
metadata) rather than optimizing the write path.

**Storage.** 5 million photos/day x 500 KB ≈ 2.5 TB/day of photo bytes, plus
5M x 1 KB = 5 GB/day of metadata (small enough to mostly ignore next to the
photo bytes). Over one year: 2.5 TB x 365 ≈ 912 TB/year, call it ~1 PB/year.
With 3x replication, physical storage grows by ~3 PB/year. That number alone
tells you "we need object storage (something like S3-class blob storage),
not a single database's disk" — which is exactly the kind of design
conclusion this estimate is supposed to produce.

**Bandwidth.** Write bandwidth ≈ 60 QPS x 500 KB ≈ 30 MB/s average, ~90 MB/s
at peak. Read bandwidth ≈ 12,000 QPS x 500 KB ≈ 6 GB/s average, ~18 GB/s at
peak — assuming every view fetches a full-size photo. In reality you'd serve
a compressed thumbnail (say 50 KB) for feed browsing and only the full 500
KB on open, which drops average read bandwidth by roughly 10x. Calling that
out shows the interviewer you're thinking about the design, not just
plugging numbers into a formula.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common estimation mistake isn't a bad formula — it's silently
  mixing time units. Candidates compute a "storage per day" number, then a
  few lines later multiply "QPS" (a per-second rate) by that same number as
  if it were also per-second. Before every multiplication, say the unit out
  loud: "so that's 2.5 terabytes *per day*, times 365 days, gives us the
  *annual* total." It costs you five seconds and catches an error that
  would otherwise produce a number off by four or five orders of magnitude
  — the kind of mistake that undermines an otherwise strong interview.
</Callout>

## Sanity-checking your numbers

Once you have a number, ask "does this feel right?" against something
familiar: a single beefy database node today comfortably handles tens of
thousands of simple reads/sec, so 12,000 average read QPS is very doable on
a handful of nodes with caching — it's not "Google-scale," and your design
shouldn't over-engineer for it. Conversely, 3 PB/year of blob storage is
firmly in "you need a managed object store, not a single filesystem"
territory. If your computed QPS or storage number feels absurd for the
product described (e.g. you calculate more storage than exists in most
enterprise data centers for a niche internal tool), you've likely made an
arithmetic or unit error — go back and check before you build a whole
architecture on top of a wrong number.
