The first two lessons in this module gave you the pieces separately: how to model publishers, subscribers, and topics, and how a broker fans a single event out to many independent consumers without one slow reader stalling the rest. An interviewer doesn't hand you those pieces one at a time — they hand you a business problem ("notify these five services when a trip ends") and expect you to reconstruct the whole design live, defending each choice as you make it.

This lesson is that rehearsal. Set a timer, talk out loud as if someone were watching, and only look at the rubric after you've committed to an answer.

## What this prompt is really testing

"Design a pub-sub system" is rarely the literal ask — it's almost always "design the messaging backbone for a specific fan-out scenario," and the specifics matter. A stock-ticker feed, an order-fulfillment pipeline, and a ride-hailing trip-completion event all use the same pub-sub primitives, but land on different partition keys, retention windows, and ordering guarantees. The interviewer is watching whether you:

1. Turn a vague fan-out requirement into concrete numbers (event rate, number of consumer groups, ordering scope, retention needs).
2. Choose a partition key and delivery guarantee that are consistent with each other and with the requirements — not two decisions made in isolation.
3. Handle the failure modes a real broker actually hits: a slow consumer, a crashed consumer, a duplicate delivery, a hot partition.

That third point is where most candidates run out of things to say. Anyone can draw "producer → broker → consumers." Fewer candidates can explain what happens when consumer group 3 falls two hours behind, or how a consumer tells a duplicate delivery from a genuinely new event.

## A structure for the 45 minutes

- **Minutes 0–8: requirements.** Roughly how many events per second, how many independent consumer groups need a full copy of the stream, whether any consumer cares about per-key ordering, and how long data needs to be replayable.
- **Minutes 8–18: topic and partition design.** Pick a partition key tied to the ordering requirement; decide topic count and partition count per topic.
- **Minutes 18–28: fan-out and consumption model.** Multiple consumer groups reading the same partitions independently, each with its own offset; push vs. pull delivery to those groups.
- **Minutes 28–36: delivery guarantees and idempotency.** At-least-once as the default, plus how each consumer group deduplicates.
- **Minutes 36–45: failure modes and a capacity check.** Slow consumer, broker/partition-leader loss, retry and dead-letter handling, plus one throughput or storage estimate.

## Questions a strong candidate asks first

A prompt like "notify billing, analytics, fraud detection, and the rider's app when a trip completes" hides several decisions inside it. Useful clarifiers:

- **Does any consumer need trips ordered relative to each other, or just events within one trip ordered?** If fraud detection only needs "trip started" before "trip completed" for the *same* trip, partitioning by trip ID is enough — you don't need a single global ordering.
- **Can any consumer tolerate losing an event entirely, or does every consumer need every event eventually?** Billing almost certainly can't lose a completed-trip event; a live map overlay might be fine dropping a stale one.
- **How far back does replay need to reach?** "Fraud detection needs to reprocess the last 7 days after a model change" is a retention and storage requirement, not just a delivery one.
- **What happens if one downstream consumer (say, analytics) falls hours behind — does that block anything else?** The answer should be "no," and getting the interviewer to confirm that up front justifies designing independent consumer offsets rather than one shared queue.

Nailing these down before drawing boxes is the highest-leverage five minutes of the interview — it also signals you know pub-sub design isn't one-size-fits-all.

## Where candidates lose points

The recurring failure pattern: a candidate draws "producer → topic → consumers," says "Kafka-like" once, and stops there — without ever explaining how ordering, deduplication, or a stuck consumer actually get handled. It sounds correct in outline and collapses the moment the interviewer asks "what if the fraud-detection consumer crashes mid-batch and comes back up — does it double-process anything?"

The fix is narrating the connective tissue between decisions, not adding more boxes: "I'm partitioning by trip ID, *because* fraud detection needs per-trip ordering but doesn't care about ordering across different trips — that lets me run many partitions in parallel instead of forcing one global sequence" is a stronger sentence than "I'll use partitions."

<Callout type="interview">
  When two reasonable options exist (e.g. push delivery to consumers vs. consumers pulling at their own pace), say the trade-off and commit: "I'll go with pull-based consumption — each consumer group advances its own offset at its own pace, so a slow fraud-detection job never causes the broker to push faster than analytics can handle. The cost is consumers polling instead of getting instant push, which is fine for this use case since sub-second delivery isn't a stated requirement." That's judgment, not just recall.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sizing the trip-completion topic

Suppose requirements surface this: 50,000 trips complete per hour at peak (~14 events/sec average, call it 50/sec at burst), each event is about 1 KB, four consumer groups (billing, analytics, fraud, rider notification) each need the full stream, and fraud detection needs 7 days of replay.

- **Partition count**: partitioning by trip ID with 24 partitions comfortably spreads 50 events/sec across parallel partitions (roughly 2/sec per partition at burst) while leaving headroom for 5–10x growth before repartitioning is needed.
- **Storage for replay**: 50,000 trips/hour × 24 hours × 7 days × 1 KB ≈ 8.4 million events/week × 1 KB ≈ 8 GB/week of raw event data — trivial to retain on disk for 7 days even with a 3x replication factor (about 25 GB total), so the interviewer's "keep 7 days for reprocessing" requirement is cheap to satisfy, not a reason to reach for cold storage.
- **Consumer independence check**: because each of the four consumer groups tracks its own offset into the same partitions, fraud detection replaying the last 7 days doesn't re-deliver anything to billing or analytics — it only rewinds its own group's cursor, which is exactly the isolation the requirements called for.
- **Delivery guarantee**: with billing in the consumer list, at-most-once (silently dropping a message on a hiccup) is unacceptable — the design commits to at-least-once delivery, and billing dedups by trip ID plus a monotonic version number stored in its own database, since a duplicate completed-trip event is far cheaper to handle than a lost one.

That's the pattern to produce live: pull a number from the requirements phase, turn it into a partition count or a storage figure, and let that number justify a design decision instead of asserting it.
