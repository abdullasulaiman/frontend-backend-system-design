The first four lessons in this module handed you the pieces one at a time: what a blob store needs to promise, how objects and metadata are modeled, how data gets spread across nodes durably, and how the whole thing gets assembled. In an actual interview, you get none of that scaffolding. You get one open-ended prompt — "design a storage service for user-uploaded photos" or "design something like S3" — and 45 minutes to build the whole thing out loud while someone evaluates how you think, not just what you know.

This lesson is that exercise. Set a timer, talk through a design before you look at the rubric, and treat the questions below as things a strong candidate raises unprompted.

## How this fits into the interview

"Design a blob store" is rarely the literal ask — it's usually "design the storage layer behind X," where X is a photo-sharing app, a video platform, a backup service, or a general-purpose object store like S3 or GCS. The interviewer wants to see you:

1. Turn "store large files" into concrete numbers — object sizes, upload/read ratio, mutability, and durability target.
2. Separate concerns cleanly: a metadata index, a data plane that stores bytes, and (usually) a CDN or cache in front of reads.
3. Make partitioning, replication, and consistency choices that reinforce each other instead of contradicting each other.
4. Volunteer the failure modes and trade-offs of your own design before being asked.

That fourth point is the real differentiator. Most candidates can draw "client -> API -> storage nodes." Fewer explain, unprompted, what happens when a multipart upload stalls halfway through, or why they picked replication over erasure coding for this specific workload.

## A structure for the 45 minutes

- **Minutes 0–8: requirements.** Object size range (KB thumbnails vs. multi-GB video), read/write ratio, mutability (are objects ever overwritten, or write-once?), durability and availability targets, and rough scale (uploads/day, total bytes stored).
- **Minutes 8–15: data model and API shape.** Bucket/key naming, metadata (owner, content-type, size, checksum, version) versus the raw bytes, and the put/get/delete/list surface a client actually needs.
- **Minutes 15–25: partitioning and chunking.** How keys map to storage nodes, how large objects get split into chunks or parts, and how multipart upload works end to end.
- **Minutes 25–35: replication, durability, and consistency.** Replication factor or erasure coding scheme, cross-node/cross-AZ placement, and what a client can observe immediately after a write.
- **Minutes 35–45: failure modes and a capacity check.** Node loss, stalled uploads, hot-object handling, and a back-of-envelope storage/throughput estimate.

## Questions a strong candidate asks first

A bare prompt like "design a blob store for user photo uploads" rewards candidates who narrow scope before drawing boxes:

- **What's the object size distribution?** A photo service (KBs to low MBs) can treat most uploads as single-shot puts; a video platform (GBs) forces multipart upload and chunked storage into the design from minute one.
- **Are objects ever mutated after upload, or strictly write-once-read-many?** Write-once simplifies concurrency control enormously — no need to reconcile concurrent overwrites of the same key.
- **What durability target, and against what failure?** "Survive a single disk failure" and "survive an entire region going dark" imply very different replica counts and placement strategies.
- **What's the read pattern — mostly recent uploads, or long-tail access to old objects?** This decides whether a CDN/cache in front of the store is load-bearing or a nice-to-have, and whether cold-storage tiering is worth discussing.

Asking these before proposing an architecture is the highest-leverage move available in the first five minutes — and it signals to the interviewer that you don't treat "just store the bytes somewhere" as the whole problem.

## Where candidates lose points

The failure pattern this lesson exists to prevent: a candidate draws a ring of storage nodes, says "we'll replicate three times," and never connects that number to a stated durability target, never distinguishes the metadata path from the data path, and never mentions what happens to an upload that fails halfway through. The design looks correct in isolation and falls apart the moment the interviewer asks "what if a 2 GB upload dies at 80%?" or "how do you know a `GET` right after a `PUT` returns the object you just wrote?"

The fix is narrating the connective tissue between decisions: "I'm splitting uploads over 100 MB into chunked parts *because* a single connection failure shouldn't force a full re-upload of a multi-gigabyte file — the client can resume from the last acknowledged part." That's a stronger sentence than just stating "we support multipart upload."

<Callout type="interview">
  When two reasonable options exist — replication vs. erasure coding, strong vs. eventual cross-region consistency — say the trade-off out loud and commit to one: "I'll replicate 3x within a region for simplicity and fast rebuild time; if this were a cold-archive tier where storage cost dominates, I'd switch to erasure coding at the cost of slower reconstruction." Ten seconds of narrated trade-off reads as judgment; silently picking one reads as guessing.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sizing the thing you just designed

Suppose requirements-gathering surfaced this: a photo-sharing app ingesting 5 million uploads/day, average object size 3 MB, with a 3x replication policy and a 5-year retention requirement.

- **Daily raw ingest**: 5,000,000 × 3 MB ≈ 15 TB/day of new object data before replication.
- **Stored footprint with replication**: 15 TB/day × 3 ≈ 45 TB/day actually written to disk across the cluster.
- **Retained footprint at steady state**: 45 TB/day × 365 × 5 ≈ 82 PB after five years — a number large enough to justify discussing tiering (hot storage for recent uploads, cheaper cold storage or erasure-coded tiers for objects untouched after, say, 90 days).
- **Write throughput**: 5,000,000 uploads/day ≈ 58 objects/sec average, but photo apps are bursty — planning for a 5–10x peak multiplier (roughly 300–600 objects/sec at peak) is a reasonable assumption to state explicitly rather than silently designing for the average.

That's the reasoning to produce live: take one number from requirements, turn it into a storage or throughput estimate, and let the estimate justify a design decision (here, that tiering becomes worth mentioning once the five-year number gets into petabytes).
