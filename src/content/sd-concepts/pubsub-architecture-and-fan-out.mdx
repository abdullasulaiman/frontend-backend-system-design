The previous lesson set up the pub-sub contract: publishers don't know who's listening, subscribers don't know who's publishing, and the broker sits in between decoupling the two in time and in number. That contract sounds simple until you ask the question an interviewer will actually push on: how does one published message end up delivered to a thousand subscribers, some of them slow, some of them offline for an hour, without the publisher waiting on any of them? The answer is the fan-out architecture — how topics are stored, how they're split for scale, and how delivery actually reaches each subscriber. Get this wrong in a design and you've built a system where a single slow consumer can stall every publisher, which defeats the entire point of pub-sub.

## The core pieces

A pub-sub broker is built from four things:

- **Topic** — a named, durable, append-only log of messages. Not a queue: nothing is removed when it's read.
- **Partition** — a topic is split into one or more ordered partitions, each an independent log with its own append order. Partitioning is the unit of parallelism.
- **Subscription** — a named registration of interest in a topic, tracking its own position (offset) into the log independently of every other subscription.
- **Consumer / consumer group** — the process(es) reading a subscription. A group can have multiple members that split the partitions between them for parallel consumption.

The key design idea: the topic is one shared, immutable log, and *fan-out happens by giving every subscription its own cursor into that same log* rather than copying the message once per subscriber at write time. A publish is a single append; a hundred subscribers each reading at their own pace cost nothing extra at write time — they're just a hundred independent offsets pointed at the same bytes.

## Partitioning: parallelism at the cost of global order

A single-partition topic gives you total order — every subscriber that reads from the start sees messages in exactly the order they were published. That's easy to reason about but caps your throughput at what one partition (one disk, one set of consumers) can handle.

Splitting a topic into partitions (commonly by hashing a key — `userId`, `deviceId`, `orderId`) lets you parallelize both the write path and the read path: each partition can live on a different broker node, and each partition can be consumed independently. The tradeoff is that you only get ordering *within* a partition, never across the whole topic. If a caller needs "all events for order 42 in order," hashing on `orderId` guarantees that, because every event for order 42 always lands in the same partition. If a caller needs "every event across all orders in global order," partitioning breaks that — you'd need a single partition, which caps scale.

## Consumer groups vs. broadcast subscriptions

Two different fan-out shapes get conflated constantly, and telling them apart is the actual skill:

- **Broadcast (topic → many independent subscriptions)**: every subscription gets every message. This is "pub-sub" in the purest sense — a `PriceChanged` event goes to the search-index updater, the cache invalidator, and the analytics pipeline, and each of those three sees every message, independently, at its own pace.
- **Consumer group (subscription → many competing members)**: within one subscription, the partitions are divided among group members so each message goes to exactly one member of that group, not all of them. This is how you scale a single logical consumer (say, "the cache invalidator") horizontally without duplicating work — five worker processes split twenty partitions, each handling four.

Put together: a topic commonly has several broadcast subscriptions (one per downstream system), and each of those subscriptions is itself consumed by a scaled-out consumer group.

## Push vs. pull delivery

Once a subscription exists, how does a message actually reach the consumer's code?

**Push**: the broker calls the subscriber — an HTTP webhook, a gRPC stream, an open socket. Latency is low (delivery happens the instant the broker has the message), but the broker now depends on the subscriber being reachable and fast. A subscriber that's down, slow, or rate-limiting the broker turns into the broker's problem: retries, dead-letter queues, and backoff policies all exist to manage this coupling.

**Pull**: the subscriber polls the broker for the next batch at its own cadence. This fully isolates the broker from subscriber health — a dead consumer just stops advancing its own offset, and the broker does nothing differently. The cost is polling latency (how often you poll trades off against staleness and request volume) and the consumer needs its own scheduling/retry logic instead of the broker providing it.

Most large-scale designs (Kafka, Kinesis) use pull for the core log because it keeps the broker simple and consumer-failure-agnostic, then let teams bolt push semantics on top (a small relay service that pulls and then pushes to webhooks) when low-latency delivery to external, less-trusted consumers is genuinely required.

## Worked example: fan-out-on-write vs. fan-out-on-read

Say you're designing the "new post" fan-out for a social feed, where publishing a post should show up in every follower's feed. A user with 500 followers posts roughly once a day; a celebrity account with 20 million followers also posts about once a day.

**Fan-out-on-write**: at publish time, write the post ID into every follower's personal feed store. For the 500-follower user, that's 500 writes — trivial. For the 20-million-follower celebrity, that single post now triggers 20 million writes, all before (or shortly after) the post can be considered "published." If a hundred celebrity-tier accounts post in the same minute, that's on the order of a couple billion feed writes competing for the same fan-out workers, which either fall dangerously behind or need enormous burst capacity that sits idle the rest of the day.

**Fan-out-on-read**: don't write anything at publish time beyond the one post itself; when a follower opens their feed, merge the posts of everyone they follow on the fly. Publishing a celebrity's post is now a single write no matter the follower count, but every feed *read* becomes more expensive, since it has to merge across every followed account at request time.

Neither one is right alone. The standard resolution is a hybrid: fan-out-on-write for ordinary accounts (the 500-follower case, where the write cost is cheap and reads stay fast), and fan-out-on-read for accounts past some follower threshold (merge celebrity posts into the feed at read time instead of pre-writing them everywhere). This is the concrete reason "how do you fan out?" questions in an interview almost always want you to name a follower-count threshold and defend it, not just pick one strategy globally.

<Callout type="warning">
  A frequent mistake is designing fan-out as a synchronous loop — "on publish, call every subscriber's endpoint and wait" — because it looks simple on a whiteboard. This makes publish latency depend on the slowest subscriber and turns one dead or throttled consumer into an outage for every publisher. Decouple fan-out from publish: persist the message durably first (the log append), acknowledge the publisher immediately, and let delivery to each subscription happen asynchronously off that durable log at its own pace. The publisher's job ends at "the broker has it," never at "everyone received it."
</Callout>

## Handling the slow-subscriber problem

Because partitions are the unit of both parallelism and ordering, one slow or stuck consumer inside a shared partition blocks every message queued behind it for that partition — the broker can't skip ahead without breaking the ordering guarantee that partition promises. Two practical mitigations: give known-slow or bursty consumers their own dedicated partition or subscription rather than sharing one with fast consumers, and set a per-message processing timeout with a dead-letter subscription so a single poison message doesn't permanently wedge a partition. Both are really the same idea — isolate blast radius so one subscriber's problem can't become every subscriber's problem.
