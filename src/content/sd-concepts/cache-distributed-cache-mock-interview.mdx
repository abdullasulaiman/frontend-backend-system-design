Every lesson so far in this module handed you one piece of the puzzle — where the cache sits, how it evicts, how it stays consistent, how it shards. In the real interview, nobody asks you those questions one at a time. You get a single open-ended prompt, a whiteboard, and 45 minutes, and you have to assemble all four pieces yourself, out loud, while someone watches how you think.

This lesson is that exercise. It's shorter on new theory and longer on how to run the conversation: what order to raise decisions in, which numbers to ask for, and where candidates typically lose the thread. Treat it like the real thing — set a timer, talk through your design before you look at the rubric.

## How this fits into the interview

A "design a distributed cache" prompt is really "design a distributed cache *for something*" — a caching layer in front of a product catalog, a session store, a leaderboard, a feed. The interviewer wants to watch you:

1. Turn a vague ask into concrete requirements (traffic shape, data size, staleness tolerance).
2. Make placement, eviction, consistency, and sharding decisions that are consistent with each other, not four disconnected answers.
3. Defend the weak points of your own design before being asked.

That third point is what separates a pass from a borderline result. Anyone can draw a ring of cache nodes. Fewer candidates volunteer "here's how a node failure degrades this system" without being prompted.

## A structure for the 45 minutes

Don't start drawing boxes in minute one. A rough time budget that works well for this specific prompt:

- **Minutes 0–8: requirements.** Read vs. write ratio, approximate QPS, average object size, how stale a value is allowed to be, and whether losing a cached entry is "annoying" or "wrong data shown to a user."
- **Minutes 8–15: placement and API shape.** Local process cache vs. shared cluster vs. both; cache-aside vs. read/write-through, tied to the read/write ratio you just got.
- **Minutes 15–25: sharding and replication.** How keys map to nodes, how you rebalance on scale-out, and how many replicas per shard given your durability bar.
- **Minutes 25–35: consistency and invalidation.** TTL-only vs. active invalidation, and what happens on a write under your chosen model.
- **Minutes 35–45: failure modes and scale check.** Hot keys, node loss, thundering herd, and a rough capacity number.

## Questions a strong candidate asks first

Vague prompts ("design a distributed cache for a social feed") reward candidates who narrow scope before designing. Useful clarifiers:

- **What's being cached — computed results, or copies of source-of-truth rows?** A computed feed ranking is fine to lose and rebuild; a session token being wrong is a security bug, not a performance blip.
- **What's the write pattern?** A catalog with 10,000 reads per write behaves completely differently from a counter that's written on every request.
- **Is slightly stale data acceptable, and for how long?** "A price can be up to 30 seconds old" is a very different system from "a price must be exact the instant it changes."
- **What does the client do on a cache miss or cache-node outage?** If there's no fallback path to the origin store, the cache isn't optional infrastructure — it's a single point of failure, and that reshapes the replication story.

Getting these answers before designing is the single highest-leverage thing you can do in the first five minutes — it's also, unprompted, evidence to the interviewer that you don't treat "cache" as an interchangeable black box.

## Where candidates lose points

The failure pattern this lesson exists to prevent: a candidate draws a clean ring of cache nodes, picks LRU because it's the eviction policy they remember best, and never connects the eviction choice, the consistency choice, and the sharding choice to each other or to the requirements gathered earlier. The design sounds textbook-correct in isolation and falls apart the moment the interviewer asks "what if this key gets 50x normal traffic" or "walk me through what the client sees if this shard's primary just crashed."

The fix isn't more knowledge — it's narrating the connective tissue: "I'm choosing write-through here *because* the read/write ratio you gave me is 200:1, so paying write latency to keep the cache accurate is cheap relative to how much read benefit we get" is a stronger sentence than just stating "I'll use write-through."

<Callout type="interview">
  When you're unsure which of two reasonable choices to make (e.g. TTL-only vs. active invalidation), say the trade-off out loud and pick one rather than silently defaulting or freezing up: "I'll go with short TTL plus best-effort invalidation on write — it's simpler to operate and the staleness window you described is tolerable. If it weren't, I'd add a pub/sub invalidation path at the cost of more moving parts." That sentence takes ten seconds and shows judgment, not just recall.
</Callout>

## Worked example: sizing the thing you just designed

Suppose the requirements phase surfaced this: a product catalog service serving 40,000 reads/sec and 200 writes/sec, average cached object size 2 KB, and a staleness tolerance of about 10 seconds.

- **Memory footprint**: if you cache the hottest 5 million SKUs (a reasonable working-set estimate for a large catalog), that's 5,000,000 × 2 KB ≈ 10 GB of live data. With headroom for metadata and fragmentation, budgeting 16–20 GB across the cluster is realistic — small enough that this could run comfortably on 3–4 modestly sized cache nodes rather than dozens.
- **Read throughput per node**: 40,000 reads/sec spread across 4 nodes (ignoring hot-key skew for a moment) is 10,000 reads/sec/node — well within what a single in-memory cache process handles, so the bottleneck here is availability and rebalancing, not raw throughput.
- **Replication choice**: with only 200 writes/sec, the cost of write-through plus 2 replicas per shard (for durability against a single node loss) is negligible relative to the 40,000 reads/sec being served — a good example of how the read/write ratio you asked for up front directly justifies a specific replica count instead of an arbitrary one.
- **Staleness budget**: a 10-second tolerance means TTL-based expiry alone might be sufficient without building an active invalidation pipeline — worth stating explicitly as "I'm choosing not to build push invalidation because the staleness bar doesn't require it," which shows you're avoiding unnecessary complexity rather than missing it.

That's the shape of reasoning to produce live: pull a number from the requirements, turn it into a memory or throughput estimate, and let the estimate justify the design choice.
