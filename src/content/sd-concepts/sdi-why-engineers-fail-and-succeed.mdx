Two candidates can walk into the same system design interview, both knowing roughly the same amount about caching, sharding, and load balancers, and come out with wildly different results. That's confusing the first time you see it — if the "knowledge" is equal, what decided the outcome? The answer is that system design interviews rarely fail on knowledge. They fail on *process*: how you handle ambiguity, how you allocate your 45 minutes, and how visibly you think out loud. This lesson names the specific, recurring failure patterns and their counterparts — the habits that predict a pass — so you know exactly what to practice, not just what to study.

## The failure patterns that show up again and again

If you watch enough system design interviews (as an interviewer or through mocks), the same handful of failure modes account for the vast majority of "no hire" outcomes. None of them require an exotic architecture to trigger.

### 1. Diving into components before requirements

The single most common failure: hearing "design a ride-sharing app" and immediately sketching a load balancer, an API gateway, and a database, without first asking what the system actually needs to do. Does it need to match drivers in real time? Support scheduled rides? Handle surge pricing? Each answer changes the design meaningfully. A candidate who skips this step is designing *a* system, not *the* system the interviewer asked for — and everything built on that shaky foundation looks less credible no matter how correct the individual pieces are.

### 2. Treating ambiguity as a problem instead of the point

Real prompts are deliberately underspecified ("design a notification system") because handling ambiguity is exactly what's being tested. Candidates who freeze or silently guess at scope are failing the actual test, even if their guess happens to be reasonable. The fix isn't cleverness — it's just asking: "Are we talking push notifications, email, both? What's the expected volume? Does delivery order matter?"

### 3. Going wide instead of deep

A design that lists twelve components in the first ten minutes and never returns to any of them in detail feels impressive at a glance but tells the interviewer nothing about how you actually reason. Interviewers consistently rate one deep, well-justified dive (e.g., "here's exactly how we detect and handle a duplicate payment") far above a wall of correctly-named boxes with no follow-through.

### 4. Skipping numbers entirely

"We'll use a cache" and "we'll use a cache sized for roughly 50M active users, ~2KB per session, so about 100GB, which fits comfortably in a sharded Redis cluster" are not the same answer, even though both mention a cache. Numbers force you to notice problems early — a design that "sounds fine" in prose can collapse the moment you estimate that a single database would need 200,000 writes/second it can't possibly sustain.

### 5. Going silent while thinking

Interviewers can't grade a thought they can't hear. A candidate who stops talking for two minutes to work something out in their head, then presents a polished answer, has given the interviewer nothing to evaluate in between — and often looks stuck even when they aren't. Thinking out loud, including false starts, is part of the deliverable, not an embarrassing tangent.

### 6. Treating pushback as failure

When an interviewer asks "what happens if that service goes down?", plenty of candidates hear "you got something wrong" and become defensive or start second-guessing their whole design. Almost always, the question means "I want to see you reason about this specific failure mode" — it's an invitation to go deeper, not a verdict.

## What success actually looks like

Flip each failure mode and you get the success pattern — but a few habits deserve their own emphasis because they're the ones candidates underrate most.

- **Narrating trade-offs unprompted.** The strongest signal in an interview is a candidate who says "I'm picking a message queue here instead of a direct call because I want the write to survive the downstream service being briefly unavailable — the cost is added latency and eventual, not immediate, delivery" without being asked. That single sentence demonstrates requirements-gathering, trade-off awareness, and technical depth simultaneously.
- **Managing time like a resource, not an afterthought.** A 45-minute interview spent entirely on requirements, or entirely on one component, both fail — not because the content was wrong, but because time is itself part of what's being evaluated. Strong candidates checkpoint themselves out loud: "Let's spend a couple more minutes on requirements, then move to high-level design so we have time for a deep dive."
- **Being willing to revise in front of the interviewer.** Committing to an architecture at minute 10 and refusing to adjust it when new information surfaces at minute 30 (e.g., "actually, writes need to be strongly consistent") reads as inflexibility. Redrawing part of the design and explaining why is a strength, not a confession of earlier error.
- **Closing the loop.** Ending with a quick "here's what we built, here's what I'd reconsider with more time, here's the biggest risk" gives the interviewer a clean summary to grade against — many strong technical designs lose points simply because time ran out mid-thought with no wrap-up.

## Worked example: same prompt, two outcomes

Both candidates get: "Design a system that lets users upload a photo and see it appear in their friends' feeds."

**Candidate A** starts sketching immediately: client, upload service, a database for photos, a feed service reading from it. Ten minutes in, the interviewer asks "how fresh does the feed need to be?" and the candidate hasn't considered it — there's no notion of fan-out, no discussion of push vs. pull feed generation, and the rest of the interview is spent retrofitting an answer to a question that should have shaped the design from the start.

**Candidate B** opens with two minutes of scoping: "Are we talking close friends only, or public followers too? Is near-real-time freshness required, or is a short delay acceptable? Roughly how many photo uploads and feed reads per second?" Told it's closer to Instagram-scale with a large follower/followee skew, Candidate B explicitly picks a hybrid fan-out approach — push for typical users, pull-on-read for celebrities with huge follower counts — and states the trade-off out loud: extra write amplification for most users in exchange for not overwhelming the write path when a celebrity posts. When asked "what if the fan-out service crashes mid-write?", Candidate B talks through idempotent retries rather than treating the question as a gotcha.

Same prompt, same rough knowledge of feeds and fan-out. Candidate A fails on process — no scoping, reactive instead of proactive trade-off discussion. Candidate B passes on process, not on knowing a fact Candidate A didn't.

<Callout type="warning">
  A subtle version of the "going wide" failure: candidates who over-correct after being told to "ask more questions" and spend eight or nine minutes clarifying every conceivable edge case before drawing a single box. Clarifying questions should converge quickly — three to five sharp questions that materially change the design, then move. Endless clarification is its own stall tactic and reads as avoidance of the harder work of actually designing something.
</Callout>
