If you just landed here, it's worth spending five minutes deciding *how*
you're going to move through this course before you start clicking into
lessons. Frontend system design is a broad enough topic that a
front-to-back read without a plan tends to produce a lot of passive
familiarity and not much interview-ready fluency. The structure below is
built to avoid that: two tracks that reinforce each other, plus two
built-in checkpoints (quizzes and mock interviews) that force you to
actually apply what you read instead of just recognizing it.

This matters more for frontend system design than it might for a topic
you can memorize. There's rarely a single "correct" architecture for a
prompt like "design a chat application" — what an interviewer is actually
evaluating is whether you can reason through trade-offs out loud, in
real time, under a bit of pressure. Reading twenty lessons about caching
strategies doesn't build that skill by itself; producing your own answer
to a messy prompt and comparing it against a rubric does. The two tracks
and the practice loop below are designed around that gap specifically.

## The two tracks

### Concepts track

The Concepts track is the fundamentals layer. It's organized into modules
covering nonfunctional requirements, design patterns and architectures,
performance and optimization, security, and a dedicated design framework
you can carry into any interview prompt. Each lesson here teaches one
idea precisely — rendering strategies, state management trade-offs, caching,
API shape, and so on — independent of any specific product.

Think of this track as building your vocabulary and your mental toolbox.
When a case study later asks "how should this client stay in sync with a
server during a flaky connection," you want the answer to already live in
your head from a concept lesson, not be something you're inventing live.

The Concepts track modules build on each other roughly in this order:
Introduction (what the discipline covers and how to use the course),
Nonfunctional Requirements (performance, accessibility, and reliability as
things you scope explicitly rather than assume), Design Patterns &
Architectures (state management, component boundaries, data-fetching
patterns), Performance & Optimization (rendering cost, bundle size,
caching), Security (the client-side risks specific to frontend work), and
finally the Design Framework module, which gives you a repeatable
structure for approaching any open-ended design prompt from a blank page.
That last module is deliberately placed near the end — it's most useful
once you already have the vocabulary from the earlier modules to plug
into it.

### Interview Prep track

The Interview Prep track is five end-to-end case studies: a newsfeed, a
video streaming platform, a chat application, a ride-hailing app, and a
calendar application. Each case study has four lessons that walk through
requirements, architecture, and a deep-dive concern specific to that
product (say, ordering and de-duplication for chat, or adaptive bitrate
for video) — and every case study closes with a mock interview lesson
built around the `MockInterview` component.

This track is where the concepts get stress-tested against a realistic,
messy prompt instead of a clean abstract question.

## Recommended order

Don't finish the entire Concepts track before touching a single case
study, and don't jump straight into case studies with no foundation
either. Both extremes waste time. A better pattern:

1. Read the Introduction module (you're in it now) and the
   Nonfunctional Requirements module first — they set up vocabulary every
   later lesson assumes.
2. Pick one case study and work through its four lessons, pausing to read
   the matching concept lesson whenever the case study references an idea
   you haven't covered yet (the case study lessons will point you to the
   relevant concept by name).
3. After finishing that case study's mock interview, go back and finish
   the concept module it leaned on most heavily — by then you have a
   concrete example to hang the abstract material on.
4. Repeat for the remaining four case studies, working through the rest
   of the Concepts track (Design Patterns & Architectures, Performance &
   Optimization, Security, Design Framework) alongside them.

If you only have a few days before an actual interview, invert the
priority: do the Design Framework lesson first, then run through as many
mock interviews as you can, using the concept lessons as lookups when a
rubric point stumps you rather than reading them cover to cover.

## How the quizzes and mock interviews reinforce learning

A `<Quiz>` sits at the end of every concept lesson. It's short on purpose
— 2 to 4 questions — because the goal isn't to test trivia, it's to catch
the specific misunderstanding you're most likely to walk away with (mixing
up two similar techniques, missing a trade-off's downside). Take it
immediately after reading the lesson body. If you get a question wrong,
re-read the section it maps to before moving on rather than just noting
the correct answer and continuing.

The `<MockInterview>` component works differently. It gives you an
open-ended prompt and a rubric of 4 to 6 points a strong answer would hit,
but the rubric stays hidden until you choose to reveal it. The value is
almost entirely in the struggle before the reveal: set a timer (30-45
minutes is realistic for these prompts), talk through your design out
loud or write it down as if a whiteboard were involved, and only then
compare against the rubric and self-score. Revealing the rubric first and
reading it as a study guide teaches you what a good answer contains, but
not the harder skill of producing one under the mild pressure of a timer
and a blank page.

## Using the cheatsheet bullets for revision

Every lesson's frontmatter carries a `cheatsheet` — 3 to 7 short, standalone
bullets that summarize the actual takeaway of that lesson, not just its
title. These aren't meant to replace reading the lesson; they're meant for
the week before an interview, when you don't have time to reread thirty
lessons but do have time to skim thirty cheatsheets.

A practical spaced-revision approach: after finishing a module, copy its
lessons' cheatsheet bullets into your own notes. A few days later, skim
just those bullets without opening the lessons — if a bullet doesn't fully
make sense anymore, that's a signal to reopen that specific lesson rather
than the whole module. Repeat that skim again the night before an actual
interview.

## Worked example: prepping for a chat-application interview

Say you have two weeks and know your upcoming interview will likely be a
messaging or collaboration-tool prompt. A reasonable plan:

- Days 1-2: Introduction module, Nonfunctional Requirements module.
- Days 3-5: Case Study: Chat Application — all four lessons, including its
  mock interview, done honestly (timed, notes-first).
- Days 6-7: The Design Patterns & Architectures and Performance &
  Optimization modules, since real-time UI updates and connection
  management lean on both heavily.
- Days 8-9: Re-run the chat application's mock interview from scratch,
  without looking at your first attempt's notes, and compare.
- Days 10-13: One more case study end to end (ride-hailing or newsfeed
  are good picks — they cover different concerns like geolocation and
  infinite-scroll data fetching) to make sure your framework generalizes
  beyond chat specifically.
- Day 14: Skim every module's cheatsheet bullets in one pass.

The second mock-interview attempt is doing the real work here — it's the
difference between having read about a topic and being able to produce a
structured answer to it under time pressure.

<Callout type="warning">
  The most common way people waste this course is reading every lesson
  once, top to bottom, and never opening a `Quiz` or `MockInterview`
  component at all. Passive reading feels like progress because the
  material makes sense while you're looking at it, but recognition isn't
  recall — the failure mode shows up in an actual interview when you need
  to produce the structure from a blank page, not select it from options
  on a screen. Budget real time for the interactive pieces, not just the
  prose.
</Callout>
